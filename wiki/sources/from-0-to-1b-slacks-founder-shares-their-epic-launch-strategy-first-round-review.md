## Summary
[[FirstRoundReview]] interviews [[StewartButterfield]] about [[Slack]]'s early launch, arguing that staged beta cohorts, category education, bottom-up team adoption, systematic customer feedback, product-specific activation metrics, and focused differentiation reinforced one another. Slack moved from friendly companies to progressively larger teams, used a press-driven preview to build demand, and then spent six months improving the new-user experience before public launch. The account is a favorable 2015 founder retrospective whose adoption and retention figures are historical, self-reported, and not presented with cohort definitions or independent validation.

## Key Claims
- Slack expanded testing through progressively larger teams because team size and structure exposed product failures that internal use could not reveal.
- A launch press blitz generated 8,000 invitation requests on the first day and 15,000 within two weeks, while repeated network sharing was treated as more important than publication alone.
- Selling a new team-collaboration category required teaching customers that fragmented email, chat, SMS, and social tools already formed a poorly served workflow.
- Low-cost team-level adoption let mid-level managers try and expense Slack without first winning company-wide executive or procurement approval.
- Slack treated support as product research and marketing by making messages replyable, answering tickets and tweets, tagging qualitative feedback, routing it into searchable internal channels, and joining quality assurance with support.
- Slack used 2,000 team messages as a product-specific activation threshold; Butterfield reported that 93% of teams crossing it remained active, despite more than 90% of created teams never inviting anyone or beginning meaningful use.
- The product concentrated quality effort on search, cross-device leave-state synchronization, and simple file sharing rather than treating feature breadth as the primary differentiator.

## Key Quotes
> "We amplified the feedback we got at each stage by adding more teams." - Butterfield on progressively larger beta cohorts.

> "Every customer interaction is a marketing opportunity." - the article's link between responsive support and recommendation.

## Contradictions
- The article's claim that Slack grew without major integrated marketing campaigns is compatible with, but should not be confused with, an absence of marketing: the same account treats months of launch preparation, a substantial press blitz, network amplification, and continuous Twitter engagement as central growth work.
- The reported 2,000-message threshold is observational. The source gives no cohort window, denominator, segment breakdown, or evidence that pushing teams toward the threshold caused retention rather than identifying teams already likely to stay.
- The claim that every team member effectively held a veto clarifies Slack's adoption risk but is a founder generalization rather than measured evidence across team sizes or buying processes.
- The article is a favorable historical interview. Its user, team, ticket, tweet, staffing, activation, and retention figures are self-reported snapshots and do not establish current Slack practice or general applicability.
