## Summary
[[SusanSu]] synthesizes interview practices attributed to [[ShaunClowes]], [[ElenaVerna]], [[NickSoman]], [[AndrewChen]], and [[BrianBalfour]], who collectively had screened or interviewed more than 1,000 growth candidates. The article treats growth hiring as a test of open-ended problem solving, experimentation, causal depth, resilience, and real-time system understanding rather than vocabulary alone. It also makes the interview reciprocal: candidates should investigate the growth function's trajectory, metrics, culture, autonomy, resources, near-term expectations, and remaining room for impact.

## Key Claims
- Unusual estimation or ecosystem prompts can expose how a candidate chooses variables, reasons under uncertainty, and creates a model without a memorized playbook.
- Live backlog generation tests breadth under time pressure, while later ranking or critique tests whether divergent ideation is followed by objective judgment.
- Questions about setbacks and exceptional strengths reveal resilience and potential upside that a weakness-only screen can miss.
- Repeatedly asking “why,” separating individual contribution from team results, and whiteboarding an observed product's growth system can distinguish causal understanding from rehearsed stories or jargon.
- A candidate should clarify whether “growth” covers acquisition alone or the wider funnel and how that remit is expected to evolve with the business.
- Retention, lifetime value, drop-offs, execution pathways, team autonomy, dedicated engineering and design, and a six-month roadmap expose whether the role has measurable goals and the ability to act.
- Asking how often obvious surfaces have already been optimized helps distinguish a neglected system with accessible gains from a mature program where marginal impact will be harder.

## Key Quotes
> "there's no right answer" - Elena Verna on using the Golden Gate Bridge estimate to observe a candidate's reasoning process.

> "Why did you do that?" - Shaun Clowes's compact prompt for moving beyond a rehearsed account of past work.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction found. The article strengthens reciprocal, work-relevant interviewing but gives no scoring rubric, interviewer-calibration method, candidate-outcome data, accessibility analysis, adverse-impact analysis, or evidence that its unusual prompts predict job performance.
- Brain teasers can become arbitrary or coachable unless interviewers define the growth-relevant behavior being observed and score the reasoning process consistently. Pressure for a sixth or seventh idea may measure comfort with rapid verbal improvisation as much as experiment quality.
