## Summary
[[WhileWest]] argues that startup employee equity is often misread as a predictable route to wealth when it is closer to a high-variance "mystery box." Using [[Slack]], [[TinySpeck]], [[Fab]], [[Secret]], [[Square]], [[Facebook]], and [[Google]] as examples, the article says employees should treat equity as risky, illiquid, and structurally different from founder equity. Its practical advice is to favor cash when company prospects are doubtful, while acknowledging that equity can still align employees with company goals.

## Key Claims
- Hindsight makes successful startup equity look obvious, but employees usually choose under deep uncertainty, as shown by the [[TinySpeck]] to [[Slack]] pivot.
- Startup employee equity is mainly a salary-negotiation device until it becomes liquid, because employees generally cannot spend, trade, or reliably convert it.
- Vesting cliffs and liquidation preferences mean equity described as "awarded" may not become economically owned or paid out by employees.
- Founders have a structurally different relationship to equity because they can allocate it, trade it for investment, and sometimes take secondary liquidity.
- Employees who want lower risk should negotiate for more cash when they doubt the company's future.

## Key Quotes
> "Anything of value can be traded for or converted into something of equal value." - context for the article's liquidity critique.

> "The probability of getting rich from employee equity is almost zero" - bottom-line warning about employee equity upside.
