## Summary
[[KenNorton]] argues that organizations pursuing order-of-magnitude impact must question the assumptions behind their existing products, tolerate informative failure, use evidence to test disputed possibilities, and measure desired outcomes rather than activity. [[Kodak]] anchors the warning: inventing an early digital camera did not overcome management's incentive to protect profitable film. The essay presents [[TenXThinking]] as a strategic complement to repeated experimentation, while its selected anecdotes and 2015 forecasts do not show that extreme goals generally outperform disciplined [[Incrementalism]].

## Key Claims
- Profitable incumbents can rationally defend current revenue while missing a successor technology, combining [[LossAversion]], hierarchy, and the [[InnovatorsDilemma]].
- A 1% chance of $1 billion has an expected value of $10 million, roughly ten times a 99% chance of $1 million, but expected value alone does not capture ruin, variance, timing, or learning.
- A 10% target tends to preserve familiar tools and assumptions, whereas a 10x target can force teams to redefine the problem and search for a different mechanism.
- Failure tolerance becomes useful when teams run repeated attempts, learn from evidence, and can speak openly; failure without bounded exposure or learning is not a strategy.
- Mobility, employee voice, and broad information sharing can widen the number of people and combinations from which a breakthrough might emerge.
- Teams should test assumptions with data and define success through desired impact rather than task counts or predetermined effort.
- Long-run demographic, cost, adoption, and technology trends can make an initially extravagant product promise economical by the time it reaches scale.

## Key Quotes
> "It's often easier to make something 10x better than it is to make it 10% better." - attributed to Astro Teller.

## Connections
- [[KenNorton]] - author adapting a 2015 Mind the Product keynote into this essay.
- [[Kodak]] - central incumbent case used to connect digital invention, film economics, and strategic failure.
- [[TenXThinking]] - the essay's order-of-magnitude problem-reframing method.
- [[LossAversion]] - behavioral mechanism offered for protecting profitable existing products and preferring high-probability modest gains.
- [[InnovatorsDilemma]] - incumbent allocation conflict between current economics and an uncertain successor.
- [[CorporateRiskAversion]] - organizational counterpart to individual downside sensitivity and hierarchical approval.
- [[Incrementalism]] - complementary learning mechanism and explicit scope tension with the essay's critique of 10% thinking.
- [[ProlificPractice]] - the ceramics-class anecdote argues that repeated production can improve quality through learning.
- [[DataDrivenOperations]] - neighboring practice of replacing opinion contests with measured tests and outcome diagnosis.

## Contradictions
- The essay's categorical contrast between 10x and 10% creates a scope tension with [[Incrementalism]], which shows that large outcomes can emerge from accumulated small changes. The positions are compatible when 10x describes the outcome or problem frame and incremental work supplies experiments, learning, and execution.
- The expected-value example favors the low-probability project only under repeated or survivable risk. A single organization facing capital, time, or existential constraints may rationally reject the higher arithmetic expectation.
- Kodak's decline is presented as a compact management caution rather than a comparative history; the essay does not isolate digital-camera suppression from later execution, competition, capital, market timing, or smartphone substitution.
- The article's Amazon, DHL, Gmail, aging, and ceramics examples are illustrative anecdotes without comparative outcome data, and several cost and adoption figures are historically bounded to 2015.
