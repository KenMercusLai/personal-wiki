## Summary
[[GuyShachar]] argues that startup demand, growth, and fundraising do not establish a viable business when the value generated by a customer fails to cover the cost of acquiring and serving that customer. He applies this warning to 2016-era on-demand services, arguing that smartphone convenience, venture funding, and transaction volume could conceal per-order losses, premium pricing, and labor-regulation exposure. The essay closes by treating commercial viability as a necessary part of opportunity selection rather than assuming that every real customer pain supports a sustainable company.

## Key Claims
- [[UnitEconomics]] should test whether expected customer lifetime value exceeds customer acquisition cost rather than treating growth as sufficient evidence of a business.
- Founder optimism can turn untested assumptions about indefinite retention and organic virality into inflated LTV and understated CAC expectations.
- On-demand services can create genuine smartphone-enabled convenience while still depending on higher prices, added fees, investor subsidy, or loss-making transactions.
- Transaction volume does not by itself repair negative per-order economics; scaling a structurally loss-making transaction can increase total losses.
- Gig-worker classification and changing labor rules can alter the cost structure on which an on-demand service depends.
- A painful or inefficient customer problem is only half of a startup opportunity; the other half is a viable way to solve it.

## Contradictions
- Qualifies growth-positive startup and marketplace narratives by separating convenience, demand, fundraising, and volume from sustainable transaction economics.
- The essay's broad skepticism toward on-demand companies is not backed by company-level CAC, LTV, retention, contribution-margin, or cohort data; [[SubsidizedUnitEconomics]] contains a later, more quantitative Uber-specific critique.
