## Summary
Ben Gilbert uses interviews with former Disney game executives and developers to explain why [[Disney]] abandoned internally published console and PC games for licensing. The account connects [[CorporateRiskAversion]], limited game-development knowledge, shifting studio mandates, layered approvals, and competition for corporate capital with repeated cancellations and studio closures. [[DisneyInfinity]] briefly made the division profitable but was ended when high development costs and declining toys-to-life growth made its future unattractive.

## Key Claims
- [[Disney]] preferred licensing major games because it transferred development and publishing risk while preserving access to the company's intellectual property.
- [[AlexSeropian]] says uncertain game returns pushed Disney toward spreadsheet-led decisions, while insufficient institutional knowledge made it harder to judge products and sustain commitments.
- Wideload Games reportedly cycled from innovation to family games, free-to-play, and mobile work in four years, with projects repeatedly pitched, prototyped, and killed.
- Layered Disney and Marvel approvals slowed one mobile project even though that format depended on small, fast development cycles.
- [[GamePublishingPortfolioEconomics]] requires several expensive, long-horizon bets because many games fail and one success may need to cover the portfolio.
- Disney reportedly bought and later closed at least six studios; [[DisneyInfinity]] and studio closures eventually helped the games business become profitable after years of losses.
- Disney ended Disney Infinity despite its billion-dollar franchise milestone because management expected toys-to-life sales to decline and judged the development model too costly.

## Key Quotes
> "We feel like we're better off managing the risk" - Bob Iger on licensing rather than publishing.

> "You have to have enough scale to do four or five of these bets" - Alex Seropian on portfolio economics.

## Contradictions
- The source does not directly contradict an existing page, but it qualifies acquisition-as-capability arguments in [[AcquisitionStrategy]]: buying studios does not create durable institutional knowledge when the acquirer repeatedly redirects work, cancels projects, or closes the teams.
- Disney Infinity's reported billion-dollar franchise milestone coexisted with a forecast of declining category sales and high development costs; cumulative revenue therefore did not establish attractive future profitability.
