## Summary
A HackerNoon writer traces a video he found among submissions to Snapzu, the community platform his team built, to a YouTube channel that publishes fully automated news videos: an 80s-style intro, a robotic text-to-speech voice-over, and floating low-resolution images, assembled from articles and photographs taken without credit from BBC, ABC, Reuters, and other outlets. He describes a script running around the clock that strips recent articles into video and posts them every few minutes across many similar channels, with ads that Google displays and shares revenue on. The source's numbers are estimates rather than measurements: roughly $1 to $20 per 1,000 views with about $7 cited as an average, about $3,000 of channel revenue over roughly two months, and more than 225,000 views in one month for a single channel at about 8,000 views per day. He argues that Google does almost nothing about it, that video results get preferred treatment in search because they make results look more diverse, and that smarter and faster AI will make automated plagiarism of this kind much worse. ## Key Claims
- Fully automated YouTube channels convert recent news articles into videos with machine voice-over and stock or recycled imagery, removing almost every human step between source article and upload.
- The underlying material is taken from named news organizations such as the BBC, ABC, and Reuters without payment, license, or even a mention, so the channel collects the audience and the ad money for work done by others.
- The pipeline is continuous and portfolio-wide: videos are generated every few minutes, one channel carries hundreds of them, and further channels follow the same concept.
- The economics work because view volume compounds across many cheap videos: the source estimates ads at roughly $1-$20 per 1,000 views, around $7 on average, about $3,000 earned by the featured channel in roughly two months, and 225,000-plus views in the past month at about 8,000 views per day, with one video reaching almost 50,000 views in two days.
- Enforcement is presented as absent rather than merely incomplete: Google does almost nothing to stop it, and the author frames the situation as a new kind of fully automated plagiarism that nobody is addressing.
- The platform's own presentation incentives help the abusive format, because video results receive preferred treatment in Google's mixed search results and make the page look more diverse with video, images, and other non-link content.
- The author treats this as theft rather than a clever growth hack, while accepting that growth hacks in general can be admirable, and warns that AI progress will make the automated version faster, smarter, and more pervasive.

## Key Quotes
> "A new type of fully automated plagiarism has been creeping up and no one is doing anything about it." - the article's central accusation.

> "Typically I admire many clever growth 'hacks' to build an audience. But this is just plain theft." - the line separating the author's tolerance for growth hacking from his judgment on this practice.

> "when countless news publications and blogs are barely scraping by, they now also have this growing obstacle deal with." - the framing of stolen-content video as an additional burden on struggling publishers.

> "just wait a few more years before it gets out of hand as AI inevitably becomes smarter, faster, and more efficient." - the forecast that automation will escalate the problem.

> "Google is doing almost nothing to stop it." - the article's summary of the enforcement response.

## Contradictions
- The article's revenue figures are extrapolations from a CPM range that the article itself calls debatable, so the roughly $3,000 estimate should be read as an illustration of an incentive, not as measured channel income.
- Its claim that video receives preferred treatment in Google search conflicts in emphasis with the wiki's [[PlatformPublisherRevenue]] and [[WebAdEconomics]] material, which treats platforms as needing professional content and frames fair payment as a shared interest; this source instead describes a platform profiting from the theft of that content.
- The article reports one moment in a moving enforcement story and provides no follow-up on the named channels, so it cannot show whether the described inaction persisted after its publication.
- The source treats the video format as the abusive element, but the same automation described here can be and later was used for original synthetic content, which the wiki's AI-era material treats as a separate editorial question.
