## Summary
[[ErikDietrich]] argues that programming is sometimes art but usually serves functional ends better described as algorithmic delegation or optimization. Using aesthetic purpose as his working test, he treats entertainment-oriented game programming as a clear artistic case while presenting ordinary business software as primarily instrumental; the preserved discussion broadens the frame toward self-expression, craft, engineering, correctness, and context-dependent rigor.

## Key Claims
- An open-ended solution space does not by itself make an activity art; purpose matters alongside the creativity used to reach it.
- Programming can be art when aesthetic experience is the product, as in entertainment-oriented video games, but most business software is purchased to automate work or increase efficiency.
- Software development is neither uniformly art nor uniformly science: it can resemble art, science, engineering, craft, or routine data entry in different contexts.
- Dietrich's most general account is algorithmic delegation: programmers identify work a computer can perform and construct that delegation, including when the delegated outcome is entertainment.
- The audience for source code includes future maintainers, but readability and aesthetic elegance can support a functional end without making the software itself art.
- The comment thread challenges an aesthetics-only definition by proposing self-expression, metaphor, beauty, and craft, while also emphasizing correctness, standards, team constraints, and the greater rigor demanded by safety-critical systems.

## Key Quotes
> "Is programming art? Sometimes, but usually not." - Dietrich's concise answer.

> "At its core, software development is optimization" - his account of programming as constructing algorithmic delegation.

## Contradictions
- Commenters dispute the article's narrow aesthetic-purpose test, arguing that functional artifacts can still contain art through self-expression, metaphor, elegance, or the creative quality of their implementation.
- The claim that video games are bought for aesthetics rather than learning or economic benefit is a simplifying generalization that does not cover educational games, professional simulation, competitive livelihoods, or mixed motives.
- “Optimization” is used conceptually rather than as a measured or formally defined property, and “algorithmic delegation” fits the essay's examples more precisely than mathematical optimization.
- The article and comments are practitioner arguments, not empirical evidence that one professional metaphor best predicts software quality, work practice, or audience judgment.
