## Summary
NPR reports on research by [[ElizabethDunn]] and colleagues suggesting that spending money on time-saving services can improve happiness more reliably than spending the same money on material goods. The article connects an experiment with 60 Vancouver adults to a broader 6,000-person survey across the U.S., Canada, and Europe, while [[EmanuelMaidenberg]] qualifies the finding by asking whether the emotional boost lasts beyond the immediate purchase period. The inspected lead illustration shows one person relaxing while another mows the lawn, reinforcing the article's contrast between leisure and outsourced chores without adding a separate factual claim.

## Key Claims
- [[FounderTimeLeverage]] generalizes beyond startup founders: paying for chores, errands, lawn care, cleaning, meal delivery, or grocery delivery can buy back scarce free time.
- Participants in a weekend experiment reported more positive emotion after spending $40 on time-saving services than after spending $40 on material goods.
- In a larger survey, people who spent money to buy time reported nearly one rung higher life satisfaction on a 10-point scale than people who did not.
- The happiness effect was reported across income levels, including lower-income working adults and wealthy respondents.
- People may resist buying time because paying someone else to perform doable household tasks can produce guilt.
- The clinical qualification is that the evidence is intriguing but leaves open whether the benefit is durable or only an immediate mood response.

## Key Quotes
> "Money can't buy happiness, right?" - opening frame for the spending-choice question.

> "People from across the income spectrum benefited" - Dunn's qualification that the effect was not limited to wealthy people.
