## Summary
This practitioner essay uses [[GregWilson]]'s critique of unsupported claims about domain-specific languages to argue that influential software-methodology claims should be labeled unproven when they lack citations or data. It extends that criticism to [[AgileSoftwareDevelopment]], consultant incentives, and practitioner authority, while proposing funded empirical research and closer attention to computing's mathematical foundations as better guides for [[SoftwareEngineering]]. Its strongest contribution is the epistemic distinction between false and not yet demonstrated; its broader claims about Agile advocates' motives, competence, and influence are opinion rather than evidence supplied by the article.

## Key Claims
- Popularity, professional authority, and anecdote are not adequate substitutes for evidence when software leaders make causal claims about productivity, communication, or return on investment.
- [[GregWilson]]'s “not proven” framing separates a claim's lack of support from proof that the claim is false.
- The author argues that [[AgileSoftwareDevelopment]] advocates should promote and fund studies of methodology outcomes rather than rely on consulting authority and practitioner stories.
- The essay alleges that commercial speaking and consulting incentives can discourage public self-criticism, but it presents no financial or behavioral evidence establishing that mechanism.
- Computing practice rests partly on mathematical and research traditions, and the author expects research to keep transferring into industry.
- Software professionals should state uncertainty and evidential limits explicitly when research does not justify a confident conclusion.

## Key Quotes
> “it means that we don't know” - Wilson's distinction between an unsupported claim and a disproved one.

> “We need better science” - the author's proposed corrective for software-methodology claims.

## Contradictions
- Qualifies [[blog-martin-fowler-foreword-to-the-art-of-agile-development]]: Fowler presents Agile as a connected capability system, while this essay argues that influential Agile claims still require comparative evidence and should not be accepted on practitioner authority alone.
- The source does not demonstrate that Agile methods are ineffective. Its central defensible conclusion is narrower: the examples it discusses do not establish the claimed outcomes.
