## Summary

This source describes a lightweight founder-team alignment ritual called "wine evening" or "How do you feel" dinners. The author frames it as a response to cofounders drifting out of sync: unspoken fears about power, skill, product direction, and commitment can harden into grudges before they are visible as operational disagreement.

At TAT, the six founders held casual quarterly dinners with one or two bottles of wine. The agenda had three questions: personal life, work, and what should be done differently. One person spoke at a time, others could only ask clarifying questions, and the group avoided designating a chair or secretary so status stayed equal.

The key ordering is personal before work. The author argues that envy about VC meetings, R&D focus, travel, or quality ownership often appears as a work dispute but reflects family constraints, desire for interesting experience, fear of running out of money, or feeling left out. Starting with feelings creates shared ground where proposed fixes can be evaluated against underlying needs instead of becoming arguments over titles, responsibilities, or hiring plans.

The format ends with actual notes and decisions about what to do differently, after enough context has been surfaced. The author contrasts this with conventional strategy meetings that begin at conclusions and invite ego-defending rather than alignment.

The source also treats the ritual as adaptable. At the author's later company, where the founders had small children, the same pattern became a recurring lunch instead of an evening dinner.

A closing story describes three founders who used the process after the CEO feared the CTO was challenging his role. The conversation revealed larger divergence around product direction, next steps, and individual motivations. They chose to close the company and separate while preserving their friendship rather than spend another year on a company whose founders no longer wanted the same thing.

## Key Points

- Founder alignment can fail as personal motivations drift before strategy disagreement is acknowledged.
- A simple cadence and constrained agenda can surface feelings, work concerns, and desired changes without turning into debate first.
- Personal-before-work sequencing exposes the underlying needs behind fights over roles, travel, focus, responsibility, and hiring.
- Equal participation and clarifying questions help reduce status and power effects.
- The ritual can reveal both repairable drift and terminal divergence early enough to choose a cleaner path.
