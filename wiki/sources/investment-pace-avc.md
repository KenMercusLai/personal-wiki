## Summary
[[FredWilson]] argues that high-touch early-stage venture investing imposes a hard capacity constraint: one or two new deals per partner per year can already create a portfolio of roughly seven to fourteen companies when involvement lasts seven to ten years. He presents [[UnionSquareVentures]]' eight-to-ten-deal annual pace as a deliberate service model supported by thesis-driven selection, collaborative decisions, partner bandwidth, and post-investment help. The essay's claims about superior long-run returns and repeated billion-dollar exits are firm-authored assertions without portfolio data or a comparison group.

## Key Claims
- Wilson reports personally leading about sixty early-stage investments over more than thirty years, averaging fewer than two per year.
- USV usually made eight to ten new investments per year, or roughly one to two per partner.
- At one to two new deals annually and an assumed seven-year average holding period, a partner would concurrently support about seven to fourteen portfolio companies; Wilson describes the low end as manageable and the high end as not.
- Early-stage venture capital is framed as a service business in which the entrepreneur and company are the customer, requiring partner attention plus a supporting team.
- Capacity limits require rejecting most opportunities and reserving commitments for investments that survive a high-conviction selection process.
- USV's stated mechanism combines an evolving investment thesis, rigorous collaborative decisions, and substantial post-investment value-add rather than broad "spray and pray," herd, or momentum investing.
- Wilson says USV expected at least one billion-dollar-plus exit per year and had achieved that for almost ten years, but the article supplies no company list, return definition, fund-level results, or independent verification.

## Key Quotes
> "early stage venture capital done right is a service business" - Wilson's rationale for treating partner attention as a portfolio-capacity constraint.

> "managing the investment pace tightly" - the operating consequence Wilson draws from long holding periods and high-touch support.

## Contradictions
- The essay quantifies a plausible workload relationship but does not measure time spent per company, reserve and follow-on work, company-stage differences, exits before seven years, partner turnover, shared board coverage, or the contribution of platform teams.
- Wilson's statement that thesis-driven active early-stage investing has always produced the best returns is broader than the evidence supplied and is not compared with passive, index-like, diversified, or higher-volume venture strategies.
- The claimed sequence of annual billion-dollar-plus exits is not accompanied by company names, ownership, realized versus announced value, fund attribution, or net return data; it should not be treated as independently established performance.
- Framing founders as customers emphasizes service and alignment but does not remove governance duties, fund-return incentives, power asymmetry, or possible conflict between a portfolio company and the investor's other obligations.
