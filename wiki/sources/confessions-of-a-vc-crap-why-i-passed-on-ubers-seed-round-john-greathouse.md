## Summary
[[JohnGreathouse]] explains that he declined even to hear [[Uber]]'s seed pitch and later rejected its Series A because he saw a thesis-mismatched service business, limited city potential, regulatory exposure, and a difficult two-sided market. In retrospect, he identifies Uber's focused San Francisco and premium-town-car entry, expansion into the taxi market, and use of riders as political advocates as mechanisms he underestimated. His practical lesson is narrower than indiscriminate optimism: trusted repeated referrals should earn direct diligence rather than rejection based on secondhand framing.

## Key Claims
- Greathouse did not hear Travis Kalanick's seed pitch despite repeated referrals and later rejected Uber's Series A as too expensive relative to its progress.
- His initial filter favored software sales over real-world service fulfillment, causing him to treat Uber as outside Rincon Venture Partners' thesis before testing whether that description captured the opportunity.
- He and his partner expected licensing conflict and insufficient supply-demand density outside a small set of technology-oriented cities.
- [[Uber]] reduced its [[MarketplaceColdStart]] risk by starting in dense San Francisco, serving smartphone-owning and price-insensitive town-car customers, and building one local market before broadening.
- The narrow premium-car entry obscured the larger possibility of replacing taxis rather than remaining a town-car niche.
- Uber used free rides, superior service, social media, and direct email tools to turn customer demand into pressure against hostile local regulation.
- The author concludes that repeated recommendations from a trusted operator should trigger a direct meeting, because secondhand descriptions can hide a founder's intended market and expansion path.

## Key Quotes
> "It might work in San Francisco, Boulder and Austin, but I don't see it expanding beyond a few small cities with strong tech communities." - Greathouse's recalled 2010 market-size judgment.

> "Listen To Trusted Sources And Take The Freakin' Meeting" - the author's retrospective diligence lesson.
