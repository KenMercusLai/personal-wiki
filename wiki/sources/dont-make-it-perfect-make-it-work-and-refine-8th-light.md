## Summary
[[NickDyer]] argues that software developers can escape [[AnalysisParalysis]] by narrowing plausible options, limiting deliberation, making a cheap and reversible first choice, and then using the implementation as evidence. The proposed [[IterativeRefinement]] loop separates starting from finishing: version control, small tested steps, refactoring, and a deliberate stopping decision preserve quality while reducing the cost of choosing imperfectly. The article's two image references are duplicates of a decorative hero photograph and add no evidence beyond the prose.

## Key Claims
- Too many plausible implementation choices can consume attention through anxiety, self-doubt, and decision fatigue without producing evidence.
- Once clearly unsuitable options have been removed, a time limit, instinct check, or even a coin toss can break a low-stakes tie between similarly viable choices.
- In reversible software work, implementing one option cheaply can generate more useful information than continuing to predict every outcome in the abstract.
- A quick first decision is not permission to ship mediocre code; it begins a refinement loop of assessment, testing, refactoring, naming improvement, and simplification.
- Successive refinement, test-driven development, and Kent Beck's simple-design rules all reduce large speculative decisions into small, changeable steps.
- Quality remains governed by the standard for stopping, even when work begins with an intentionally incomplete solution.

## Key Quotes
> "Making a decision is the first step, not the last." - on separating commitment from refinement

> "We are making a quick decision in order to make a better one." - on using implementation to produce decision evidence
