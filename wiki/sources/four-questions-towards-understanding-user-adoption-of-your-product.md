## Summary
[[JoshElman]] argues that early products should diagnose adoption by interviewing "bounceback" users who abandoned the product, returned after at least a week or preferably a month, and then became highly active. Their before-and-after stories can reveal the original promise, first-use failure, return trigger, and successful activation behavior more clearly than generic retention benchmarks alone. The source uses [[Twitter]]'s 2009 research with [[AdaptivePath]] as a case in which learning that returners followed useful accounts led to changes in onboarding and messaging.

## Key Claims
- Early-stage teams should first understand the small group of deeply engaged core users rather than optimize aggregate retention against generic benchmarks.
- Bounceback users are especially informative because the gap between their failed first attempt and later habitual use creates a natural contrast between non-activation and activation.
- Teams should select currently active users with varied gaps between signup and habitual use and aim for roughly ten interviews, which may be divided among team members.
- Four questions recover the adoption sequence: why the user first tried the product, what failed or confused them, why they returned, and what worked on the successful attempt.
- Repeated patterns should inform both marketing and product design: emphasize the messages that prompted a return and simplify the behaviors that produced active use.
- Twitter reportedly learned that first-time users often saw the service as a broadcasting megaphone, while returners who deliberately followed relevant people or services built a useful timeline; onboarding and messaging shifted toward finding and following.
- The exercise should be repeated approximately every six months because adoption barriers and successful paths can change.

## Key Quotes
> "Ignore the benchmarks. Find the patterns in the stories of people who do get your product." - recommendation for early-stage adoption diagnosis.

> "What worked the second time that got you using it more frequently?" - question that isolates the behavior or condition associated with activation.

## Contradictions
- The source does not directly contradict existing pages. It qualifies benchmark- and analytics-led growth advice by prioritizing purposively sampled interviews during early product discovery, but the two evidence types can be complementary rather than mutually exclusive.
- The method is a practitioner recommendation supported by one historical Twitter case. It provides no interview protocol beyond four prompts, no sampling or analysis safeguards, no measured comparison with other research methods, and no evidence that ten interviews or a six-month cadence is generally sufficient.
