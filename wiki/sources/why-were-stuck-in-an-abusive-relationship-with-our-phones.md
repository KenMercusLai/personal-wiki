## Summary
[[ChrisBrycki]] argues that smartphone notifications combine interruption costs, fear of missing out, variable rewards, and engagement-driven product incentives into a system that repeatedly captures attention. He places responsibility across users, developers, and mobile platforms: individuals can batch notifications through do-not-disturb, but [[NotificationDesign]] should also help developers deliver fewer, higher-value alerts by using platform context and response data.

## Key Claims
- The article reports an average of about 65 phone notifications per day, or roughly one every 16 waking minutes, making interruption a routine rather than exceptional demand on [[AttentionManagement]].
- It cites research saying interrupted workers take about 25 minutes to return to a task and that notifications can substantially increase errors on attention-demanding work.
- Fear of missing out and variable rewards make people reluctant to disable notifications, even when they say the volume is harmful.
- Product teams measured by time spent and daily active users have an incentive to send more alerts because one useful notification may preserve engagement.
- Building intelligent notification prioritization inside each app is costly, while [[Apple]] and [[Google]] control platform context such as sleep, meetings, driving, and notification-open history.
- [[NotificationDesign]] should therefore optimize for fewer, higher-value interruptions and "time well spent," rather than raw attention capture.
- Always-on do-not-disturb offers an individual fallback: alerts accumulate for deliberate review instead of arriving as involuntary interruptions.

## Key Quotes
> "the true scarce commodity is increasingly human attention" - Satya Nadella, quoted on competition for attention.

> "we each have the power to take back control of how and when we have information delivered to us" - on the author's do-not-disturb practice.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction with existing wiki content. The article qualifies individual-discipline advice by arguing that engagement metrics, app incentives, and operating-system capabilities shape the environment in which self-control is exercised.
- The numerical estimates are drawn from 2014-2015 studies or the author's extrapolation, and the willpower-depletion explanation is presented by the source rather than independently established by this note.
