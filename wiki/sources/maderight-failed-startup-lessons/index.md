## Summary

This source is a first-person shutdown reflection about Maderight, a software-powered apparel manufacturer. The company let customers submit designs and manage prototyping, production, and logistics through a web app. It had been accepted into Y Combinator and StartX, raised venture funding, and operated for three years, but it shut down after failing to find product-market fit.

The article frames early startups as bundles of assumptions that need to be documented, tested, and evaluated. Maderight eventually used a recurring question set to name the hypotheses that mattered, choose tests such as customer conversations, sales, or landing pages, decide how results would be evaluated, and keep weekly work tied to learning speed.

The second lesson argues that runway should be judged by the number of meaningful hypotheses it can test, not just by calendar duration. Maderight was proud of operating lean, but the author later believed the team should have examined a small owned factory more seriously because it might have put them closer to lead-time and quality problems than a leaner service-team path.

The sales lesson is that founder-led sales is mandatory before product-market fit. Maderight hired a VP of sales too early, then took too long to reverse the decision. In the source's framing, pre-fit sales is less about revenue than customer discovery, problem identification, and testing whether a changing product actually solves the right problem.

The final lesson is about avoiding "do both" choices. Maderight had revenue and repeat orders from its service business while new customers began adopting the technology product. The team chose to serve both paths because it feared losing revenue, but the split divided time and resources enough that both suffered.
