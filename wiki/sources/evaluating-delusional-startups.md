## Summary
[[ZachHolman]] offers candidates an interview-stage screen for startup delusion: distrust promises that employee equity will make them rich, notice blame and contempt in how people discuss colleagues, and test whether product confidence has hardened into complacency or hostility toward competitors. He distinguishes the optimism needed to build something new from grandiosity that suppresses uncertainty, accountability, learning, and realistic compensation judgment.

## Key Claims
- Startup equity should not be treated as dependable pay, especially when a recruiter or leader presents wealth as assured rather than contingent.
- Interview failures, public blame, complaints about leadership, and contemptuous descriptions of coworkers can reveal how a company responds to mistakes and conflict.
- A blameless culture is better positioned to recover from incidents because teams can examine setbacks without first protecting themselves from humiliation.
- Product pride becomes a warning sign when past reputation substitutes for continued improvement or when leaders cannot describe what remains inadequate.
- Nuanced discussion of competitors is more informative than flat hatred because it tests whether the company understands its own product philosophy and differentiation.
- Startups require unusual conviction, but ambition should remain compatible with caution, humility, and honest uncertainty.

## Key Quotes
> "don't join a startup for the pay" - on treating promised equity riches as a recruiting warning.

> "Blameless cultures are better cultures." - on recovery from mistakes and setbacks.

## Contradictions
- The article qualifies optimistic startup-equity narratives by arguing that salary, bonus, and stock outcomes should not be conflated and that employee wealth is never assured.
- Its rejection of blame does not reject accountability; the implied distinction is between learning from failures and humiliating individuals or teams.
- The evidence is a humorous first-person essay built from a few interviews, friends' reported compensation outcomes, and one quoted executive. It does not establish how accurately these signals predict company performance or employee experience.
