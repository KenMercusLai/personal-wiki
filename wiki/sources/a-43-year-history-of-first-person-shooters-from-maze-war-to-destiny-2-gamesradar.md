## Summary
GamesRadar+ presents [[FirstPersonShooterEvolution]] as a long refinement of one core loop: aiming, moving, shooting, and making feedback feel immediate. The history runs from non-commercial first-person experiments through arcade vector combat, PC run-and-gun and modding cultures, console control breakthroughs, cinematic campaigns, online multiplayer, and finally always-online co-op progression. The article frames the genre's development as partly shaped by the different affordances of PC keyboard/mouse play and console controllers.

## Key Claims
- [[FirstPersonShooterEvolution]] began before commercial FPS markets with Maze War and Spasim experimenting with first-person movement, avatars, maps, and shooting.
- Arcade and home-computer titles such as Battlezone and MIDI Maze commercialized or broadened early first-person play, with MIDI Maze foreshadowing networked multiplayer.
- [[IdSoftware]] turned the FPS into a durable mainstream PC genre through Wolfenstein 3D, Doom, and Quake, combining technical advances, speed, modding, deathmatch, and community depth.
- [[Bungie]], [[Rare]], and console hardware makers helped make console FPS legitimate by adapting pacing, level design, multiplayer, and twin-stick control for living-room play.
- [[Valve]], [[EpicGames]], and player mod communities helped split the mature FPS ecosystem into cinematic single-player, arena multiplayer, tactical team play, and hybrid forms.
- Modern Warfare and Destiny extended the genre toward annualized blockbuster releases, eSports-friendly progression systems, and live-service co-op persistence.

## Key Quotes
> "At their core, first-person shooters are, and always have been, about making a simple gameplay loop - aiming, moving and shooting - feel good." - Thesis framing the genre around feel, feedback, and immediacy.

> "Doom was the first FPS to successfully foster the kind of massive-scale user base so many shooters still seek today." - Context for Doom's modding, co-op, and deathmatch importance.
