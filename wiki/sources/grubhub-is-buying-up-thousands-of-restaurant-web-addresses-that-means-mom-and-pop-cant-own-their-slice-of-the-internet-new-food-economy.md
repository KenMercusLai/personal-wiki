## Summary
[[HClaireBrown]] reports that [[Grubhub]] and subsidiary [[Seamless]] registered tens of thousands of domains associated with restaurants and used generic microsites and platform-controlled phone numbers to route customers into commission-bearing orders. Restaurant owner accounts and site comparisons frame this as control over branded discovery and direct customer relationships, while Grubhub says the domains and microsites were contract-authorized marketing services that it would transfer on request. The article connects domain ownership, search position, ranked marketplace visibility, call attribution, and thin restaurant margins as mechanisms of [[AggregatorMonopolyPower]], while preserving disagreement about permission and merchant benefit.

## Key Claims
- The publication found more than 23,000 domains registered by Grubhub and thousands more by Seamless, mostly appearing to correspond to real restaurants; it cautions that some registrations may later have expired.
- Generic microsites used restaurant identities while linking only to Grubhub and displaying platform-controlled forwarding numbers, allowing the platform to attribute calls and charge commissions.
- Some Grubhub-controlled pages appeared to compete in search with restaurants' independent sites, potentially recasting customers already seeking a named restaurant as platform-originated demand.
- One Brooklyn and Queens operator's invoice showed an $8,000 monthly payment to Grubhub, and she said a direct lunch order left the restaurant about 30% more revenue than a platform-mediated order.
- Grubhub executives reportedly estimated about $30 million in annual phone commissions, while the platform's order-detection errors placed the burden on restaurants to audit recorded calls and dispute charges.
- Permission is contested: restaurant owners said they did not knowingly authorize the sites, while Grubhub said its contracts permitted the practice, denied cybersquatting, had stopped offering it, and would transfer domains on request.
- Merchant value depends on context: an owner who lacked his own delivery operation regarded the extra web presence as potentially useful, while operators with direct ordering and delivery had more to lose from intermediation.

## Key Quotes
> "There was just one problem: Someone already owned the web domain that matched her restaurant's name. She looked up the buyer. It was GrubHub." — Brown on the barrier Shivane encountered when considering independent online ordering.

> "You have GrubHub coming in and inserting itself between a restaurant and a customer" — [[StacyMitchell]] on platform control of a nominally direct relationship.

## Contradictions
- Owners' claims that they did not give permission conflict with Grubhub's statement that domain registration and microsites were consistent with restaurant contracts; the article does not reproduce the relevant agreements or establish what owners understood when signing.
- The article's framing of shadow pages as harmful is qualified by Steve Warwick's view that extra web presence might help his business and by differences in whether a restaurant needs platform-provided delivery.
- Counts, fees, search placement, and commission estimates are historical and source-scoped. The article does not provide a complete registration audit, current domain status, search-ranking experiment, representative merchant sample, or independent calculation of net platform value.
