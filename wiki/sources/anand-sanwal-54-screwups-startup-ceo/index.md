## Summary

Anand Sanwal's article is a first-person list of 54 mistakes he made while building CB Insights as founder and CEO. The mistakes span culture, hiring, pricing, sales and marketing, product management, administration, infrastructure, and founder behavior. The article's core lesson is that early startup improvisation stops working as the company grows; practices that once felt scrappy have to become explicit operating systems.

The culture section argues that culture, feedback, context, onboarding, celebration, and cross-team awareness do not emerge automatically from hiring smart people. Sanwal says CB Insights had to write a Culture Code, improve onboarding, do more one-on-ones and feedback, tell the team more about company direction, and make work across functions more visible.

The hiring section focuses on quality, timing, and role clarity. The company hired smart people without clear roles, hired under pressure, moved too slowly on important candidates, fired too slowly, skipped or underused reference checks, and expected new hires to solve hard problems almost magically. It also tried remote work before internal workflows and processes were ready, and hired customer success later than the pace of product change required.

The sales and marketing section is a founder-led learning account. Sanwal says he spent too much early time on product work instead of selling, tried to sound institutional with jargon, relied on partnerships to solve distribution, chased poor leads, avoided asking for the sale, followed up poorly after demos, took rejection personally, and gave prospects unnecessary reasons not to buy. He later saw sales improve when conversations became more diagnostic and customer-centered.

The pricing and product sections reinforce focus. CB Insights initially competed on cheap pricing and confusing plan language, then moved toward value-based pricing and clearer customer selection. Sanwal also warns against taking product advice from non-customers, chasing fads, over-polishing before shipping, obsessing over competitors, and building features because competitors did rather than because customers needed them.

The operational and miscellaneous items show how small founder habits become company constraints. Staying too long in a cramped office, manually running payroll, delaying advisor changes, buying from customers without diligence, networking without agenda, single opt-in introductions, and casual investor meetings all consumed attention that should have gone to product, customers, and team execution.

## Key Points

- Culture needs deliberate systems once a startup grows beyond direct founder contact.
- Hiring quality depends on clear roles, patient evaluation, useful references, and fast correction of bad fits.
- Founder-led sales teaches the company how customers think before sales can be treated as a repeatable machine.
- Pricing is a market-selection and positioning tool, not just a revenue lever.
- Product priorities should come from customers and credible prospects, not fads, non-customers, or competitor anxiety.
- Remote work requires operating practices before distance is added to an immature workflow.
- Founder attention is a scarce resource; networking, advisors, admin, and fundraising conversations need explicit filters.
