## Summary
[[LucaDellanna]] argues that aggregators such as [[Airbnb]], [[Uber]], and [[Spotify]] profit from two coupled advantages: control over user demand and enough trust infrastructure to let strangers transact without separately vetting each other. The article extends [[AggregationTheory]] by claiming that aggregators flatten profit distribution among suppliers while centralizing trust, demand, and profits at the aggregator layer. It also introduces [[TrustMinimizationTechnology]] as a possible future threat to aggregators when smart contracts, smart locks, self-driving cars, or similar "dry technologies" reduce the need for platform-provided trust.

## Key Claims
- [[AggregationTheory]] should include trust transfer, not only demand control and matching, because customers often use popularity as a proxy for supplier trustworthiness.
- Aggregators reduce the need for buyers, sellers, hosts, guests, riders, drivers, advertisers, or platforms to trust one another directly.
- When an aggregator becomes trusted by demand, suppliers no longer need to be individually famous, so profits can flatten among suppliers while centralizing at the aggregator layer.
- [[Airbnb]] illustrates the trust mechanism through reviews, payment timing, insurance, reimbursement, and the threat of delisting.
- [[Uber]] illustrates how ride-hailing replaces taxi trust and dispatch with a platform-mediated stranger-transaction system.
- Level-3 aggregators such as Google and Facebook also contain a trust element because advertisers trust targeting and impression quality.
- [[TrustMinimizationTechnology]] could erode the portion of aggregator profit tied to trust enablement, even if demand ownership remains hard to attack.

## Key Quotes
> "customers use popularity as a proxy" - on why trusted suppliers capture profit without aggregators.

> "transfers the trustworthiness burden" - on the aggregator moving trust and profit up a layer.

> "trusted shelves are limited" - on digital abundance still producing monopoly-like trusted discovery surfaces.
