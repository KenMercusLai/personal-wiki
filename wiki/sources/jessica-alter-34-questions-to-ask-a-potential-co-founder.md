## Summary
Founder Institute republishes [[JessicaAlter]]'s discussion prompts for prospective co-founders across personal motivation, financial and family constraints, working style, culture, roles, authority, equity, and legal conflicts. The checklist is useful as an agenda for exposing assumptions before commitment, but it is practitioner guidance rather than evidence that asking the questions predicts compatibility; the article itself says conflict behavior is best learned through a side project or actual work together.

![Two people discussing a potential co-founder relationship across a cafe table](../../wiki-assets/jessica-alter-34-questions-to-ask-a-potential-co-founder/potential-co-founders-in-conversation.jpg)

## Key Claims
- Friendship or family history does not establish alignment; prospective co-founders should compare why they are starting, what motivates them, how much time they will commit, and what outcomes they want.
- Personal cash needs, unpaid-work tolerance, outside commitments, location, fundraising appetite, and family responsibilities can materially shape the partnership and should be discussed early.
- Cultural fit includes desired values, working style, attitudes toward feedback and conflict, product taste, employee-equity reserves, and assumptions about the first hires.
- Founders should make role, CEO, voting, firing, departure, cash-investment, equity-split, and decision-right expectations explicit before those questions become disputes.
- A side project or period of working together reveals conflict behavior more credibly than self-description alone, while references can add third-party evidence about past conduct.
- Adaptability is part of founder fit because product, market, and company plans will change; an immovable assumption can become a partnership risk.

## Key Quotes
> "Fights are healthy, how you get through them is the question." - on evaluating conflict behavior through joint work.

> "the only guarantee is that things will change" - on treating adaptability as a co-founder criterion.

## Contradictions
- The title promises 34 questions, but the supplied article visibly numbers 35 top-level prompts across its four sections (8 + 9 + 9 + 9), before counting nested follow-ups.
- The article supplies a broad conversation agenda, not validated selection criteria or outcome evidence. Answers may be aspirational, rehearsed, incomplete, or change over time; its own side-project advice implicitly qualifies reliance on discussion alone.
- Questions about spouses, children, criminal history, health-adjacent stress behavior, references, conflicts, and non-compete obligations can involve privacy, bias, consent, and jurisdiction-specific legal issues. The checklist is not employment or legal guidance, and sensitive inquiries should be limited to information genuinely relevant to the partnership.
