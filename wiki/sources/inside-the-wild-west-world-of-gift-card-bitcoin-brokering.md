## Summary
The Intercept follows an anonymous broker who uses [[Paxful]] to sell [[Bitcoin]] at a premium, accepts retailer gift-card codes, converts them into fresh gaming gift cards at [[Walmart]], and resells those cards to replenish his bitcoin. The case shows how [[PeerToPeerCryptoTrading]] can serve legitimate access and informal work while sharing infrastructure with [[GiftCardFraud]]: reputation checks, immediate redemption, card-for-card conversion, retailer controls, and dispute resolution all manage risk without proving where a card originated. Walmart's announced 2018 restrictions targeted this laundering path, but the broker reported denomination-level gaps that still allowed it in practice.

The source references one remote lead image, but the original URL returned HTTP 404 during ingest and no reliable duplicate was found, so it could not be inspected or retained and contributes no image-derived evidence.

## Key Claims
- The broker sold [[Bitcoin]] internationally on [[Paxful]] for gift-card codes, with the marketplace reportedly taking about 1 percent and providing transaction histories plus dispute resolution.
- His quoted bitcoin markup had fallen from roughly 100 percent to about 30 percent as competition and volatility increased; in the observed $400 trade, a 28 percent sale premium and 25 percent repurchase premium left a 3 percent, or $12, margin.
- Gift cards acted as cross-border payment instruments because codes could be transmitted electronically, redeemed through an app-generated barcode, converted into globally desirable gaming cards, and resold without physical delivery.
- Buying new cards with received cards reduced the broker's exposure to a seller draining the original balance, but the same conversion could turn stolen or counterfeit value into fresh cards and make victim recovery harder.
- The broker delayed releasing bitcoin until he had spent the full card balance and checked counterparties' histories, illustrating a trust workflow that reduces his own loss without establishing lawful provenance.
- Target and Best Buy reportedly stopped gift-card-for-gift-card purchases before Walmart; Walmart's announced nationwide changes limited loads, purchase amounts, and certain gaming-card purchases while adding employee training.
- The broker said Walmart's two-card limit was applied per denomination rather than across a transaction, contradicting the spokesperson's account of the new policy and leaving a practical enforcement gap.

## Key Quotes
> "It's so I have a card that no one but me and whoever I'm selling it to has seen." - the broker on converting received cards into fresh cards.

> "It would hurt my livelihood if Walmart changed its policy, but it would protect the little guy." - the broker on supporting controls that threatened his own income.

## Connections
- [[Bitcoin]] - asset sold at a premium and reacquired through the gift-card resale loop.
- [[Paxful]] - peer-to-peer marketplace used for counterparty discovery, reputation history, and disputes.
- [[Walmart]] - retailer whose checkout and gift-card controls made the observed conversion possible.
- [[GiftCardFraud]] - stolen-value risk that card-for-card conversion can conceal and lock in.
- [[PeerToPeerCryptoTrading]] - informal market structure connecting global buyers with a local broker.
- [[MarketplaceTrust]] - reputation, delayed release, spend verification, and dispute resolution compensate for weak counterparty knowledge.

## Contradictions
- Walmart's spokesperson said the newly restricted purchases were not allowed, while the broker reported completing them by buying two cards in each of several denominations. This is an implementation-versus-policy contradiction within the source, not proof of Walmart's later or current practice.
- The broker described himself as trying to trade honestly and expose a harmful loophole, but his risk-reduction method used the same conversion mechanism that can launder stolen gift-card value.
