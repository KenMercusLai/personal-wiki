## Summary
Botnerds argues that "chatbot" is a poor general label for the bot industry because it sets humanlike chat expectations, names only one narrow category of software agent, and trivializes business automation work. The article qualifies [[ConversationalUI]] and [[MessagingAsPlatform]] by separating chat-only bots such as [[Xiaoice]] from task-oriented bots that should be described more neutrally as bots or automation. The inspected images support the argument through a failed CNN Messenger interaction, a Google Trends spike around Facebook F8 2016, and a small survey chart comparing business terminology preferences.

## Key Claims
- "Chatbot" sets unrealistic user expectations because casual human chat suggests open-ended language understanding that 2016-era NLP could not reliably deliver.
- Directed bot conversations should signal constraints clearly so users understand the syntax, scope, and likely recovery path.
- "Chatbot" is accurate for systems whose main purpose is open-ended conversation, including historical Turing-test style systems and modern companions such as [[Xiaoice]].
- The broader bot industry should use "bot" rather than "chatbot" because many valuable bots perform work rather than merely converse.
- In B2B contexts, "chatbot" can trivialize the product category compared with business-process or automation language.
- Poor terminology can create adoption friction because buyers and users react to what the label makes them expect, not only to what builders intend.

## Key Quotes
> "words matter" - on why category language shapes an emerging industry.

> "Bot is a short, memorable, and neutral." - on the preferred general label.

> "Don't build people's expectations too much" - quoted Guardian lesson on keeping bot experiences simple.
