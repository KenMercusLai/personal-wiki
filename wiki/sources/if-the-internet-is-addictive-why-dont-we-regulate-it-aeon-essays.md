## Summary
[[MichaelSchulson]] argues that addiction-like internet use is neither an inevitable property of the network nor solely a failure of individual self-control: attention-funded products deliberately combine variable rewards, repeated triggers, and user investment to prolong engagement. Drawing on [[NirEyal]]'s habit model, [[NatashaSchull]]'s gambling research, and [[TristanHarris]]'s Time Well Spent proposal, he treats compulsive use as an engineered human-machine interaction shaped by an [[EngagementIncentiveConflict]]. He proposes [[DigitalCompulsionRegulation]] that would expand user agency through distraction controls, breaks in endless consumption, warnings for unusually compulsive patterns, usage feedback, and voluntary cutoffs, while acknowledging diagnostic uncertainty and regulatory evasion.

## Key Claims
- Variable and unpredictable rewards can produce persistent checking even when most actions deliver nothing valuable, making Skinner's reinforcement experiments and slot machines useful analogies for email, feeds, and games.
- “Internet addiction” lacks a stable diagnosis or prevalence estimate, and the useful network medium must be distinguished from particular experiences such as gambling, pornography, social feeds, or compulsive email.
- The four-stage *Hooked* cycle—trigger, action, variable reward, and investment—turns repeated product use into a self-reinforcing pattern, but the same behavior-design tools can also support beneficial actions such as medication adherence.
- In an advertising-funded [[AttentionEconomy]], longer engagement produces inventory and revenue, so appeals to voluntary moderation conflict with the business incentive to amplify users' wants into longer sessions.
- Schüll's “machine zone” reframes apparent distraction as narrow focus on repetitive motion and reward, produced through the interaction between a person and a deliberately designed machine.
- Individual blocking tools and “organic web” alternatives can restore some control, but effort, technical knowledge, and access make them an incomplete response to a system-level power imbalance.
- Regulation could require granular delivery controls, prohibit or interrupt endless-feed features, detect and warn about problematic usage patterns, show usage measures, and enforce user-selected cutoffs.

## Key Quotes
> “The internet is not a predetermined experience.” — Schulson separates the network from particular compulsive designs.

> “There’s a balance of power.” — the article frames attention and privacy exchange as a contract whose architecture favors designers.

## Contradictions
- The article explicitly rejects a settled clinical category: it supplies analogies, interviews, examples, and policy arguments rather than diagnostic criteria, prevalence estimates, controlled product experiments, or evidence that any named feature causes addiction.
- Gambling machines impose direct monetary loss per continued play, whereas useful digital services exchange attention and privacy for communication, information, or entertainment; the analogy identifies design mechanisms and power, not equivalence of harm.
- Eyal distinguishes persuasion from coercion, describes beneficial health uses, refuses some clients, and supports warnings for severe patterns; Schüll replies that design can amplify an existing want beyond what the user intended.
- The proposed dashboard, feature-ban, warning, and cutoff rules are speculative. The article anticipates evasion, does not define thresholds or enforcement, and does not address privacy, false positives, accessibility, emergency use, or evidence that these interventions improve welfare.
- Time Well Spent and blocking tools may increase agency, but voluntary adoption can favor affluent, technically confident users and leave the underlying revenue incentive intact.
