## Summary
[[SimonShaw]] reports a February 2019 [[TrinityMcQueen]] experiment in which a nationally representative sample of 2,102 British adults evaluated nine real hotel-booking scarcity and social-proof claims. Most participants interpreted the prompts as sales pressure, many expected them to reduce trust, and few believed them, leading Shaw to argue that repeated exposure can teach consumers to recognize influence tactics and trigger [[PsychologicalReactance]]. The article treats behavioral interventions as part of a changing feedback loop rather than context-free techniques with stable effects.

## Key Claims
- In the hypothetical hotel-booking scenario, 65% interpreted the scarcity and social-proof claims as sales pressure, 49% said the claims were likely to make them distrust the company, and 16% believed them.
- A third of participants reported a negative emotional response such as contempt or disgust, which the author links to perceived intent to create anxiety and hurry a purchase.
- Repeated exposure can change how people interpret a heuristic: a familiar scarcity or popularity prompt may become evidence of manipulation rather than evidence about supply or demand.
- The author hypothesizes [[PsychologicalReactance]] because 40% agreed they feel like doing the opposite when forced, while seven in ten preferred a competitor when a large company dominates a market.
- Intervention effects should be re-evaluated over time and in context instead of being assumed from earlier studies or transferred unchanged across domains.
- Commercial overuse may damage trust in behavioral science and could weaken related interventions used for prosocial goals, although the study does not test that spillover.

## Key Quotes
> "Heuristics are dynamic, not static" - Shaw's summary of how experience changes the meaning of familiar prompts.

> "Feedback loops affect the efficacy of behavioral interventions more than we realize." - the article's practical conclusion.

## Connections
- [[SimonShaw]] - author and interpreter of the 2019 study.

## Contradictions
- Qualifies [[12-best-practices-for-boosting-product-page-conversions]], which includes social proof, scarcity, and urgency as conversion levers: in this study's British hotel-booking scenario, similar prompts were more often read as pressure than believed.
- The article does not establish that all nudges backfire. It evaluates bundled scarcity and social-proof claims in one hypothetical commercial context and presents cross-domain erosion as a future research question.
