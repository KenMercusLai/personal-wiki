## Summary
[[LindsayHolmwood]] argues that moving from engineering into management is a career change rather than a promotion because the manager's main work is to multiply team effectiveness through listening, trust, conflict resolution, advocacy, context, and systemic improvement. The article attributes engineers' under-preparation partly to the technology industry's devaluation of non-technical skills and partly to overconfidence about unfamiliar management work. It recommends changing the measure of success from individual output to group performance and deliberately building management capability through training, diverse mentors, peer support, self-education, and reflection.

## Key Claims
- [[ManagementRoleFit]] requires treating management as a distinct discipline whose daily work and success criteria differ substantially from individual engineering contribution.
- A manager acts as a multiplier by removing roadblocks and interruptions, connecting strategy to actionable team context, advocating for people, resolving conflict justly, and improving systems with the team.
- Technology culture often rewards technical output while overlooking listening, teamwork, coordination, and conflict resolution, leaving new managers underprepared for people leadership.
- The article invokes the Dunning-Kruger effect to argue that engineers without leadership experience may overestimate their management competence and underestimate expertise in others.
- Management mistakes have multiplied human consequences because a leader influences team members' happiness, wellbeing, trust, and future workplace expectations.
- Satisfaction must shift from personal engineering performance and visible output to group performance; otherwise necessary managerial work may feel unreal or unproductive.
- New managers should combine professional training, multiple mentors, peers undergoing the same transition, broad reading, written critique, emotional reflection, and continued practice.

## Key Quotes
> "Management is not a promotion. It is a career change." — the article's central distinction

> "Your job is not to be an engineer. Your job is not to be a manager. Your job is to be a multiplier." — the proposed purpose of leadership

> "You only reap the benefits of this if you shift your measure of job satisfaction from your own performance to the group's." — the required change in success criteria

## Contradictions
- No direct factual contradiction was found. The categorical claim that a manager's job is not engineering is qualified by [[EngineeringManagerRoleDesign]], which documents manager roles that retain substantial technical contribution depending on team topology and scope.
- The industry-wide undervaluation claim, the application of the Dunning-Kruger effect to engineers, and the proposed development practices are practitioner arguments without comparative outcome evidence in the source.
