## Summary
[[EduardsSizovs]] argues that companies create an avoidable talent shortage when they search only for already polished, self-confident developers while giving experienced engineers too little time or preparation to teach. He proposes [[EngineeringMentorship]] as organizational capacity: hire for learning attitude, relax unnecessary requirements, and develop technical skill through pair programming, continuous feedback, career guidance, learning resources, and beginner bootcamps. The article is a practitioner argument rather than comparative evidence, and its "broken toys" and "raw diamonds" metaphors risk objectifying candidates whose confidence or prior environment differs from conventional interview expectations.

## Key Claims
- Strong future developers may interview poorly, lack current technical practices, or experience [[ImposterSyndrome]] without lacking the capacity to grow.
- Multi-stage selection processes and rigid job requirements can reject candidates who have not had access to supportive teams or mentors.
- A recurring search for already polished "rockstars" does not expand the supply of capable engineers when every employer competes for the same pool.
- Experienced developers need protected time and communication skill to mentor through pairing, feedback, career guidance, and curated learning opportunities.
- Hiring for eagerness and capacity to learn can shift some organizational investment from prediction during interviews to development after entry.
- Mentoring capacity can simplify selection only if the organization can actually provide sustained supervision, feedback, learning work, and progression.
- Beginner bootcamps are proposed as a profession-level supply intervention, but the source gives no curriculum, access, cost, completion, placement, or job-performance evidence.

## Key Quotes
> "Companies are searching for the best engineers, instead of raising them." - on treating experienced talent as a fixed external supply.

> "Hire for attitude, and teach technical skills." - the article's condensed development-first hiring rule.

## Connections
- [[EduardsSizovs]] - author advocating a development-first alternative to competition for already polished candidates.
- [[EngineeringMentorship]] - the organizational capability at the center of the article's prescription.
- [[HiringSystemDesign]] - the article shifts some emphasis from selection precision toward post-hire development capacity.
- [[JuniorEngineerLearning]] - mentees need pairing, feedback, questions, practice, and progressively useful work to turn adaptability into judgment.
- [[WorkplaceLearning]] - mentoring makes real work, expert reasoning, and feedback available as learning material.
- [[InclusiveHiring]] - relaxing irrelevant requirements may widen entry, while fair evaluation must still distinguish teachable gaps from role-critical capability.
- [[ImposterSyndrome]] - confidence and interview fluency are treated as unreliable proxies for growth potential.

## Contradictions
- The article challenges hiring systems that optimize against false-positive hires, but supplies no evidence comparing its mentoring-first approach with structured, job-relevant selection or showing which skills can be taught within a role's time constraints.
- It argues that mentoring can simplify interviews, yet mentorship itself requires scarce senior time, communication skill, appropriate work, manager support, and enough staffing headroom; moving effort after hiring does not eliminate its cost.
- Hiring for "attitude" or "growth mindset" can become another subjective culture-fit screen unless defined through observable, job-relevant behavior and audited for bias.
- The claim that most women apply only when they meet all requirements is presented without a source and should not be treated as a universal gender rule.
- Mentoring can support confidence and access, but it cannot by itself correct discrimination, unsafe teams, exploitative pay, inaccessible work, or structural exclusion.
