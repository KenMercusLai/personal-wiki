## Summary
[[JustinReich]] satirizes early [[MOOCLearningAnalytics]] studies that used massive clickstream and log datasets from platforms such as [[KhanAcademy]], [[Udacity]], [[GoogleCourseBuilder]], and [[EdX]] to rediscover that students who do more course activities tend to complete or perform better in those same courses. The article argues that rich [[BehavioralData]] often gets compressed into simple effort measures, producing correlations that are plausible but causally limited and pedagogically unsurprising.

## Key Claims
- Large online-learning platforms collect very granular behavioral logs, but published analyses often reduce them to small summary variables such as minutes logged in, problems completed, videos watched, activities completed, or event counts.
- Across Khan Academy, Udacity/San Jose State, Google Course Builder, and HarvardX/edX examples, student activity correlates with achievement, passing, completion, or certification.
- These correlations are not the same as causal evidence that platform activity independently produces learning, because prior ability, motivation, classroom attention, and course fit may also explain both activity and performance.
- The article's "Reich's Law of Doing Stuff" is a deliberately comic label for an obvious pattern: people who do more course work tend to do more and perform better on course work.
- MOOC research should use big-data affordances to ask richer questions about learning processes instead of repeatedly publishing obvious activity-achievement correlations.

## Key Quotes
> "researchers take big data and make it small data" - Reich's critique of log-data compression.

> "students who did stuff were more likely to pass" - the article's satirical condensation of the Udacity finding.

> "activity predicts further activity or effort" - the proposed corollary for studies that only confirm the obvious.
