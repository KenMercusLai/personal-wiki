## Summary
[[DavidFrankel]] argues that the visible milestones of a [[StartupFundingRound]] conceal a volatile investment decision in which enthusiasm can fall after references, industry diligence, or comparison with a newer opportunity. Founders cannot remove every concern, but they can support [[FundraisingMomentum]] by showing continuing commercial progress, concentrating the process, and responding quickly to diligence requests.

## Key Claims
- A founder experiences fundraising as a sequence of deck, meeting, partnership pitch, term-sheet, and closing milestones, while an investor's confidence can rise and fall repeatedly between them.
- An impressive founder background, strong team, product, and traction can create initial excitement, but off-list references may reveal concerns that were invisible in a meeting.
- Industry diligence can weaken conviction when the market appears smaller, more competitive, or more capital-intensive than it first seemed.
- A competing opportunity with apparently greater upside and fewer visible risks can displace a startup even when nothing intrinsic about that startup has worsened.
- Because every deal has weaknesses, continuing evidence of customer wins, sales growth, or market progress can restore confidence after diligence exposes risk.
- A concentrated process can create simultaneous term sheets and time pressure, while fundraising during a seasonal lull or a period of flat revenue can weaken perceived momentum.
- Prompt, transparent support for product, roadmap, customer, and other diligence requests helps preserve attention and trust while delay or obfuscation can end the process.

## Key Quotes
> "Every deal has warts." - Frankel's reason that investor concern does not automatically end a financing process.

> "Momentum matters when it comes to raising venture capital." - the essay's central founder-side prescription.
