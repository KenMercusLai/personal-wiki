## Summary
[[BillBarnett]] argues that recurring scheduled meetings often survive because they symbolize rational work rather than because they produce useful outcomes. Using [[MaxWeber]]'s [[ProceduralRationality]], the article frames formal meetings as a "solution looking for a problem" when people schedule first and ask why later. It contrasts that with [[InformalCollaboration]], where spontaneous conversations continue only while they are useful, citing [[BillDurham]], [[SteveJobs]], and the [[HomebrewComputerClub]] as examples of productive random interaction.

## Key Claims
- Scheduled meetings can consume entire workdays and become self-justifying routines.
- [[ProceduralRationality]] explains why meetings can look rational even when their substantive value is unclear.
- Meeting culture often reverses the right order of reasoning by scheduling first and discovering the agenda later.
- Informal chats can be more useful because participants can stop them when they stop producing value.
- Physical and social environments can be designed to increase productive random encounters, as in Steve Jobs' Apple campus vision and the Homebrew Computer Club memory.
- The practical recommendation is to delete default meetings, protect work time, and meet informally only as long as the interaction remains useful.

## Key Quotes
> "meetings are a perfect example of a solution looking for a problem" - on scheduled meetings without a clear why.

> "Delete all meetings from your calendar." - practical recommendation.
