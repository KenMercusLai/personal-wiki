## Summary

This source is a candidate-side startup diligence article. It starts from the usual attractions of startup work: meaningful daily progress, product impact, less bureaucracy, and a motivated team. It argues that those reasons are incomplete unless a candidate also studies the company's odds of succeeding.

The article rejects the idea that startups are lottery tickets that ordinary employees cannot evaluate. Its practical claim is that a prospective early employee can ask a small set of direct questions that reveal whether the company has funding discipline, credible backers, qualified founders, clean governance, a strong early team, real business progress, and a vision the candidate can believe.

Funding diligence is the first screen. The source distinguishes money raised from runway: two companies can both have $2 million in the bank, but very different burn rates can mean roughly forty months of operating time versus less than seven. Candidates should therefore ask about cash in the bank, burn rate, and projected runway rather than treating a large seed round as enough information.

The article treats investor quality, founder quality, and board structure as early proxies for company judgment. Strong investors can indicate that a startup has passed some external filtering, but the founders should still explain why they chose those investors. Founder assessment should include experience, startup history, temperament, motivation, and whether the candidate expects to learn from the CEO. Board assessment should distinguish founder control from unqualified relatives or angels with decision power.

The article also recommends looking at the early team and the company's milestones. High-quality early employees are a signal because top performers usually want to work with other top performers, while rushed hiring can create long-term cultural damage. Strong milestones are business signals such as recurring revenue, paid customers, or clearly defined active users; press mentions, traffic spikes, fundraising, and accelerator affiliation are weaker if they are not tied to business progress.

Finally, the source makes belief in the startup's idea part of the candidate's responsibility. If the space is unfamiliar, founders should be able to sell the candidate on the vision. If they cannot convince an early employee, the source suggests they may also struggle to convince customers. Joining an early-stage startup is framed as a multi-year career commitment, so the diligence should happen before accepting the role.

## Key Points

- Startup candidates should evaluate company risk instead of treating startup outcomes as pure chance.
- Cash raised is less informative than cash in the bank, burn rate, and projected runway.
- Investors, founders, and board members are signals of judgment, credibility, and future control.
- Early employee quality matters because repeated hiring compromises can shape long-term culture.
- Useful milestones are business metrics such as revenue, paid customers, and active use, not vanity attention.
- A candidate should believe in the idea or give the founders a clear chance to sell the broader vision.
