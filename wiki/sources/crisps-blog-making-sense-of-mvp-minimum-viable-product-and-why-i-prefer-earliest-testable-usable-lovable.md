## Summary
[[HenrikKniberg]] argues that iterative product development should deliver complete, testable slices of a customer outcome rather than unusable components of a predetermined solution. He recasts the ambiguous [[MinimumViableProduct]] label as a progression from [[EarliestTestableUsableLovable]] products, using [[Spotify]], [[Minecraft]], [[PUST]], and [[Lego]] to show how early contact with real users can expose technical feasibility, desirability, context, and design errors. The article's skateboard-to-car sequence is a metaphor for discovering the simplest adequate way to satisfy an underlying need, not a literal production plan for a known car. Its appended comments also preserve an important disagreement: some readers argue that the skateboard is a prototype or agile increment, while Lean Startup MVPs can be non-product experiments such as a video or bus ticket.

## Key Claims
- A delivery creates useful learning only when real users can test a coherent outcome; a sequence of unusable components remains risky even if it is technically incremental.
- Teams should begin with the customer's underlying need rather than treating the requested solution as fixed, because feedback may show that a simpler or different product works better.
- The earliest useful experiment asks for the cheapest and fastest way to learn, and it need not be a polished or broadly released product.
- [[EarliestTestableUsableLovable]] separates three thresholds that the word "viable" often conflates: generating feedback, improving an early adopter's situation enough for voluntary use, and creating something customers love and will pay for.
- Spotify's rough playback prototype tested whether near-instant streaming was technically possible and appealing before the company had a polished interface, full catalog, licensing model, or broad release.
- Minecraft's six-day public build and PUST's restricted crime-type and regional rollout illustrate early external feedback, while Lego Universe illustrates the risk of extensive internal iteration without real-user release.
- Up-front analysis remains useful, but complex product assumptions should be tested through prototypes and releases before large irreversible buildout.

## Key Quotes
> "What is the cheapest and fastest way we can start learning?" - the article's governing question for an early product experiment.

> "Think of the skateboard as a metaphor for the smallest thing you can put in the hands of real users, and get real feedback." - clarification that the vehicle sequence represents learning about an outcome.

> "Your 'viable' is my 'horrible'." - explanation for replacing the ambiguous MVP label with explicit testable, usable, and lovable thresholds.

## Contradictions
- Commenters dispute whether the skateboard sequence accurately represents a Lean Startup MVP: an MVP can be a video, paper prototype, bus ticket, or other experiment rather than a usable product increment. The article partly acknowledges this by considering an earlier "feedbackable" stage, so [[EarliestTestableUsableLovable]] should be read as one vocabulary for release maturity, not a complete definition of every MVP.
- Other commenters argue that replacing a skateboard with a scooter and bicycle repeatedly discards implementation and therefore describes prototypes rather than incremental construction. Kniberg's claim depends on optimizing for the customer's transport outcome, not for cumulative reuse of vehicle parts.
- The success and failure stories are practitioner anecdotes selected retrospectively. They do not isolate early release as the sole cause from talent, funding, distribution, licensing, market timing, technical execution, or organizational conditions.
