## Summary
The Year of the Looking Glass essay distills lessons from a Facebook product-development framework into practical guidance on framing, execution, measurement, and team dynamics. It argues that strong product work begins with a clear problem, audience, and reason to care, then proceeds through broad solution exploration, explicit hypotheses, fast evidence gathering, careful launch-quality decisions, and post-project learning. The repeated steel-frame photographs are illustrative construction imagery and do not add separate evidence beyond the article's product-building metaphor.

## Key Claims
- A product succeeds by solving a real problem for a clearly understood audience, and narrow audiences can make early intuition more reliable.
- Good execution means reaching believable conclusions quickly, while bad execution fails to produce transferable learning or takes too long to learn an avoidable lesson.
- Teams should explore many possible solutions before selecting one, then use empirical evidence and prototype reactions to choose among them.
- Product plans should be framed as hypotheses and tested through the fastest method that can produce a clear signal.
- The quality bar for a test can be lower than the quality bar for broad launch, so teams should make an explicit post-signal launch decision.
- Success metrics should be chosen before launch, paired with countermetrics, and grounded in what the team would want to know if it had a crystal ball.
- Product teams work better when members focus on team success over role boundaries, fall in love with problems rather than solutions, assume good intentions, and run post-mortems after both wins and failures.

## Key Quotes
> "A product succeeds because it solves a problem for people." - on product framing.

> "Good execution is about getting to believable conclusions" - on learning speed.

> "Teams that fall in love with a problem have more successful outcomes" - on team motivation.
