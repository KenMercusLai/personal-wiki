## Summary
[[DanShipper]] argues that asking a new entrepreneur whether they would pursue an idea for ten years is an unreliable and potentially harmful test. Early founders lack evidence about their durable preferences, and volatile short-term results can make a distant commitment feel alternately obvious and intolerable; a better near-term test is whether the work is producing the kind of learning they want.

## Key Claims
- Young entrepreneurs are poor judges of whether a new subject will hold their interest for ten years because temporary enthusiasm is difficult to distinguish from durable commitment.
- A ten-year commitment question sets an unnecessarily high bar during the unstable early phase of company building.
- Using a bad day or a good day to answer a long-horizon question turns noisy short-term outcomes into premature career conclusions.
- Asking whether the work is producing desired learning gives founders a more stable and observable reason to continue through setbacks.
- At [[Firefly]], Shipper says this learning test supplied stamina when surface-level results were discouraging.

## Key Quotes
> "Am I learning? Importantly, am I learning what I want to learn?" - on replacing speculative ten-year commitment with an observable near-term test.

## Contradictions
- The source challenges startup advice that treats willingness to work on one subject for ten years as an early selection test. It does not reject long-term work; it argues that beginners lack enough evidence to forecast their future desire reliably.
