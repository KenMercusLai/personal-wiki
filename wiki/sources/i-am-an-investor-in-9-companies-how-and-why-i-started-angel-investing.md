## Summary
[[JoelGascoigne]] describes how partial liquidity from selling [[Buffer]] shares let him begin [[AngelInvesting]], starting with a $10,000 investment in CloudPeeps in December 2014 and reaching nine companies by August 2016. He treats startup investing as illiquid capital he must be willing to lose, while also seeking personal fulfillment, founder support, social impact, and continued exposure to early-stage company building. His low-time process uses a fixed check size, practical availability to founders, and follow-on investment when affordable, but Buffer's cash-flow problems, his salary reduction, leadership changes, and a $250,000 Buffer share purchase put new investments on hold.

## Key Claims
- Gascoigne's first angel investment followed a relationship-first path: years of knowing Kate Kendall, advising CloudPeeps, discussing the business, and helping with fundraising gave him direct exposure to the founder, product, culture, and community before he invested.
- He classifies startup investment as very high risk and completely illiquid, accepts that most startups fail, and invests only money he is comfortable losing rather than relying on angels as his primary wealth-building strategy.
- The desired return is broader than money: he wants to advise founders, revisit early-stage company building, encourage positive social or environmental contribution, and advocate for sustainable companies that preserve founder freedom, health, finances, and relationships.
- His hobby-scale process standardizes initial checks at $10,000, prioritizes time for founder calls and email over extensive professional analysis, and generally favors follow-on investment when affordable to maintain ownership and concentrate more capital in companies that continue raising.
- From December 2014 through August 2016, he reports investing in CloudPeeps, First Code Academy, inDinero, Conversio, HappyMed, Crew, Outsite, CloudApp, and Akkroo; most checks were $10,000, with euro- and pound-denominated checks slightly larger in dollar terms.
- Gascoigne stopped making new investments after Buffer's 2016 cash-flow problems led him to reduce his salary by 40%, then kept the activity on hold during 2017 leadership changes and a $250,000 personal purchase of Buffer shares from former teammates.
- The retrospective presents direct participation as a learning method: Gascoigne says he still has much to learn as an investor and advisor, but regards starting before mastering the field as one of the process's main lessons.

## Key Quotes
> "I need to be comfortable losing all the money I invest into startups." - on sizing exposure to a highly risky and illiquid asset.

> "I will always be on the founders’ side." - on the investor posture he intends to take, except in cases of obvious bad behavior.

> "One of the best ways to learn is to dive in and start doing it." - on learning angel investing through participation.

## Contradictions
- The source's follow-on heuristic assumes that raising another round is a useful signal of success, but Gascoigne explicitly calls the correlation imperfect; further funding can also reflect capital intensity, favorable markets, or investor momentum rather than durable company performance.
- The reported portfolio, check sizes, Buffer liquidity, salary reduction, and $250,000 share purchase are first-person disclosures without cap tables, transaction documents, independent performance data, or a comparison against rejected investments.
- The essay describes Gascoigne's personal method, not a general angel-investing prescription. It does not address deal instruments, valuation, due diligence, portfolio size needed for diversification, accreditation, tax, conflicts between founder support and investor governance, or realized returns; as of publication he reported no returns after more than three years.
- The source's risk spectrum is informal: grouping cryptocurrency and startup investment together as "very high risk" does not compare volatility, liquidity, loss distribution, time horizon, or portfolio effects rigorously.
