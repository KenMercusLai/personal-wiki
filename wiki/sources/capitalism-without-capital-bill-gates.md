## Summary
[[BillGates]] argues that software and other intangible assets no longer fit the familiar tangible-goods supply-and-demand model because the first unit can be expensive while later units are nearly free to reproduce. Reviewing [[CapitalismWithoutCapital]], he presents [[JonathanHaskel]] and [[StianWestlake]]'s account of intangible investment as a major underappreciated shift with consequences for company valuation, competition, taxation, patents, GDP measurement, and which cities thrive. The source connects Microsoft's early investor education problem to a broader need for policymakers to update economic tools around [[IntangibleAssetEconomics]].

## Key Claims
- Traditional economic models assume production costs rise with output, but software, data, insurance, e-books, and movies often have high first-copy costs and very low marginal-copy costs.
- [[IntangibleAssetEconomics]] changes competition, risk, valuation, taxation, intellectual-property policy, and urban advantage because intangible industries behave differently from manufactured goods.
- [[CapitalismWithoutCapital]] is praised for explaining the transition without treating intangible investment as inherently good, evil, or policy-simple.
- Economic measurement lags behind intangible investment: Gates notes that U.S. GDP did not include software until 1999 and still omits areas such as market research, branding, and training.
- [[Microsoft]]'s early business model made investor education difficult because investors were used to tangible-asset returns rather than long-run software leverage.
- Policymakers should debate patent/trademark strictness, competition policy, taxation, and stimulus tools for a world where more capitalism happens without conventional capital.

## Key Quotes
> "software is an intangible asset" - Gates contrasting software with cars, agriculture, and property.

> "the rules that govern the economy haven't kept up" - Gates on the policy gap created by rising intangible investment.
