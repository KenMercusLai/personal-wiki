## Summary
[[JimScheinman]] compares his experience at [[Friendster]] and [[Bebo]] with Facebook's early development, presenting ten linked operating lessons about focus, product cadence, performance, technology choice, virality, customer selection, hiring, leadership, organizational politics, and monetization timing. The retrospective argues that Facebook and Bebo benefited from narrowing their initial audiences, protecting site speed, using proven scaling approaches, and delaying revenue work until the product and network were stronger. It is an operator's comparative account rather than independent evidence that these choices alone explain the companies' different outcomes.

## Key Claims
- Early social products benefit from serving one coherent demographic first: Facebook began with Harvard and then colleges, while Bebo targeted students in six English-speaking countries and Friendster launched broadly.
- Product teams should ship and test small features quickly while refusing expensive requests that do not strengthen the core experience; Friendster's early instant-messaging effort is offered as a costly counterexample.
- Site speed is a product constraint, not merely an infrastructure concern: Friendster's six-degree calculations and database-heavy homepage reportedly made the service painfully slow and blocked further product work.
- Fast-growing startups should prefer proven technology and simple scaling paths when experimental infrastructure would add reliability risk; the account contrasts Friendster's bleeding-edge choices with Facebook's server-per-university approach and Bebo's conservative stack.
- Virality requires dedicated product attention, repeated A/B testing, and loops users value; Scheinman credits Facebook's specialized growth work and Bebo's team-wide experimentation rather than treating spread as accidental word of mouth.
- Hiring quality, stable founder leadership, and cooperation between product and engineering preserve execution speed; Friendster's rapid hiring, three CEOs in two years, and team conflict are presented as failures on these dimensions.
- Consumer social networks may rationally delay monetization until user growth and engagement are strong, but the source explicitly says this is not the right rule for every startup.

## Key Quotes
> "Speed to execution is a critical success factor for a start-up." - on frequent, bounded product experiments.

> "It can't be someone's part-time job" - on giving viral growth explicit ownership.

## Contradictions
- The recommendation to delay revenue work is deliberately source-scoped. It applies to rapidly growing social networks in Scheinman's account and does not override evidence that other startups need early willingness-to-pay or default-alive tests.
- The praise of founder continuity does not establish that founding CEOs are always superior to professional managers; it is a retrospective comparison involving three companies and leaves governance quality, succession needs, and alternative explanations unmeasured.
- Claims about team size, page views, growth staffing, company decisions, and causal lessons come from Scheinman's recollection and are not independently substantiated in the supplied document.
