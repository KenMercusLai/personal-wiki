## Summary
Tim Stenovec reports [[MarcLore]]'s case for using visible, standardized compensation at [[JetCom]] to support trust, fairness, and employee challenge rather than individual salary bargaining. The reported system combined 13 salary levels, level-standardized equity, no bonuses, and promotion-only raises, but the article provides Lore's assessment rather than comparative employee, retention, productivity, or pay-equity evidence.

## Key Claims
- [[SalaryTransparency]] at Jet meant employees could see one another's pay and challenge decisions they considered unfair.
- Jet used 13 salary levels ranging from $40,000 for entry-level employees to $300,000 for executives; pay increases required promotion to another level.
- Each full-time employee received equity, and people at the same salary level received the same equity amount.
- The company paid no bonuses and did not counter outside job offers with individually negotiated raises.
- Lore argued that opaque, negotiation-sensitive pay lets managers allocate limited raise budgets according to who complains or threatens to leave, undermining trust when differences later become visible.
- Jet reportedly had 266 full-time employees at the article's snapshot, but the source gives no workforce composition, level-assignment method, demographic pay audit, employee testimony, retention data, or outcome comparison.
- The single embedded image was inspected and omitted as a non-evidentiary office photograph showing a Jet-branded mug and an employee at a laptop.

## Key Quotes
> "There's mutual trust. There are no games. Everything's fair. And if something's not fair, you know everything so you can call someone out on it." - Lore on the culture he associated with early-stage startups.

> "Because you feel empowered. You're like 'I know what everyone's making, and then you're open and free to talk to your manager and say 'hey listen, this doesn't seem fair.'" - Lore on the intended effect of visible salaries.
