## Summary
[[TomTunguz]] argues that a B2B software sale depends on an individual champion who puts reputation at risk and is more likely to advocate when product success advances goals recognized by the employer. [[InternalChampionSelling]] therefore aligns the product's organizational value with the buyer's incentives, while treating absent ownership or an unfavorable risk-reward balance as a qualification signal. The essay offers a useful stakeholder lens but no evidence that promotion is the universal motive, that the proposed ten-times threshold predicts adoption, or that buyer career framing improves sales outcomes.

## Key Claims
- Corporate software purchases are made or advanced by individuals whose reputations can become attached to the decision.
- A salesperson can make value concrete by connecting product outcomes to the internal champion's measured goals, recognition, and career prospects.
- Alignment between product value and buyer incentives can create urgency, while a weak personal or organizational payoff can leave a product without a willing champion.
- Product research should ask whether expected upside is large enough to justify the advocate's implementation, reliability, and reputational risk.
- The article presents software that is roughly ten times better than its replacement as a rule of thumb for overcoming adoption risk.
- Questions about goals, priorities, evaluation, and culture help reveal how a prospective champion is rewarded.

## Key Quotes
> "Salespeople are in the business of selling promotions." - the article's shorthand for aligning a software purchase with the advocate's career incentives.

> "Behind the logo on the lead list is a person." - on treating an account as an individual decision and reputation context.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction with existing wiki content was found; the source adds an individual-incentive layer to the existing sales, SaaS, and organizational-risk material.
- Promotion is not a universal or necessarily dominant buying motive. Champions may optimize for team outcomes, mission, compliance, reliability, cost, peer welfare, professional duty, job security, or avoidance of blame, and their incentives can conflict with the organization's interests.
- The ten-times threshold is undefined and unsupported here: the essay supplies no baseline, measurement method, adoption sample, deal outcomes, or comparison showing that a particular improvement offsets implementation and reputational risk.
- The article does not measure promotion, pipeline, conversion, cycle time, retention, deployment success, or whether questions about internal rewards improve sales performance.
- Career alignment can illuminate legitimate value, but using personal ambition to bypass procurement, exaggerate outcomes, or transfer hidden risk would be manipulation rather than evidence-based selling.
