## Summary
[[ScottBelsky]] argues that employees and candidates at late-stage private companies should not value equity by multiplying their share count by the latest headline valuation. They should instead investigate the fully diluted denominator, liquidation preferences, debt, runway, financing strategy, audited financials, and plausible paths to liquidity because a celebrated acquisition can still leave common shareholders with little. The practical proposal is a scenario-based payout table showing what an employee’s options would return across several sale or IPO values.

## Key Claims
- A high private-company valuation can conceal investor protections that preserve the headline price while shifting downside onto earlier common shareholders, including employees.
- Share count and the last-round valuation are insufficient to value an employee grant without the fully diluted denominator and the company’s payout waterfall.
- Liquidation preferences determine who is paid first and can make common equity worthless in a moderate exit, especially when investors receive more than a standard 1x non-participating preference.
- Limited [[StartupRunway]] can push a company toward financing with unfavorable preferences, while debt creates another senior claim on exit proceeds.
- Employees should assess whether leadership would accept a lower valuation or instead protect unicorn status through a “dirty” round whose terms impair common-stock value.
- A credible liquidity path matters: companies that plan to remain private may need employee secondary sales, while companies that raise hundreds of millions usually need a large acquisition or public offering to return capital.
- Audited financials and scenario-based option payouts can make the relationship between company outcomes, financing terms, and employee proceeds more concrete.

## Key Quotes
> “It’s like negotiating your salary without specifying the currency you’re being paid in.” - on asking for more shares without understanding their economic context.

> “The value of your shares can go up or down, but liquidation preferences stick around.” - on durable financing terms beneath a changing valuation.

## Contradictions
- Qualifies valuation-based descriptions of employee upside: a rising last-round valuation does not show what common shareholders receive after preferences and debt.
- Qualifies secondary-sale optimism because access may be selective, culturally sensitive, or unavailable even when founders or some employees obtain liquidity.
- The article is a 2017 practitioner argument, not a quantified study of unicorn employee outcomes. Its predicted wave of high-profile low-payout exits is illustrative, and the source does not provide a cap-table example, tax analysis, legal guidance, or evidence about how often candidates can obtain the requested information.
