## Summary
Lisa M. Krieger reports that [[AnneWojcicki]] used public pressure and personal connections to help an uninsured, critically ill friend move from [[SantaClaraValleyMedicalCenter]] to [[StanfordMedicalCenter]] for advanced care. The case shows how payment assurance, delayed access to medical records, specialist discovery, and system complexity can compound into [[HealthcareAccessBarriers]], while emergency insurance, legal and counseling help, improvised record capture, and prominent [[PatientAdvocacy]] can partially overcome them. Its resolution is a single attributed account rather than evidence that publicity produces fair or repeatable access.

## Key Claims
- Stanford reportedly required a $1 million deposit before evaluating the uninsured patient, while explaining generally that complex procedures may require assurance of funding for follow-on care.
- The family was reportedly told that obtaining records from Valley Medical would take five to ten business days, delaying its search for a second opinion and suitable specialist.
- Wojcicki's public Twitter campaign drew help from Bay Area Legal Aid and Covered California counselors; Hospital Presumptive Eligibility reportedly supplied temporary coverage, after which Stanford accepted the patient.
- The family photographed available lab values and clinical information by phone while Wojcicki searched for clinicians who might offer advanced treatment.
- The episode illustrates unequal advocacy capacity: prominent networks and public pressure helped this patient, but the quoted advocates argue that access should not depend on fame or social-media shaming.
- Wojcicki proposed broader support for insurance counselors and legal advisers, easier second opinions and specialist navigation, and a less complex healthcare system.

## Key Quotes
> "Everyone needs an advocate, but not everyone has one." - Patricia Knight on the unequal availability of advocacy power.

> "Good health care comes to those who complain." - Wojcicki's deliberately critical summary of the episode.

## Connections
- [[AnneWojcicki]] - organized public and private advocacy for her critically ill friend.
- [[StanfordMedicalCenter]] - destination for the second opinion and advanced treatment after insurance was arranged.
- [[SantaClaraValleyMedicalCenter]] - initial treating hospital criticized for the reported delay in releasing records.
- [[23andMe]] - company whose founder's public standing amplified the campaign.
- [[PatientAdvocacy]] - case of escalation, navigation, information gathering, and public pressure on a patient's behalf.
- [[HealthcareAccessBarriers]] - payment, records, specialist discovery, and complexity formed interacting obstacles to care.

## Contradictions
- The hospitals did not address the individual case because of patient-privacy rules, so the deposit, record-delay, diagnosis, treatment options, and causal effect of Wojcicki's intervention remain reported through Wojcicki and the patient's side rather than independently reconciled.
- Valley Medical said its electronic records travel between doctors, which sits in tension with the family's reported five-to-ten-business-day wait but does not establish whether patient release and clinician transfer followed the same process.
- Stanford's general explanation frames financial assurance as continuity-of-care planning after complex procedures; the article frames the reported deposit as refusal based on ability to pay. The source does not provide the institution's case-specific policy or decision record.
