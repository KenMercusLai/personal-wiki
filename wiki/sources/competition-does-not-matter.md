## Summary
This founder-advice essay argues that competition is usually a poor reason to reject a self-funded SaaS side project. It presents existing competitors as evidence of educated demand, customer preference differences as openings for alternatives, and focused differentiation plus close customer service as ways to carve out a viable segment. Its Facebook, Google, Slack, DuckDuckGo, and Drip examples illustrate possibility rather than establish comparative odds, so the advice is best read as a rebuttal to automatic market avoidance rather than proof that competitive conditions never matter.

## Key Claims
- A self-funded SaaS side project can build a durable customer base by solving requested problems and serving customers well even when alternatives already exist.
- Interface and product preferences vary, so dissatisfaction with an incumbent can create a reachable niche for a different implementation.
- Existing competition can reduce market-education work because prospective customers already understand the product category.
- A new entrant still needs a marketing angle or meaningful differentiation that places it in a distinct segment rather than on the same comparison layer as incumbents.
- The essay cites Facebook, Google, Slack, DuckDuckGo, and Drip as examples of later entrants succeeding in established categories.
- Long-term commitment and continuous customer contact are presented as stronger practical constraints than the mere existence of competitors.

## Key Quotes
> "competition is irrelevant and should be ignored 99% of the time." - the essay's deliberately broad thesis for self-funded SaaS side projects.

> "The trick is to make sure you are not on the same layer as anyone else." - on using segmentation and differentiation to avoid direct comparison.

## Contradictions
- The headline claim overstates the evidence: competition can also raise acquisition costs, strengthen network effects, constrain distribution, compress prices, or make switching uneconomic, none of which the essay evaluates.
- The success stories are selected anecdotes without base rates, counterexamples, causal comparisons, or evidence that the cited companies won because they ignored competition.
- The asserted three-year business-building dip and the claim that determination will produce customers are not supported with measurements.
