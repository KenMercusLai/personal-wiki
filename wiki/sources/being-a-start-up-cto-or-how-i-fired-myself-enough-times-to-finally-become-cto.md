## Summary
[[JoshuaWohle]] recounts how his role at [[SuperAwesome]] changed from restoring acquired physical-server infrastructure and recruiting trusted friends into product management, company-wide product vision, strategic customer work, and anticipation of future scaling constraints. His central lesson is that a startup CTO is not a fixed job: growth repeatedly requires the technical founder to hand operational ownership to stronger specialists, including a Chief Architect, Head of Engineering, and Chief Product Officer.

## Key Claims
- An early startup CTO may begin as the person keeping systems alive, learning inherited infrastructure under pressure, and refactoring to reduce repeat failures.
- Early technical recruiting can depend on personal trust and founder-led vision selling before the company has a mature hiring process or employer reputation.
- As building replaces survival work, the CTO may temporarily absorb product-management responsibilities because prioritization, rationale, and stakeholder alignment become central.
- Technical and organizational growth can make the founder less qualified or less available to own architecture, engineering management, or the full product portfolio.
- Hiring a Chief Architect, Head of Engineering, and Chief Product Officer let Wöhle transfer distinct responsibilities to people with deeper focus and expertise.
- [[StartupCTORoleEvolution]] is continuous rather than a one-time promotion: Wöhle says the role changed materially every six to twelve months as company constraints moved.
- Effective founder leadership can require relinquishing control, accepting that others are better placed to own former responsibilities, and looking ahead to the next bottleneck.

## Key Quotes
> "Being a start-up CTO is not a defined role." - Wöhle's conclusion after describing eight stages.

> "If the company is scaling, your role is constantly changing" - on why a startup CTO's responsibilities cannot remain fixed.
