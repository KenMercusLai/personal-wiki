## Summary
Restaurant consultant Brandon O'Dell argues that [[Groupon]]'s daily-deal model can turn customer acquisition into a loss-making transaction for restaurants. His worked example says a restaurant offering $40 of food through a $20 voucher receives only $9.30 after Groupon's share and stated processing fees, less than the estimated $10–$16 food cost before labor, supplies, displaced full-price demand, and other expenses. He further argues that deal-seeking customers may remain loyal to the discount platform rather than the restaurant, but the article supplies no campaign cohorts or independent outcome data to establish that generalization.

## Key Claims
- A 50%-off voucher coupled with a roughly 50% platform share can leave a restaurant with less cash than the direct food cost of fulfilling the discounted order.
- The article's example yields a claimed loss of $0.70–$6.70 per redeemed voucher before incremental labor, paper goods, cleaning supplies, and opportunity cost.
- Treating the transaction loss as marketing spend makes daily deals potentially expensive customer acquisition whose value depends on incremental visits, add-on spending, breakage, and profitable repeat behavior.
- The author argues that discount shoppers are loyal to [[Groupon]] rather than individual restaurants and may move to the next deal instead of returning at full price.
- The article predicts that merchant harm and learning will exhaust Groupon's domestic supply of willing businesses, forcing it toward less-informed markets.
- Reader comments add anecdotes about rejected vouchers, restaurant distress, weak return visits, damaging reviews, and merchants declining Groupon offers.

## Key Quotes
> "Groupon was a bad idea from the start." — Brandon O'Dell's thesis

> "Ultimately, nobody wins with Groupon, except the people who buy the Groupons." — conclusion about the parties' incentives
