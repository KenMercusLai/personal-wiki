## Summary
[[NnamdiOkike]] and [[AaronHoliday]] argue that venture capital's power-law return distribution makes missed breakout investments more consequential than ordinary failed bets. Writing from [[645Ventures]], they identify seven recurring [[VentureCapitalBlindSpots]]: misreading founders, rejecting price, mistaking the investor for the target customer, following the group, underestimating market size, assuming a market is already won, and missing behavioral or technical change. Their cases suggest replacing intuition alone with prepared market theses, diverse perspectives, direct customer evidence, independent judgment, expansion analysis, and attention to changing adoption curves.

## Key Claims
- Venture returns are concentrated enough that mistakes of omission - failing to invest in rare exceptional outcomes - can matter more than a high rate of failed investments.
- Founder pattern matching, [[Homophily]], homogeneous decision groups, and network-dependent deal flow can exclude capable founders who do not resemble historical winners.
- A perceived high entry price should be weighed against the company's maximum plausible outcome and probability of reaching it, rather than treated as an independent reason to reject an otherwise exceptional opportunity.
- Investors who substitute their own preferences or those of socially similar people for target-customer evidence can miss broad demand, especially in consumer categories serving different incomes, genders, geographies, or lifestyles.
- Consensus inside and across firms can turn uncertainty into herd behavior, causing many investors to reject unfamiliar opportunities and then pursue them together once a prestigious firm validates the deal.
- Small-looking markets may be sleeper markets with underestimated existing demand or Trojan-horse markets whose initial niche enables expansion into much larger customer groups.
- Apparently mature or repeatedly failed categories can reopen when product innovation, technology economics, customer preferences, or adoption behavior changes.

## Key Quotes
> "Venture investing is all about investing in massive winners, not optimizing for failure rate." - on why omission errors dominate the article's analysis.

> "How large could this company become if things go right?" - the proposed question for escaping the valuation trap.

## Contradictions
- Qualifies [[StartupValuation]]: a high entry valuation still raises the outcome required for a return, but this source argues that price alone can obscure an exceptional team's product and market upside.
- Extends [[MarketSizing]] while preserving uncertainty: sleeper and Trojan-horse framing can reveal underestimated demand or expansion, but the article offers successful retrospective examples rather than a forecasting test that distinguishes them from markets that remain small.
- The reported loss rates, return concentration, valuations, revenues, customer counts, and market shares are historical figures repeated from cited third-party material; the captured article does not provide enough method or current evidence to independently validate them.
