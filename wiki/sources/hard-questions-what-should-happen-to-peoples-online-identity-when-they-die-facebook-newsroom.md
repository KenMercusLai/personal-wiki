## Summary
[[MonikaBickert]] combines a first-person account of grief with [[Facebook]]'s 2017 policy for accounts whose owners have died. The article frames [[PosthumousAccountGovernance]] as a conflict among the deceased person's known or unknown wishes, bereaved people's competing needs, third-party message privacy, and laws that may not map ordinary inheritance rules cleanly onto digital accounts.

Facebook's stated default was to memorialize the account without changing its existing audience, label the profile “Remembering,” and block new logins. Users could instead request deletion or appoint a legacy contact with limited management and archive powers, while private messages remained inaccessible to that contact.

![Illustration of a Facebook memorialized profile labeled Remembering](../../wiki-assets/hard-questions-what-should-happen-to-peoples-online-identity-when-they-die-facebook-newsroom/memorialized-facebook-profile.png)

## Key Claims
- Digital traces can support grief through continued connection and shared memories, but automated reminders, resurfaced posts, and context-specific images can also intensify loss.
- When a deceased user's wishes are unknown, Facebook said it preserves the account as left, adds a memorial label, blocks new logins, and maintains the prior visibility of existing material.
- Users could direct Facebook to delete their account after death, and next of kin could also request deletion when they represented that this matched the deceased person's preference.
- A legacy contact could manage selected memorial features, accept friend requests, pin a post, request deletion, and—if authorized in advance—download shared photos, posts, and profile information.
- A legacy contact could not impersonate the deceased or read private messages, separating stewardship of a memorial surface from succession to the person's identity and confidential communications.
- Family interests can conflict with one another and with the privacy expectations of living correspondents, so bereavement alone does not determine who should control an account or its messages.
- The article says inheritance law often gave incomplete guidance for digital assets and that US communications law could prevent disclosure based only on family consent.

## Key Quotes
> “we try to leave the account exactly as that person left it” — Facebook's default when the person's wishes are unknown.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction with existing wiki claims was found. The source extends Facebook's persistent-identity profile from self-presentation during life to account governance after death.
- The article is a first-party Facebook policy explanation from 2017, not independent evidence that the described processes consistently resolved disputes or remain current.
- Memorialization preserves prior visibility but cannot prove that preservation was the user's preferred outcome; deletion requested by next of kin likewise depends on an unverifiable account of the deceased person's wishes.
- The recovered lead image directly depicts the “Remembering” memorial state and was retained. Two recovered divider illustrations—a framed portrait with a flower and a lock over messages—repeat the adjacent headings without adding evidence and were omitted.
