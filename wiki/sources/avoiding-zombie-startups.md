## Summary
[[MathiasPastor]] argues that prospective startup employees should investigate a company's momentum and incentives with investor-like care because remaining at a stagnant [[ZombieStartup]] consumes a scarce chance to try a stronger opportunity. His five-part screen looks beyond money raised, awards, and publicity to investor quality, demonstrated execution and sales, team equity incentives, the reason for each hire, and founders' willingness to share the real state of the business.

## Key Claims
- A [[ZombieStartup]] can retain startup branding and financing while moving slowly, remaining small, and surviving on weakly discriminating capital rather than customer-backed momentum.
- Fundraising is only a partial signal; candidates should investigate who supplied the money, their record and incentives, and whether the company has actually launched a product.
- Awards, pitch competitions, and press coverage can become negative signals when founders optimize for external validation instead of building, selling, and serving customers.
- Employee equity should be broad and meaningful enough to keep the team committed through setbacks, not treated solely as the candidate's individual upside.
- A healthy early-stage hire usually delegates work the founders understand but can no longer fit into their time; hiring someone to discover a desperate "silver bullet" invites micromanagement and blame.
- Internal transparency is a career-risk control because employees need enough business information to recognize when the company's prospects have materially deteriorated.

## Key Quotes
> "Always ask yourself why they are hiring." — the article's test for delegation versus desperation.

> "Companies can have different values but transparency is non-negotiable." — the article's minimum cultural requirement for startup employees.

## Contradictions
- The article's broad suspicion of public money, inexperienced investors, awards, and publicity qualifies their value as signals but does not establish that any one of them causes stagnation. A small or slow-growing company may also be healthy by design rather than a failed venture-style startup.
