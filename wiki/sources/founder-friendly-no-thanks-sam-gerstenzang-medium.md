## Summary
[[SamGerstenzang]] rejects “founder friendly” when it means a board will support a founder regardless of the company's needs or the founder's conduct. He argues that founder operating knowledge and market intuition deserve substantial weight, but that board members contribute cross-company pattern recognition, standards for executive excellence, and a duty to make difficult governance decisions. The resulting model separates a board's evaluative role from a non-board investor's coaching role and treats company-building, not founder status, as the governing objective.

## Key Claims
- “Founder friendly” can mutate from protection against investor abuse into unconditional support for a founder, weakening board accountability.
- A founder who is effective from idea through product-market fit may not be the right CEO for incentive design, organizational scaling, or multi-product expansion.
- Founder operating proximity supplies detailed information, repeated market experience, and valuable intuition that boards usually lack.
- Experienced directors can contribute cross-company pattern recognition, role-level standards, effort, and moral independence, but should not behave as either passive friends or operational micromanagers.
- Coaching and formal oversight create different relationships: an investor can act as a founder's confidential adviser or hold a board seat with evaluative duties, but combining both can create tension.
- Founder ego and board timidity can jointly prevent a company from reaching its potential, harming employees and customers as well as investors and founders.

## Key Quotes
> “We're here to build companies, not egos.” — Gerstenzang's standard for founder and board decisions.

> “I can grade you, or I can teach you, but I can't do both.” — the role conflict he uses to distinguish oversight from coaching.

## Contradictions
- The essay rejects unconditional founder support, not founder autonomy. It explicitly says boards usually have less operating information and should not push a founder-CEO around.
- Its company-centered standard creates a real tension with founder-protective governance: the source does not specify who defines the company's interests, what evidence justifies intervention, or how boards should manage investor conflicts and unequal power.
- The scaling and succession claims are normative practitioner arguments without comparative company data, decision criteria, or evidence that replacing founders produces better outcomes.
- The Plaxo, Facebook, Benchmark, and Uber references are brief historical interpretations rather than documented case studies; the source says it does not know what happened at Plaxo and does not establish the counterfactual that earlier Benchmark action would have improved Uber.
