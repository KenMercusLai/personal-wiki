## Summary
This Inc. article uses [[Wistia]] and [[Buffer]] to show how profitable or potentially profitable venture-backed companies can negotiate share repurchases when founders no longer want to optimize for venture-scale growth and liquidity. Wistia borrowed $17.3 million, offered investors a 10x return, regained full control, and replaced employee options with profit sharing; Buffer used cash flow to buy roughly two-thirds of its Series A investors' holdings for about $3.3 million and a reported 40% return. The cases expose a structural mismatch between a good founder-owned operating business and the outlier returns a venture fund needs, while also showing that a buyout depends on financing capacity, negotiated pricing, prior expectations, and investor willingness.

## Key Claims
- [[Wistia]]'s founders concluded that a growth-at-all-costs strategy had diverted attention from the core product, produced losses, and damaged a previously valued company culture.
- After receiving three acquisition offers in 2017, Wistia instead borrowed $17.3 million and offered investors a 10x return to repurchase their shares and regain full control.
- Wistia replaced employee stock options with [[EmployeeProfitSharing]], making operating improvements such as higher gross margin more directly and regularly legible to employees.
- [[Buffer]] could fund its initial buyback from cash flow because Series A investors owned only 6.2%, slower growth could improve profitability, and an original liquidity clause supplied a negotiation baseline.
- Buffer spent about $3.3 million in July 2018 to buy roughly two-thirds of its Series A investors' holdings, which the article says produced about a 40% return.
- [[PrivateCompanyInvestorBuyout]] can resolve some founder-investor misalignment, but debt service, cash reserves, valuation, contractual rights, and holdout investors constrain who can use it.
- [[VentureCapitalFundStructure]] makes a stable tens-of-millions-revenue company or a $25 million exit potentially valuable to a founder but too small to matter to a portfolio seeking rare, very large multiples.

## Key Quotes
> "Sell as much or as little as you want, but we are not planning for another liquidity event." - [[ChrisSavage]] on setting expectations for Wistia's investor buyout.

> "You're in a very different kind of math class at that point." - Bryce Roberts on moving from venture-return arithmetic to negotiated company and founder economics.

> "VC is wrong for 99 percent of companies." - Rand Fishkin on the narrow fit between venture capital and company outcomes.

## Contradictions
- The Wistia account supports direct investor-return misalignment, while [[getting-out-of-the-startup-rat-race-baremetrics]] says Baremetrics' investors did not demand its aggressive spending; outside capital can create capacity and structural expectations without being the proximate source of every growth decision.
- Buybacks are presented through successful negotiations and do not establish how often companies lack the cash flow, borrowing capacity, contractual room, or investor consent needed to complete one.
- The article reports company figures and founder recollections but supplies no financial statements, transaction documents, lender terms, investor interviews, or post-buyout performance data.
- The sole local image was opened and omitted because it is a generic stock photograph of a person viewing dashboards, not evidence about either company or transaction.
