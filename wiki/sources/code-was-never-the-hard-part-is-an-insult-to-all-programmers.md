## Summary
This practitioner essay rejects the AI-era slogan that coding was never difficult and argues that implementation craft and understanding why software should exist are both necessary. It presents programming as skilled work shaped by complexity, maintenance, abstraction, and technological change, while urging senior developers to learn adjacent product and business disciplines and junior developers to deepen their systems fundamentals. Its durable recommendation is to retain understanding, judgment, empathy, taste, and responsibility while adapting to AI-assisted development, but its labor-market comparisons and portraits of programmers and product roles are rhetorical rather than empirical. The supplied Markdown contains no effective image references.

## Key Claims
- Coding is a learned craft requiring skill, patience, attention to detail, experience, and judgment; historical demand, training, specialist books, exceptional practitioners, burnout, and persistent bugs are offered as signs that implementation is not trivial.
- Discovering what to build, understanding customers, aligning stakeholders, and creating good code are complementary forms of difficult work rather than substitutes in which one makes the other easy.
- [[ProductMindedEngineering]] should combine a deep understanding of the system with a deep understanding of why it is being built, without pretending every developer already excels at customer empathy or product work.
- Software complexity, maintenance, abstraction growth, changing technology, ambiguous user needs, customer-user tension, business constraints, and hype will persist even as AI changes implementation work.
- Developers should respond to AI-era change with curiosity and criticism rather than either dismissing code as easy or treating human-written code as intrinsically unautomatable art.
- Senior developers can broaden their leverage by learning user experience, customer interviews, and business strategy, while junior developers should still study algorithms, data structures, pointers, recursion, memory hierarchy, networks, and HTTP.
- [[HumanCodeResponsibility]] includes refusing to outsource understanding, judgment, empathy, taste, and accountability to AI even when implementation tools change.

## Key Quotes
> "A deep understanding of the system we're building, together with a deep understanding of why we're building it." - on joining implementation and product understanding.

> "Be equal parts curious and critical about the new stuff." - on adapting without either hype or denial.

> "Don't outsource your understanding, judgement, empathy and taste to AI." - on the human boundary around AI-assisted work.

## Contradictions
- The article directly rejects slogans that coding is easy or that implementation was never the hard part, qualifying automation-positive accounts that shift most scarce work toward deciding, specifying, and reviewing.
- Its positive conclusion aligns with existing wiki material: faster code generation does not remove product judgment, verification, maintenance, understanding, or responsibility.
- Labor demand, salary, interviews, books, education, burnout, and bugs do not independently prove which parts of software work are intrinsically difficult; institutions, scarcity, incentives, coordination, and poor processes can also produce them.
- The portrayals of product managers, customer-facing roles, and programmers rely on rhetorical questions and caricatures rather than representative evidence, and the author explicitly retracts one mental-health joke as excessive.
- Claims about what software, users, customers, and technology will "always" do are forecasts from one practitioner essay, not timeless or measured laws.
