## Summary
This HackerNoon essay argues that software schedules become more credible when engineers decompose a project into phases, estimate each phase, and expose the reasoning to several codebase-familiar peers. It connects estimation error to engineering cost and opportunity cost, while presenting repeated practice as a way for product managers and non-technical founders to improve their own delivery judgment. The method is practical but uncalibrated: the article provides one salary-cost illustration rather than measured evidence that decomposition or group review improves forecast accuracy.

## Key Claims
- A single large duration estimate conceals uncertainty and is likely to be wrong.
- Estimation error has direct labor cost and opportunity cost because delayed work keeps engineers from other priorities.
- Engineers can make a schedule more credible by dividing a project into phases and estimating each phase separately.
- Several engineers who know the codebase should review the phase plan, discuss it openly, and surface missing work, likely blockers, and help needs.
- Product managers and non-technical founders can use structured engineering discussion to reason about development time without pretending to possess implementation expertise.
- Repeated participation in estimation can improve a product manager's intuitive judgment about how long software work may take.

## Key Quotes
> "how does that sound?" - the open-ended prompt recommended for peer review of a phase-by-phase estimate.
