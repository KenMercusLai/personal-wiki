## Summary
[[MarkLane]] argues that a programmer's Bay Area decision should compare more than salary and rent: renting may leave a cash-pay advantage over Seattle, while homeownership, California tax, family needs, lifestyle, career progression, and the uncertain value of equity can reverse or amplify the result. The article attributes a longer-run advantage to [[SiliconValley]]'s concentration of employers, investors, high-growth companies, and career mobility, but its 2016 figures are historical and its publisher, [[Triplebyte]], benefited from placing engineers at Bay Area companies. Three evidence-bearing remote charts were referenced, but their host could not be resolved and the images could not be opened or retained; chart-level conclusions here are limited to values explicitly stated in the prose.

## Key Claims
- BLS, Indeed, Glassdoor, and Triplebyte figures were interpreted as a roughly $15,000-$33,000 annual cash-salary premium for engineers at top Bay Area technology companies relative to Seattle, before tax differences.
- Zillow's August 2016 reports placed Bay Area median rent roughly $17,000-$18,000 per year above Seattle, so the estimated salary premium could cover rent for a renter but not necessarily create a large after-tax gain.
- Median 2016 home values of $807,800 in the San Francisco metro and $948,600 in San Jose, versus $394,600 in Seattle, made ownership and family-sized housing a substantially different comparison from renting.
- The article cites a reported destination-market wage premium for engineers leaving San Francisco and much larger senior total-compensation differences between Google or Facebook and Amazon or Microsoft as evidence of possible career progression benefits.
- Startup equity remains high variance and usually illiquid, but a denser startup market may give candidates more opportunities to select for growth, revenue, market size, and trajectory.
- [[GeographicTechCareerTradeoffs]] depend on career stage, ownership goals, household and lifestyle constraints, taxes, frugality, risk tolerance, and access to unusually strong companies rather than on one universal best city.

## Key Quotes
> "This growth creates opportunity." - the article's core explanation for the Bay Area premium.

> "The salaries here do cover the higher cost of living" - the author's qualified bottom line for renters with strong offers.

## Contradictions
- The source qualifies rather than directly contradicts [[andrew-chen-how-to-break-into-silicon-valley]]: it supports an ecosystem career premium while making housing tenure, tax, family, and lifestyle constraints central to whether relocation is worthwhile.
- The article's recommendation has an unresolved incentive conflict because Triplebyte helped candidates obtain Bay Area jobs. Its salary sources use different populations and methods, the article does not provide a complete after-tax household budget, and company valuations or equity outcomes are not employee wealth.
