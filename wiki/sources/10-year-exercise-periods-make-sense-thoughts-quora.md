## Summary
[[AdamDAngelo]] argues that [[Quora]]'s move to 10-year stock-option exercise periods is fairer and more market-rational than the common 90-day post-employment window. Responding to [[ScottKupor]] and [[AndreessenHorowitz]], the article says extended exercise windows do not meaningfully transfer wealth away from current employees when compensation is set by current market value and option pools can expand. It frames [[ExtendedStockOptionExerciseWindow]] policy as a transparency and labor-market issue: employees should understand whether leaving before an IPO means losing already vested equity, especially when turnover is involuntary.

## Key Claims
- [[Quora]] was an early private-company adopter of 10-year stock-option exercise periods, and the policy later influenced other startups and [[YCombinator]] recommendations.
- Companies should be free to choose compensation structures, but candidates need clear disclosure about equity exercise rules and their consequences.
- [[AdamDAngelo]] rejects the claim that former employees retaining options automatically reduces compensation available to future employees, because [[Quora]] grants equity according to current market compensation rather than recovered-share accounting.
- A 90-day post-employment exercise window can operate as a hidden penalty on employees who leave before an IPO, including people who leave for family, performance, role-change, or other involuntary reasons.
- In a transparent labor market, startups using harsh exercise windows would need to compete openly against companies offering more employee-protective equity terms.

## Key Quotes
> "Everyone agreed to the stock vesting schedule explicitly" - context for rejecting hidden penalties on employees who leave after vesting.

> "Not all turnover is voluntary" - section framing exercise-window risk for employees who do not freely choose to leave.
