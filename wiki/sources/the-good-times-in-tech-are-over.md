## Summary
[[SeanGoedecke]] argues that the 2010s bargain between technology companies and software engineers depended on cheap capital, aggressive growth, and unusually broad corporate ambition. He interprets the post-2022 shift toward layoffs, profitability, and concentrated executive priorities as a [[TechEmploymentRegimeShift]], with the COVID hiring boom as an accelerant rather than the root cause and contemporary AI displacement as an unsupported explanation for the change.

## Key Claims
- Near-zero interest rates made speculative technology investment, rapid hiring, high compensation, employee perks, and low-probability initiatives comparatively easy to fund even without current profit.
- Higher rates reversed those incentives by raising the value of profitability and focus, while the end of COVID-era demand added a nearer-term reason to unwind hiring.
- The author rejects AI job substitution as a current cause of the 2023–2025 software-engineering labor shift, while allowing that it could become one later.
- Tighter capital makes companies concentrate resources on fewer executive priorities and defund open source, developer experience, platform expansion, and other work that lacks a direct strategic case.
- Engineers may legitimately retain interests that conflict with their employer's, but the author warns that pursuing work outside executive priorities now creates more employment risk, especially for junior or otherwise vulnerable staff.
- The new bargain makes company value more legible as progress against explicit leadership plans, but it trades away some compensation, autonomy, security, and breadth of experimentation.

## Key Quotes
> "Companies now are actually trying to focus." - Goedecke's description of the post-boom operating change.

> "Your interests now conflict with your company's interests." - the article's warning that engineers should distinguish personal commitments from funded corporate priorities.

## Contradictions
- The essay offers a clear macroeconomic mechanism but no hiring series, investment data, profitability analysis, company comparison, or causal identification showing that interest rates are sufficient to explain the sector-wide change.
- Its contrast between near-zero and roughly 5% rates compresses differences among policy rates, borrowing costs, public markets, company stages, and countries; the conclusion is centered on US technology firms despite the author's Australian perspective.
- COVID demand, pandemic over-hiring, rate changes, public-market repricing, management imitation, company maturation, and other forces may interact rather than divide cleanly into root cause and accelerant.
- The rejection of AI-driven layoffs is the author's contemporary assessment, not an empirical decomposition of layoff causes, and should not be projected beyond the article's March 2025 publication date.
- Defining company value solely as advancing executive plans describes a power and reward system, not proof that those plans maximize durable company value or excuse unsafe, illegal, deceptive, or professionally irresponsible work.
- The source generalizes across "tech companies" and software engineers without separating profitable incumbents, startups, contractors, countries, disciplines, seniority levels, or firms that continued to fund broad technical work.
