## Summary
[[BradEllis]] argues that large iPhones made the traditional top [[IOS]] navigation bar ergonomically weaker, because important controls drifted outside easy one-handed thumb reach. The article proposes [[ReachNavigation]]: put navigation and frequent actions near the lower, naturally reachable part of the screen, using sheets, drawers, floating controls, swipe gestures, and bottom placement while reserving harder-to-reach areas for dangerous actions. The inspected images reinforce the argument with thumb-zone diagrams, iOS swipe-back and Reachability examples, Apple Maps and Apple Music sheet layouts, app examples from [[Lyft]], [[PokemonGo]], Overcast, and [[Twitter]], and a Safari Apple Store mockup that moves browser controls toward the bottom.

## Key Claims
- Larger phones weaken top navigation bars because thumb travel to the top corners becomes physically awkward in one-handed use.
- [[ReachNavigation]] should move high-frequency navigation and actions toward the reachable bottom of the screen through sheets, exposed drawers, bottom controls, and broad swipe gestures.
- [[IOS]] already contained partial reach accommodations, including edge-swipe back navigation and Reachability, but Reachability is framed as a temporary workaround rather than a design solution.
- Apple's iOS 10-era Maps and [[AppleMusic]] redesigns are presented as evidence that Apple was already reducing dependence on persistent top navbars.
- Tab-bar apps should avoid putting important destination or mode controls in the top navbar; critical destinations should become tabs or live in the content area.
- Dangerous or high-consequence actions can be deliberately placed farther from the thumb to reduce accidental activation.

## Key Quotes
> "Better navigation is within reach." - closing design principle.

> "Prioritize placing buttons at the bottom of the screen." - implementation guidance.
