## Summary
[[MitchellHarper]] uses his reported experience pitching about 150 venture investors and raising $125 million for [[BigCommerce]] to argue that founders should select investors for a long post-close relationship, not merely accept available capital. His criteria join personal trust, operating experience, geographic flexibility, follow-on reserves, candid but restrained advice, consistent treatment, founder support, team-protective change-of-control terms, and simple financing documents. The essay also supplies a concrete terms boundary—1x non-participating preference as acceptable versus 2x participating preference as a warning—while leaving the performance effects of its checklist unmeasured.

## Key Claims
- Harper reports that roughly one in every 40 investor pitches produced a yes, enough for [[BigCommerce]] to raise $125 million across four rounds.
- Founders should treat investor selection as a five-to-ten-year partnership decision and walk away when pre-investment behavior signals an unacceptable working relationship.
- Relevant operating experience, geographic flexibility, follow-on reserves, direct candor, restrained unsolicited advice, pivot support, and consistent pre- and post-close conduct are proposed as signs of a good investor.
- Harper prefers investors who support the founder through missed targets and strategy changes, but his unqualified “back the CEO every time” rule conflicts with governance arguments for performance and conduct accountability.
- Founders should favor understandable, conventional documents and team-protective change-of-control provisions rather than treating complexity as unavoidable.
- The essay presents 1x non-participating liquidation preference as fair and 2x participating preference as a warning sign because senior payout rights materially change the economics beneath headline ownership.
- Investor capital availability does not make investors interchangeable because governance behavior, terms, reserves, and relationship quality persist after closing.

## Key Quotes
> “If we couldn't understand the term sheet without a lawyer, we didn't proceed” — Harper's screening rule for document complexity.

## Contradictions
- Harper's preference for investors who back the CEO “every time” conflicts with [[founder-friendly-no-thanks-sam-gerstenzang-medium]], which argues that directors should respect founder context but intervene when performance, stage fit, or conduct threatens the company. The sources do not clarify whether Harper means support through ordinary misses or unconditional loyalty under every circumstance.
- The claim that capital was cheap and everywhere is a January 2016 market judgment, not a durable property of fundraising conditions. The reported one-yes-per-roughly-40-pitches experience also shows that abundant aggregate capital did not make acceptance easy.
- This is one founder's retrospective checklist. It supplies no investor perspectives, contract set, failed-versus-successful investor comparison, portfolio outcomes, or evidence that operating experience, founder loyalty, simple documents, or social chemistry reliably predict company performance.
- Advice to reject any term sheet a founder cannot understand without a lawyer should not be read as advice to skip legal review; plain language and independent professional diligence solve different problems.
