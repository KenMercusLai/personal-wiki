## Summary
[[AnilDash]] argues that Twitter's 2016 problems were not a shortage of possible ideas but a failure to make product progress, safety commitments, success metrics, user segmentation, and developer-platform posture legible to the outside world. The essay tells [[JackDorsey]] that Twitter should ship meaningful features consistently, confront abuse as a platform threat, stop letting signup metrics define the company, give different user groups appropriate tools, and decide whether developers and bots are strategically welcome. The piece treats [[Twitter]] as culturally important infrastructure whose public narrative, safety model, and platform trust had become as important as individual product features.

## Key Claims
- Twitter needed to prove it could consistently ship meaningful features, because outside perception of product stagnation undermined trust in any broader turnaround.
- Abuse was a platform threat model problem, not just an individual blocking problem; organized harassment required public policy, product design, and enforcement attention.
- Twitter's investor story was distorted by overemphasis on flat signups rather than the platform's cultural role in creating and distributing public conversation.
- Twitter should serve new users, power users, creators, community managers, brands, and verified accounts with different tool surfaces instead of tying important controls to confused status categories.
- Twitter's developer relationship was damaged by API instability and neglect, but bots and connected devices still represented a missed opportunity for platform renewal.

## Key Quotes
> "So you have to ship." - on why public product cadence matters

> "Change the metrics, change the story" - on replacing signup fixation with cultural relevance

> "Decide if you give a damn about developers or not" - on Twitter's damaged API-platform relationship
