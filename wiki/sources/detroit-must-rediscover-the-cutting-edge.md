## Summary
This 2016 Automotive News column argues that Detroit automakers must stop treating innovation as annual model optimization and instead redesign vehicles and organizations around how customers actually move through daily, weekly, and seasonal life. Apps, ride-hailing, autonomous vehicles, software, sensors, and post-design reconfigurability are presented as forces shifting the business from ownership of one compromise vehicle toward convenient access to the right vehicle for a particular purpose. The proposed response combines [[CustomerLedProductDevelopment]], [[CrossFunctionalProductTeams]], [[ConnectedProductSystems]], and more modular vehicle architecture, while the failure of Sony, Philips, and Nokia to bridge internal divisions serves as an incumbent warning.

## Key Claims
- Groundbreaking incumbents study customers' daily lives closely and adapt their culture by combining previously separate disciplines.
- Most households buy one or two multipurpose cars and compromise around commuting, but shared services could make specialized vehicles economically useful at mass scale.
- Apps, mobility-on-demand services, and autonomous vehicles challenge a business model centered on individual car ownership.
- Automakers should design around the purpose of mobility and convenient use, not only optimize next year's models and brands.
- In-house teams should integrate vehicle hardware, sensors, software, and mobile software across divisional boundaries rather than rely only on suppliers, partnerships, or detached innovation labs.
- Automakers should reduce duplicative, weakly differentiated component work and invest more heavily in software and sensor systems.
- More modular vehicle architectures could allow a car to be reconfigured during its life, including between passenger and delivery uses.

## Key Quotes
> "The business model must become about convenient usage and purpose because that is what customers will care about." - on shifting from vehicle ownership to mobility access.

> "Automakers had better hope those people are working for them." - warning that mobility may be reinvented outside incumbent firms.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction was found. The column's 2016 forecast is strategic advocacy rather than measured evidence: it supplies no adoption, utilization, cost, margin, safety, or lifecycle data showing that shared specialized vehicles would become a mass market.
- Its use of Uber services as evidence for purpose-specific access is qualified by [[SubsidizedUnitEconomics]], which separates customer adoption from durable transportation economics.
- Its optimism about driverless mobility is qualified by [[AutonomousDrivingSafety]], which documents unresolved perception, handoff, social-coordination, and public-legitimacy problems.
