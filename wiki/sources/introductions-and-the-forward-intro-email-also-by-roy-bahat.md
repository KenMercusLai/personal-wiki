## Summary
[[RoyBahat]] describes a low-friction, opt-in introduction workflow in which the requester writes a self-contained email that the introducer can forward unchanged. The method divides work by comparative advantage: the requester explains who they are, what they want, and why the recipient may care, while the introducer adds relationship context and an independent endorsement before asking whether the recipient wants the connection.

## Key Claims
- A requester should explain the specific reason for the introduction and include enough context for the recipient to understand the person, organization, distinct value, and requested conversation without requiring the introducer to reconstruct the pitch.
- The email should be concise, sound like the requester, and arrive as a fresh message with a recipient-meaningful subject line.
- Each proposed introduction needs its own thread; combining recipients or replying inside an unrelated chain creates editing work, delay, and possible disclosure between competitors.
- The introducer should forward the request with a short personal judgment, seek the recipient's opt-in, and add the requester to the thread only after the recipient agrees.
- A forwardable request is preferable to ghostwriting in the introducer's voice, supplying a blurb that still needs editing, or sending a list of introductions for the introducer to disentangle.
- The request for an introduction can itself be written as the forwardable message, eliminating a separate preparation step.
- Small reductions in coordination cost compound when a person makes five to ten introductions a day.

## Key Quotes
> "Please write me an email I can forward to them." - Bahat's request for a ready-to-send introduction message.

> "People are the currency of the realm." - on why seemingly small introduction mechanics matter at repeated volume.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction was found. The source deepens existing double-opt-in guidance by specifying message construction and the requester-introducer division of labor.
- The guidance is one investor's practitioner workflow rather than comparative evidence. Its benefits depend on relationship context, recipient norms, and whether the requested connection is genuinely relevant; opt-in reduces unsolicited burden but does not guarantee fair access or a useful meeting.
