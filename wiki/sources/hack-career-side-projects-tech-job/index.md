## Summary

This Hack Career article argues that side projects can help people, especially candidates from non-traditional backgrounds, break into tech only when the project adds credible evidence to a job application. Merely having a side project is not enough; a weak or hidden project may have no hiring impact despite the time invested.

The article separates rare "Field of Dreams" side projects from ordinary "A for effort" side projects. YouTube Instant is the rare example: Feross Aboukhadijeh built it in 2010 in less than six hours, it became a media story, and it led to a YouTube job offer from co-founder Chad Hurley. Hack Career treats this as exceptional rather than a realistic baseline for most applicants.

For typical side projects, the article recommends three principles. The project should be relevant to the role, deep enough to show thought and quality, and iterated at least once after launch or early feedback. The author also emphasizes that learning a skill is not itself a side project; the candidate needs to ship something visible.

The final section focuses on distribution inside the job search. If the project is meant to strengthen an application, it should be obvious on the resume, described with useful weight, supported by credibility indicators when available, and documented publicly through a post that reflects on the process, challenges, lessons, and project artifacts.

## Key Points

- Side projects are useful hiring evidence only when they address a real weakness or reinforce a relevant strength in the application.
- Most applicants should not benchmark themselves against viral projects that generate unsolicited offers.
- A useful project shows relevance, depth, and at least two iterations rather than being a shallow resume filler.
- Shipping matters more than course completion because employers can inspect the resulting work.
- Strong projects still need deliberate visibility on resumes, profiles, and public write-ups.
