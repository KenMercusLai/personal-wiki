## Summary

This source is Mathilde Collin's short collection of startup advice that stayed useful through first-hand experience building Front. The common pattern is contextual judgment: outside advice, benchmarks, and expert opinion can help, but they should not replace the company's own customer evidence, growth signals, and founder understanding.

The product lesson is to "follow your growth." Collin describes asking Paul Buchheit which of three Front visions to pursue and receiving a deliberately unspecific answer. The lesson she draws is that customers, users, and the team's own market knowledge had better evidence than any outside expert could have from a short office-hour conversation.

The people lessons emphasize compounding hiring quality and prompt correction of bad fits. Patrick Collison's candidate test asks whether the company would want ten times as many people like the person being hired, because early hires shape later hiring. Laura Behrens-Wu's warning favors an open role over keeping a damaging person, and Daniel Yanisse's severance advice treats generous packages as a way to make necessary exits humane and managerially possible.

The management lessons separate irreversible from reversible choices. Jared Smith's one-way/two-way door framing says founders should stay involved in decisions that are hard to undo, such as fundraising, compensation plan changes, acquisitions, or product rewrites, while delegating more reversible calls. Collin pairs that with a later lesson from Front: executives need founder-instinct transfer during onboarding before they can safely operate with high autonomy.

The communication and meta-advice lessons are about repetition and context. Peter Reinhardt's all-hands advice is to restate the mission every time so people keep hearing why the company exists. Andrew Reed's benchmarking advice warns that most startup benchmarks are weak signals for companies trying to do something different. Howie Liu's final warning is to treat advice as one data point and ask for the context behind it.

## Key Points

- Customer and growth evidence should outrank generic expert opinion when choosing product direction.
- Early hiring decisions compound because early employees influence who joins later.
- Keeping a harmful person can damage the organization more than temporarily leaving a role open.
- Generous exit packages can reduce avoidance around necessary management decisions.
- Founders should stay close to irreversible decisions while delegating reversible ones.
- Mission repetition at all-hands keeps the company's purpose visible as the team grows.
- Benchmarks and advice are most useful when their original context matches the company's situation.
- Executive autonomy works better after deliberate transfer of founder judgment during onboarding.
