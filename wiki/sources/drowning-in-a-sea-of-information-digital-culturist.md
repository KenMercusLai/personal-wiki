## Summary
[[ClaytonDArnault]] uses his own compulsive checking, reading backlog, task switching, and creative frustration to describe [[InformationOverload]] as a mismatch between abundant digital inputs and limited capacity to select, absorb, and use them. He connects the condition to social and knowledge-seeking needs, portable technology, fear of missing out, interruptions, unfinished-task pressure, and homogeneous content. His reported week with WNYC's Infomagical suggests that intentional filtering and temporary disconnection can restore clarity, but the essay is a personal account supported by dated or uneven secondary statistics rather than a controlled causal study.

## Key Claims
- Infomania turns checking and accumulating information into a habitual loop even when little of the material is retained or used.
- Digital abundance shifts the scarce resource from access to selection, absorption, and judgment.
- External alerts and self-interruption can fragment concentration; frequent task switching then adds a costly return-to-task burden.
- Backlogs and unfinished tasks can preserve nagging mental reminders, so capture systems may create "idea debt" instead of closure when priorities remain unresolved.
- Constant shallow intake may reduce the uninterrupted thought needed for original creative work and can encourage imitation of widely repeated formats and topics.
- Information overload is better treated as a condition to manage through filtering, boundaries, and deliberate goals than as a problem technology can permanently solve.

## Key Quotes
> "I have an information problem." - d'Arnault's framing of the essay as a personal diagnosis.

> "The key is not disconnecting, but understanding why we need to disconnect." - on using boundaries to recover deliberate choice.

## Contradictions
- The essay's phone-unlock, media-hours, web-size, retention, reading-completion, task-switching, and attention-span figures are historical secondary claims with different scopes and methods; they should not be treated as a single measured causal chain.
- The comparison between an alleged human attention span and a goldfish is not reliable evidence for cognitive decline, and the article does not establish that Internet use caused the author's concentration or memory difficulties.
- The Infomagical result is one participant's short self-report. It supports a plausible management practice but does not show how durable the effect is or whether temporary disconnection works across people and contexts.
- The nine unique local images were inspected. The underwater photograph and separators are decorative; the highlighted excerpts and web-size screenshot duplicate claims already present in the prose, so no images were retained.
