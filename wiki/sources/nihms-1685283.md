## Summary
[[PatrickJSmith]] and [[RhondaMMerwin]] review observational and experimental evidence for physical activity in mental-health prevention and treatment, with the strongest treatment discussion focused on depression and anxiety. They argue that aerobic and resistance exercise may work through interacting [[Neuroplasticity|neurobiological]] and behavioral-learning mechanisms, while emphasizing heterogeneous response, the central importance of [[ExerciseAdherence|continued participation]], and the need to tailor support to reward, executive-function, and affect-regulation differences.

## Key Claims
- Higher habitual physical activity is prospectively associated with lower odds of depression, elevated anxiety symptoms, and anxiety disorders, but these observational findings do not by themselves establish a treatment effect.
- Randomized trials generally favor aerobic exercise for depression and anxiety, and a smaller heterogeneous literature supports resistance training and exercise as an adjunct to conventional care.
- Sustained participation after the supervised intervention is more consistently associated with remission than the assigned initial dose; the review identifies about 150 minutes per week as a practical maintenance threshold in the cited evidence.
- Exercise may create a reinforcing loop in which neurotrophins, metabolic function, lower inflammation, sleep, brain-network function, cognitive control, exposure to arousal, goal practice, and [[SelfEfficacy]] support adaptive learning and continued activity.
- Average benefits conceal substantial individual variation associated with age, symptom severity, cognition, reward sensitivity, executive function, affect regulation, and barriers to maintaining activity.
- Tailored programs may require gradual titration, motivational priming, external structure, distraction, self-management training, or biometric feedback rather than one universal exercise prescription.

## Key Quotes
> “therapeutic benefits are only sustained among individuals who continue to exercise” — on maintenance as a condition of durable benefit.

> “why, and for whom, exercise improves mental health outcomes” — on the review's personalization objective.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction with existing wiki claims was found. The review qualifies simple dose-response and universal-treatment accounts: observational associations are stronger than experimental dose evidence, trial effects vary with methods and comparators, and average improvement does not guarantee individual response.
