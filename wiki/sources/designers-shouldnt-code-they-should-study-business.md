## Summary
[[JoshuaTaylor]] argues that designers gain organizational influence when they understand how their company succeeds and use design to address business problems, rather than discussing only craft, consistency, tooling, and pixel polish. He proposes practical learning through sales, shipping and fulfillment, company projections, economics, fundraising, business models, team management, competitive analysis, and executive priorities. The article does not reject craft; it frames [[DesignBusinessLiteracy]] as the context needed to decide where craft has the greatest value and where additional polish is excessive.

## Key Claims
- Design craft, prototyping, testing, coding fluency, and style guides matter, but they do not by themselves tell a designer which problems matter most to the business.
- Designers can make stronger recommendations and gain influence when they can discuss business goals and operating constraints without requiring those goals to be translated into overly basic terms.
- User research and analytics have already helped design earn credibility by connecting design choices to observable customer and company effects.
- [[DesignBusinessLiteracy]] can be learned through direct contact with sales, shipping and fulfillment, quarterly priorities, economics, fundraising, cap tables, business models, team management, projections, and competitive analysis; an MBA is only one possible route.
- Designers should use design to help solve the problems faced by CEOs and vice presidents, not only problems internal to the design function.
- Business orientation should complement continuing craft development rather than excuse poorly designed experiences.

## Key Quotes
> “We must first understand business in general.” - on locating design craft inside company success.

> “We have to keep growing and focusing on craft.” - on the boundary of the business-first argument.
