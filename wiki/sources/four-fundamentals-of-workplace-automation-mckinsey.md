## Summary
This [[McKinsey]] article by [[MichaelChui]], [[JamesManyika]], and [[MehdiMiremadi]] argues that [[WorkplaceAutomation]] should be analyzed at the level of activities rather than whole occupations. Its preliminary US labor-market model finds broad potential to automate parts of jobs, making process and role redesign, human-machine augmentation, and management of safety, privacy, inequality, and transition costs more important than a simple automated-versus-not-automated job count.

## Key Claims
- The study maps roughly 2,000 work activities to 18 capabilities and assesses those capabilities against technologies demonstrated by 2015, with adjustments for unpredictable work settings.
- About 45% of paid US work activities, representing roughly $2 trillion in annual wages, could potentially be automated with then-demonstrated technology; median-human natural-language capability could add another 13 percentage points.
- Fewer than 5% of occupations could be automated entirely, while about 60% contained at least 30% automatable activities, so automation was more likely to redesign many jobs than eliminate them wholesale in the near or medium term.
- Automation potential was not confined to low-wage work: the model estimated that more than 20% of CEO work time and significant portions of other high-paid occupations involved automatable activities.
- Process-level benefits modeled across several industries were estimated at three to ten times cost through output, quality, reliability, and superhuman task performance, not only labor savings.
- Automation can augment expertise by shifting people toward exceptions, client interaction, creativity, emotional understanding, and other higher-value work, but realizing that possibility requires deliberate role, process, and skill redesign.
- Adoption pace and net outcomes depend on technology development, organizational adaptation, software-versus-hardware intensity, regulation, and management of displacement, inequality, privacy, quality, safety, and legal responsibility.

## Key Quotes
> "a focus on occupations is misleading" — the article's central reason for analyzing activities and capabilities instead of treating each job as one indivisible unit.

> "fewer than 5 percent of occupations can be entirely automated" — the boundary between task-level potential and wholesale occupational replacement.
