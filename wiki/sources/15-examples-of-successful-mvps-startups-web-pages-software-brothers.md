## Summary
[[SoftwareBrothers]] surveys famous early products to argue that a [[MinimumViableProduct]] should test the smallest valuable behavior, market assumption, or distribution channel before a team builds the full system. The examples include simple web pages, email workflows, explainer videos, closed betas, marketplace tests, and narrow single-feature apps. The source extends [[CustomerLedProductDevelopment]] by treating early demand signals, signups, orders, usage, and platform traction as evidence for what to build next.

## Key Claims
- A [[MinimumViableProduct]] should isolate the core customer value instead of trying to ship the eventual full product.
- MVPs can validate demand through non-scalable or manual workflows, including Groupon PDFs, Zappos store-bought inventory, AngelList email introductions, and Airbnb's manually photographed apartment listing.
- Some MVPs are not functioning products at all; Dropbox's explainer video and Buffer's landing-page pricing test collected demand signals before full implementation.
- Platform and distribution choice can be part of the MVP: Zynga's basic poker game worked because it launched on Facebook, while Product Hunt used Linkydink and an existing founder network before building a full platform.
- A successful MVP can be feature-poor if it proves the main experience, as shown by early Facebook, Spotify, Foursquare, Uber, and the first iPhone.

## Key Quotes
> "The minimum viable product a.k.a. MVP is the way to achieve this" - framing MVP as a speed-to-market mechanism for startups.

> "sometimes your MVP isn't about the product, but the platform it ends up on" - the source's lesson from Zynga's Facebook launch.
