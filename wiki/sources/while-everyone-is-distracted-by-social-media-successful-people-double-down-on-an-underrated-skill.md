## Summary
[[MichaelSimmons]] argues that abundant online information creates an allocation problem: people need to find rare, high-leverage [[BreakthroughKnowledge]] without surrendering their attention to feeds, distractions, echo chambers, or fear of missing out. His proposed response combines proactive media selection, compressed and reusable [[MentalModels]], disconfirming evidence, and deliberate [[LearningHowToLearn]]. The argument is a practitioner essay with promotional interests, not a controlled comparison of the proposed methods.

## Key Claims
- [[InformationOverload]] combines content growth, echo chambers, constant distraction, and fear of missing out, producing a default diet of low-value media.
- [[BreakthroughKnowledge]] changes a person's governing beliefs or supplies a durable new lens, whereas incremental knowledge mostly confirms what the person already knows.
- [[AttentionManagement]] should move from reactive consumption of feeds and notifications to proactive selection based on the potential value of an information source.
- Disconfirming evidence can be much more valuable than repeated confirmation because one counterexample may overturn a general belief.
- [[MentalModels]] are presented as a high-density knowledge format because they compress recurring patterns, retain value, and transfer across domains.
- [[LearningHowToLearn]] includes evaluating evidence, seeking diverse knowledge, recognizing cognitive bias, and applying useful knowledge with less wasted time.

## Key Quotes
> "Does this have the potential to fundamentally change my life?" - Simmons's proposed filter for potential breakthrough knowledge.

> "A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention" - Herbert Simon, quoted to frame the scarcity problem.
