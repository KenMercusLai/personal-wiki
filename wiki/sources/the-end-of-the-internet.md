## Summary
[[BenThompson]] argues that the open, public Internet is becoming economically and technically hostile: abundant publishing shifted power toward discovery and advertising aggregators, public social media became algorithmic entertainment, and AI lowers the cost of attacking software built on fallible human foundations. His proposed successor is [[SmallnessAtScale]]—paid niche publishing, trusted group chats, and AI-enabled [[PersonalSoftware]] for individuals or bounded associations—while [[CognitiveCapitalism]] frames cheaper intelligence as a possible source of new local demand rather than only labor displacement. The essay is a strategic forecast built from historical examples, first-person practice, one week's reported vulnerabilities, and [[VenkateshRao]]'s demand thesis rather than measured proof that the public Internet is ending.

## Key Claims
- Free web distribution made content abundant and shifted the scarce, valuable function toward discovery, which [[Google]] captured; unlimited advertising inventory then weakened publisher payouts while the most attractive inventory remained on search.
- [[NicheSubscriptionPublishing]] offers a more durable response to abundance when a distinctive voice earns more from a smaller committed audience than from low-value advertising reach.
- Public social platforms accumulated enough content and defensive behavior to favor engagement algorithms and entertainment, while trusted group chats preserved conversation within many small, finite groups.
- AI can search for software weaknesses through iteration at machine scale, increasing pressure on public self-hosting, although defenders can also use agents and AI-written software to improve security over time.
- Cheap software generation makes applications for one person, team, household, or trusted group economically plausible because the required audience no longer has to repay a large up-front implementation cost.
- [[SmallnessAtScale]] links the article's media, social, software, and market arguments: many bounded groups and associations can collectively support broad creation without one global audience or market.
- [[CognitiveCapitalism]] extends the opportunity claim: reducing “subsistence cognition” may reveal previously unaffordable needs and desires, but the scale and distribution of that demand remain speculative.

## Key Quotes
> "The Internet as we knew it is dead." - Thompson's deliberately categorical diagnosis.

> "the group chat era of software is here" - the proposed successor to mass-market application economics.

## Contradictions
- The “end of the Internet” is a rhetorical forecast, not a measured decline in public sites, self-hosting, open protocols, independent publishing, or network participation. Managed hosting can reduce operational risk without eliminating public access.
- The listed PHP, Node.js, GitLab, Java, Cisco, LiteLLM, Railway, and certificate incidents are a time-bound partial selection. The article does not measure the baseline vulnerability rate or establish that AI caused the reported cluster.
- AI can expand both attack and defense, but the source provides no comparative exploit, remediation, reliability, or cost evidence showing which side gains more or when AI-rewritten software becomes safer.
- Small groups can increase trust and participation while also enabling exclusion, insularity, harassment, weak moderation, fragmented knowledge, and dependence on large messaging or hosting platforms.
- Personal and team software still incurs verification, security, backup, maintenance, interoperability, accessibility, privacy, and succession costs; lower implementation effort does not make public software irrational in every setting.
- Rao's cognitive-demand analogy and Thompson's “infinite local markets” conclusion are prospective economic arguments, not demonstrated demand, income distribution, productivity, employment, or welfare outcomes.
