## Summary

This source argues that venture capital portfolio construction should start from the low observed frequency of outsized startup outcomes. The essay says many VC funds fail to return capital or beat public markets, while the funds that do work often depend on a very small number of exceptional companies. Concentrated portfolios can look brilliant when they contain a winner, but the article treats that as a fragile strategy when unicorn outcomes are rare.

The article uses 500 Startups' experience investing in more than 1,000 companies to describe a power-law distribution. It estimates that 50-80% of investments do not reach an exit or return less than invested capital, 15-25% produce small 2-5X exits, 5-10% become companies worth more than $100 million, and only 1-2% reach unicorn scale and produce very large returns.

From that distribution, the source argues that many VC portfolios are undersized. If unicorns occur only 1-2% of the time, a portfolio with fewer than 50 companies may reasonably contain none. The essay recommends roughly 50-100 companies for later-stage funds and at least 100-200 companies for early-stage funds, with the possibility of still larger seed portfolios because early-stage attrition is higher.

The source also criticizes industry norms that make concentrated portfolios seem like best practice. It suggests that direct board-seat expectations may push traditional VCs toward too few investments, even though investors can sometimes seek control rights without personally serving on every board.

## Key Points

- VC returns are driven by rare outliers, so average-looking startup outcomes can leave a fund below target.
- A small portfolio can outperform if it contains a major winner, but it also creates a real risk of finding no unicorns.
- 500 Startups' rough distribution treats most investments as failures or small exits, with centaur and unicorn outcomes making up a small tail.
- The article recommends sizing a portfolio around the number of investments required to have a credible chance at multiple large outcomes.
- Later-stage VC funds are framed as needing roughly 50-100 companies; early-stage funds are framed as needing at least 100-200.
- Board-seat norms may be one reason traditional VC portfolios stay too concentrated.
