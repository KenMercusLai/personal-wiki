## Summary
[[EdmondLau]] argues that managers can overcorrect for large-team coordination costs by assigning whole software projects to individuals. The resulting [[OnePersonTeamRisk]] includes weaker feedback and learning, a bus factor of one, stalled momentum, misleading perceptions of elapsed time, and lower morale. He recommends a minimum project team of two where practical and serializing priorities so engineers can share context, while still allowing individuals to own bounded tasks.

## Key Claims
- Brooks's Law explains why adding people does not reduce software delivery time linearly, but it does not imply that a one-person project is optimal.
- A teammate with shared project context can provide earlier and more useful design and code feedback than a reviewer from another project.
- Solo projects reduce learning because fewer people can challenge ideas and longer project cycles expose the engineer to fewer distinct projects.
- Shared work can improve motivation, make difficult periods less isolating, and make successful milestones more rewarding.
- A one-person project has a bus factor of one: illness, leave, departure, or a single technical stall can halt progress and leave undocumented shortcuts concentrated in one person.
- Organizations may perceive a part-time solo project as slow because they remember elapsed calendar time rather than invested work time.
- Staffing at least two people on a project and serializing priorities can preserve shared context and momentum without requiring every task to be performed jointly.

## Key Quotes
> "Software development is a team sport." - the premise Lau adopts from *Team Geek*.

> "Ensuring a minimum team size of two can therefore be extremely beneficial." - on the recommended staffing floor.

## Contradictions
- The article qualifies rather than directly contradicts [[MicroCompany]]: it addresses staffing interdependent software projects inside organizations, while the micro-company material describes operators who accept concentrated risk in exchange for autonomy and low coordination cost.
- The recommendation is practitioner guidance, not comparative evidence that two people are always optimal; narrow, low-risk, or exploratory projects may not justify paired staffing.
