## Summary
[[SachinRekhi]] presents [[NetPromoterScore]] as a customer-loyalty KPI that can complement acquisition, engagement, and monetization metrics when product teams want to improve customer delight. Drawing on his [[LinkedIn]] work, the article argues that NPS becomes useful only when teams preserve sampling and methodology consistency, analyze open-ended comments, connect results to product behavior, and feed findings into planning. The valid embedded image visually confirms the promoter/passive/detractor calculation, while a second referenced `.jpg` resolves to unrelated Snapinsta HTML and was not used as evidence.

## Key Claims
- [[NetPromoterScore]] measures customer loyalty by asking likelihood to recommend, grouping respondents into promoters, passives, and detractors, and subtracting detractor percentage from promoter percentage.
- NPS becomes actionable when paired with an open-ended explanation question and, optionally, competitor benchmark questions.
- Collection through email and in-product prompts can bias responses toward more engaged customers, so teams should manage sample representativeness around engagement and tenure.
- Survey frequency should account for customer-base size, response rates, product-development cadence, and the fact that NPS is a lagging indicator.
- Product, product marketing, market research, business operations, and the broader R&D team should participate in analysis so NPS affects product planning.
- Verbatim comment categorization and behavior-to-score correlation can reveal detractor pain points, promoter benefits, and possible activation or "magic moment" behaviors.
- Consistent methodology matters because question wording, ordering, competitor lists, sampling, and seasonality can change NPS comparability.

## Key Quotes
> "there is no substitute for the product team digging in" - on reading customer comments directly.

> "NPS analysis is not a replacement for product strategy." - limitation on how teams should use the metric.
