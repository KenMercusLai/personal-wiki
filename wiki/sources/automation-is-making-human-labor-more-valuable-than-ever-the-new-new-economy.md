## Summary
This [[Vox]] article argues that automation can make routine goods cheaper while increasing the relative value of labor-intensive services where human attention, personal connection, and visible craft are part of the product. It uses [[Etsy]], craft food and drink, [[Starbucks]], therapy, health care, and higher education to show why some apparent inefficiencies are not defects to automate away but core features customers pay for.

## Key Claims
- Automation can reduce the cost of standardized goods while shifting spending toward services where human connection is a premium feature.
- Some businesses deliberately sell the absence of automation, because craft, small scale, provenance, or personal touch create value.
- Coffee service illustrates [[HumanPremiumServices]]: slower preparation can be part of the performance customers want, not only an operational cost.
- In therapy, caregiving, pregnancy-related health services, and education, automation may supply partial access but changes the service category when it removes face-to-face attention.
- The article qualifies automation panic by arguing that service-sector "inefficiencies" often become benefits as richer consumers spend more on human-intensive experiences.

## Key Quotes
> "human labor is a mark of luxury" - thesis about service-sector value.

> "inefficiencies involved in talking to other people are often a key benefit" - conclusion about service work.
