## Summary
[[ConnieChan]] argues that China's 2015-2016 livestreaming boom turned mobile video into a new form of social self-expression, entertainment, and monetization rather than only scheduled performance or gaming. The article frames [[SocialLivestreaming]] around front-camera intimacy, chat, digital gifts, leaderboards, beautification tools, and late-night companionship, while also showing how [[LivestreamCommerce]], brands, platforms such as [[Inke]], [[YY]], [[Momo]], and [[TaobaoLive]], and regulators were adapting to the format.

## Key Claims
- [[SocialLivestreaming]] reframed mobile video from outward capture toward inward, selfie-like entertainment where interaction supplies much of the content.
- [[DigitalGifting]] aligns incentives among viewers, broadcasters, and platforms by turning attention, recognition, flirtation, and status into direct revenue.
- [[LivestreamingCreatorEconomics]] expanded broadcaster supply because social interaction, beautification tools, and bedroom production setups lower the need for traditional talent.
- [[LivestreamCommerce]] makes video a sales surface for ecommerce, product launches, discounts, demonstrations, and seller-buyer interaction.
- The Chinese livestreaming market was fragmented, young, and potentially globalizing, with many apps, uncertain network effects, and companies experimenting outside China.
- [[LivestreamingModeration]] is structurally important because personal real-time broadcasts require content monitoring, platform curation, and compliance with Chinese regulation.
- Late-night usage suggests livestreaming can function as entertainment-as-companionship, not only media consumption.

## Key Quotes
> "Livestreaming in China is thus more like a video version of selfies." - framing social livestreaming as self-expression.

> "Advertising is a very small source of revenue" - using YY to show non-ad monetization.

> "entertainment-as-companionship" - closing interpretation of the loneliness use case.
