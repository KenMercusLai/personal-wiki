## Summary
[[DanielGross]] offers engineers and data scientists a business-focused framework for evaluating ML/AI employers. The questions test whether a company solves a necessary problem, materially improves on the pre-AI alternative, learns deeply from users, has a direct revenue and distribution model, derives its market size, and possesses a durable advantage beyond an algorithm.

## Key Claims
- AI companies are especially vulnerable to building a technical solution before establishing a compelling customer problem.
- Replacing a human is not automatically valuable: the human may be affordable or desirable, so the product should enable something previously impossible, improve it by roughly an order of magnitude, or deliver comparable quality at roughly one-tenth the cost.
- Customer discovery should reveal underlying problems rather than merely collect reactions to the current solution, particularly when teams must choose acceptable precision and recall for a stochastic product.
- A credible company should explain how the current product makes money without depending on a speculative later business that is expected to become profitable.
- Word of mouth is not a sufficient growth plan by itself; founders should understand acquisition channels, customer acquisition cost, and customer lifetime value, while recognizing that paid marketing may be more dependable than hoped-for virality.
- Market size should be derived from price, customer count, and time rather than asserted as a large total-addressable-market number.
- An algorithm alone is rarely a durable software moat; defensibility may come from reinforcing business structures such as network effects.

## Key Quotes
> "Many AI companies are a solution-in-search-of-a-problem." - Warning against technology-first market reasoning.

> "Bad answer: an algorithm." - The article's response to a claim of business defensibility.

## Contradictions
- The recommendation to prefer paid marketing over word of mouth qualifies sources that emphasize organic or product-embedded distribution: paid acquisition can be a credible plan, but its sustainability still depends on retention, lifetime value, and channel competition.
- The 10X threshold is a practitioner heuristic rather than a measured universal cutoff. Human-delivered services, regulated products, enterprise workflows, and high-stakes domains may create value through smaller gains, augmentation, consistency, access, or risk reduction.
- The article is a short 2017 interview guide with no outcome data showing that companies giving strong answers subsequently perform better.
