## Summary
Uber Design describes a 2016 rider-app redesign that replaced the original ride-first premise with a destination-first flow: asking “Where to?” earlier supplied enough context to simplify product choice, expose fares and arrival times, search for pickup points in parallel, and prepare later trip steps. The team developed the product and a broad design system concurrently through daily prototype research and cross-functional collaboration, then extended the experience into the in-trip period through journey-relevant content. Seven local images were opened: three duplicate sizes of decorative launch artwork were omitted, while four 60-pixel-wide UI and design-system thumbnails were too small for their details to be interpreted independently and were therefore not retained or used as visual evidence.

## Key Claims
- [[OutcomeFirstProductFlow]] can make a complex interaction feel faster by collecting the user's destination early and using it to contextualize later choices rather than merely minimizing taps.
- Uber's earlier product-selection slider stopped scaling when city-specific offerings grew beyond three or four options and scheduling was inserted into the same crowded surface.
- Daily interviews and iterative Framer and Swift prototypes showed that riders valued decision-relevant context more than seeing every product and feature on one screen.
- Destination knowledge enabled up-front fares, arrival-time comparisons for UberPool and UberX, parallel pickup-point search, and an earlier preview of possible driver matching.
- The redesign and its [[DesignOperations|design-system layer]] were built concurrently, so product decisions tested platform standards against real data and platform decisions immediately constrained the product.
- The system covered foundations, interaction patterns, and reusable components, including grids, type, color, content, icons, motion, alerts, buttons, cards, forms, maps, loading states, selectors, and tabs.
- [[CrossFunctionalProductTeams|Cross-functional collaboration]] involved design, engineering, product management, operations, and marketing from the beginning rather than treating design as a designer-only responsibility.
- The redesign challenged a purely exit-oriented app model by treating the in-trip period as a product surface for music, destination information, and connection with other people.

## Key Quotes
> "Start at the end." - the redesign principle that moved destination entry to the beginning of the flow.

> "Design isn't just the designer's responsibility" - on joint work among design, engineering, product management, and other functions.

## Contradictions
- The source complicates [[UtilityOrientedUX]]'s Uber example. It agrees that riders value efficient transportation, but says the team had wrongly assumed its job ended when the rider entered the car and deliberately created an in-trip content surface.
- The account is a first-party launch retrospective published during gradual rollout. It reports research cadence and design rationale but provides no task-time, error-rate, adoption, accessibility, retention, or business-outcome comparison against the old app.
- Four product images in the captured Markdown are only 60 pixels wide. Their broad montage categories are visible, but labels, states, sequence details, and component behavior are not reliably interpretable, so the source note does not claim visual evidence from them.
