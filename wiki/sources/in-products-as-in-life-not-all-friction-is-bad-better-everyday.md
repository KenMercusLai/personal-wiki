## Summary
This Better Everyday essay argues that product teams should treat friction as a design variable rather than assume every removed step improves the system. Examples from [[Slack]], publishing, [[Snapchat]], [[MusicalLy]], [[MealPal]], and [[DocuSign]] show that lower immediate effort can increase noise, misinformation, or abuse, while deliberate constraints can support social learning, different economics, safety, and value elsewhere in the journey. The three effective local image references are duplicate sizes of a decorative rock-climbing photograph and add no independent evidence.

## Key Claims
- Removing friction can create material second-order effects, so teams should evaluate system behavior after adoption rather than only immediate task completion.
- [[Slack]] made workplace messaging easier, but the source says broad adoption also encouraged more frivolous messages and recreated communication noise at higher volume.
- Lower publishing barriers democratized distribution while also weakening institutional gates that had supported investigation and editorial standards, enabling misinformation and threatening journalism institutions.
- [[Snapchat]] and [[MusicalLy]] are presented as products whose non-obvious interactions encouraged users to teach one another, turning learning friction into a sharing and acquisition mechanism.
- [[MealPal]] exchanges advance payment, advance choice, one option per restaurant, and self-pickup for lower prices, predictable restaurant demand, shorter queues, less deliberation, and a communal pickup ritual.
- Ease can reduce the pause in which users notice risk: the source argues that [[DocuSign]] made agreement convenient while also making fraudulent signature requests easier to execute.
- The appropriate goal is not minimum friction in isolation but a deliberate tradeoff among immediate effort, later value, comprehension, economics, social effects, and safety.

## Key Quotes
> "Sometimes, some friction improves the performance of a product." - the essay's qualification of friction removal as a universal design objective.

> "Sometimes, reducing friction has huge second-order effects." - on evaluating effects beyond the immediate user action.

## Contradictions
- The essay directly qualifies categorical low-friction guidance such as [[users-always-choose-the-path-of-least-resistance]]: users and systems can benefit from an added step when it creates safety, comprehension, coordination, social learning, or a lower total burden.
- The examples are anecdotal 2017 practitioner observations. They provide no comparative funnel, retention, fraud, message-volume, misinformation, restaurant-throughput, or unit-economics data, and they do not establish that friction itself caused the reported outcomes.
