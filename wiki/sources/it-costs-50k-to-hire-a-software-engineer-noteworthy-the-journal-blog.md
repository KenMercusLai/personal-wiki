## Summary
This practitioner estimate decomposes the cost of making a software engineer productive into recruiting, engineering interview time, ramp-up, and package costs. Its worked example reaches roughly $50,000 for a $125,000-salary engineer, then uses that estimate to argue for maintaining hiring standards, encouraging referrals, and testing modest retention investments. The arithmetic is illustrative rather than benchmark evidence: the author explicitly says actual costs vary widely by role, location, company reputation, sourcing route, ramp time, and institutional knowledge.

![A Ford F-150 priced at $50,675 compared with an estimated $50,000 cost to hire a software engineer.](../../wiki-assets/it-costs-50k-to-hire-a-software-engineer-noteworthy-the-journal-blog/hiring-cost-comparison.png)

## Key Claims
- A useful hiring-cost model adds recruiting expense, engineering interview time, new-hire ramp-up, and signing, relocation, or similar package costs.
- In the worked example, a 20% external recruiter fee costs $25,000, internal recruiting and interview time add $5,000, and six weeks of ramp-up adds $20,000, producing a rough $50,000 total.
- Lowering the hiring bar saves little in the article's contrived comparison because recruiting and training remain large fixed or semi-fixed costs: the alternative candidate costs an estimated $41,000 rather than $50,000.
- Employee referrals can remove much of the recruiter fee even after a $3,000–$5,000 referral bonus, but referred candidates should still face rigorous evaluation.
- Avoided replacement cost can justify retention spending; the article's hypothetical 100-engineer team saves more than $100,000 if $20,000 of workplace improvements reduces attrition by two percentage points.
- Organizations should calculate their own cost using local evidence rather than adopt $50,000 as a universal benchmark.

## Key Quotes
> "It’s very expensive to hire good people, but it’s even more expensive when good people leave!" - on connecting hiring cost to retention.

> "Do your own math" - on treating the $50,000 figure as a prompt rather than a fixed industry fact.

## Contradictions
- The article's $50,000 headline is a single hypothetical 2018 estimate, not an observed average; recruiter fees, salaries, interview design, remote work, labor markets, ramp-up measurement, and replacement difficulty vary by organization and time.
- Its claim that people generally leave because they do not feel valued or respected rather than because of money is not supported with data and should not be used to discount compensation, workload, advancement, discrimination, management, or external opportunity.
- The quality-bar comparison assumes a “stellar” candidate and a “pretty decent” candidate can be reliably distinguished and that the latter takes at least as long to train; neither assumption is measured.
- The retention example assumes that office improvements cause a two-percentage-point attrition reduction and values each avoided departure at more than $50,000, but supplies no causal or measurement evidence.
