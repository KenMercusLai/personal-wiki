## Summary
[[WomenInProduct]] presents the rule of threes as a lightweight product-development heuristic for team structure, goals, storytelling, time horizons, user research, iteration, and retrospectives. The article argues that great products are products that achieve both user and business goals, and that balanced triads can keep product work grounded, measurable, and shared rather than dependent on one person or one principle. Its decorative repeated statue images reinforce the "three" motif but do not add separate product evidence beyond the text.

## Key Claims
- Product teams need clear roles, motivation, and accountability, and a three-person team can create more balance than a two-person offense-defense dynamic.
- Product goals should connect a long-term business theme to a product-level goal and then to a short-cycle metric that can be measured frequently.
- Product strategy should be told as a past-present-future story and backed by models that show expected ROI.
- Three months can be enough for scoped product development when the team uses shorter proxy metrics to make mid-development decisions.
- User research signals should be treated as an anecdote once, a possible coincidence twice, and an actionable pattern after three matching observations from target users.
- Shipping every two weeks, alternating early test releases with fuller feature versions, lets teams collect data and validate a product before investing more heavily.
- Start-stop-continue retrospectives help teams turn recent work into process learning without getting trapped in incident detail.

## Key Quotes
> "Having a third person creates balance." - on why a small product team may work better with three people than with two.

> "if you hear the same thing three times, that is a pattern." - on the article's user-research threshold.

> "A great product is simply one that achieves its goal." - on the author's pragmatic definition of product greatness.
