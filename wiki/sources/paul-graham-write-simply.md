## Summary
[[PaulGraham]] argues that ordinary words and simple sentences reduce reading friction, leaving more attention for ideas and helping more readers finish a piece. He presents simplicity as consideration for readers, especially people reading in a second language, and as a discipline that exposes weak thinking and ages better as language and culture change. His own method is rapid drafting followed by days of editing and cutting, with complexity reserved for deliberate effect rather than accidental clumsiness.

## Key Claims
- Lower-friction prose lets readers spend less energy decoding language and more energy engaging with ideas.
- Simple English broadens access because subject-matter expertise does not imply equal fluency in English.
- Ornate language can make readers work for the writer's self-presentation and can conceal the absence of a substantive idea.
- Plain expression acts as an honesty test because an empty claim becomes easier for both writer and reader to recognize.
- Simple writing is more likely to remain intelligible as cultural references and language change.
- Graham's process separates fast first-draft production from several days of revision, much of it cutting.
- Complex sentences or unusual words can serve a deliberate effect, but should not appear through inattention.

## Key Quotes
> "The less energy they expend on your prose, the more they'll have left for your ideas." - on reducing the reader's processing burden.

> "You should never do it by accident." - on using verbal complexity only as a conscious choice.
