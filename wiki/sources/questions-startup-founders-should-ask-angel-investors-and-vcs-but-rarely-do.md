## Summary
The article treats a financing conversation as two-way diligence and gives founders a first-meeting checklist for testing whether an angel or venture firm is actively deploying capital, can make a decision, fits the proposed round, and is likely to contribute after closing. It also argues that founders should prepare diligence material, pre-qualify enough targets, seek warm introductions, and compress meetings so genuine progress and competing interest create [[FundraisingMomentum]]. For calls with associates, it recommends a brief founder pitch followed by investor-focused questions and a request to meet the relevant general partner, while presenting data-mining and competitor-diligence concerns as possibilities rather than established facts.

## Key Claims
- Founders should interview prospective investors because financing creates a continuing relationship rather than a one-way approval process.
- Recent investment count, current deployment status, typical cheque size, recent deal terms, ownership taken, and convertible-note activity can test whether an investor's stated mandate matches observed behavior.
- Founders should identify who can approve an investment, whether an investment committee is involved, when it meets, whether the investor leads or follows, and whether the sector specialist has decision authority.
- Follow-on cheque size, historical follow-on rate, time from first contact to cash, sector metrics, next steps, and specific expected value-add make process and post-close expectations more concrete.
- A prepared, concentrated raise with sufficient targets, warm introductions, ready diligence documents, and visible competing interest can create urgency because waiting often benefits investors.
- An initial associate call can be used to understand the firm and earn access to the relevant general partner, although the article's suspicion that some calls gather competitive intelligence is not evidenced.

## Key Quotes
> “This is a two-way deal.” — the article's reason founders should diligence prospective angels and venture firms.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction was found. The article strengthens the wiki's existing view that investor fit includes decision speed, terms, follow-on capacity, governance path, and working behavior rather than chemistry alone.
- The checklist is an unattributed practitioner article with no founder sample, investor responses, deal outcomes, or comparison showing that asking these questions improves close rates or company performance. Several questions are compressed together in malformed imported list formatting, so the note preserves their apparent meaning rather than their exact layout.
- Concentrating meetings and invoking FOMO can produce timely decisions, but urgency does not resolve weak business evidence, unsuitable terms, or poor partner fit; claims about investor procrastination, herd behavior, data mining, and competitor diligence are asserted without supporting cases or rates.
