## Summary
This short Economist excerpt traces artificial intelligence's movement from repeated overpromising and terminological retreat to renewed public enthusiasm after the 2012 [[ImageNet]] Challenge. It presents [[GeoffreyHinton]]'s deep-learning team as the center of a reported benchmark jump from 72% in 2010 to 85% in 2012, followed by 96% in 2015, while emphasizing that the underlying idea was older than the breakthrough.

## Key Claims
- Artificial intelligence acquired a reputation for hubris and disappointment after early forecasts, including a 1956 summer-research proposal, proved far too optimistic.
- Researchers often used narrower labels such as expert systems or neural networks while “AI” itself carried that reputational burden.
- [[ImageNet]] paired millions of hand-labeled images with held-out testing and an annual workshop, creating a common supervised-learning benchmark and a venue for sharing methods.
- The excerpt reports winning image-labeling accuracy rising from 72% in 2010 to 85% for [[GeoffreyHinton]]'s team in 2012 after applying [[DeepLearning]].
- It reports 96% accuracy in 2015, above its stated 95% human average, and treats the 2012 discontinuity as the start of AI's terminological and commercial rehabilitation.

## Key Quotes
> “The artificial-intelligence boom is based on an old idea, but with a modern twist.” - the article's framing of the 2012 breakthrough.

> “The rehabilitation of ‘AI’ ... can be traced back to 2012.” - the article's central historical claim.

## Connections
- [[ImageNet]] - labeled-image dataset, annual challenge, and public comparison institution at the center of the excerpt.
- [[GeoffreyHinton]] - University of Toronto researcher presented as leader of the 2012 winning team.
- [[DeepLearning]] - technique credited with the 2012 performance jump and subsequent rapid improvement.
- [[AIWinter]] - broader expectation-cycle frame for the earlier pattern of ambitious promises and disappointment.

## Contradictions
- The source's percentages are reported simply as “accuracy”; it does not identify the ImageNet metric, class scope, test protocol, or how the cited human average was measured, so the figures should not be read as general machine-versus-human vision capability.
- The local capture contains only the article's opening two substantive paragraphs. It supports a concise benchmark and reputation history, not a complete causal account of the AI boom.
- The claim that AI's rehabilitation can be traced to one 2012 contest is a strong narrative compression; it does not isolate ImageNet from concurrent changes in data, compute, algorithms, institutions, or commercial investment.
