## Summary
[[ScottBelsky]] argues that a product's [[FirstMileProductExperience]]—its welcome, onboarding, copy, defaults, and empty states—must get interested newcomers to a state where they understand why they are present, what they can accomplish, and what to do next. He recommends proactive setup, immediate personal utility or novelty, visible feedback, familiar patterns, and continued redesign as later adopter cohorts differ from early users. The article is a 2016 practitioner framework illustrated by historical consumer-product examples rather than measured activation or retention experiments.

## Key Claims
- Product teams tend to optimize for current and power users while neglecting the newcomers who determine future growth.
- A new user reaches Belsky's "Zone" when they know why they are present, what they can accomplish, and what to do next; full product mastery is not required.
- First-use design should account for users' low willingness to invest time, desire to look competent quickly, and demand for immediate personal benefit.
- Proactively doing useful work through templates and strong defaults is generally a stronger first-use mechanism than demonstrations, which in turn are stronger than explanations.
- Personal utility or novelty should precede network effects or other delayed benefits, and product copy should make that immediate value visible.
- Empty states, default selections, familiar terminology, and established interaction patterns reduce the choices and retraining imposed on newcomers.
- The first mile must change as a product reaches new demographics, devices, countries, and levels of technical fluency rather than remaining fixed after launch.

## Key Quotes
> "New users are not the same over time." - reason to keep redesigning first use as a product broadens.

> "DO>SHOW>EXPLAIN" - Belsky's ordering of proactive assistance, demonstration, and instruction.

> "the devil's in the default" - Dave Morin's phrase, quoted to emphasize the leverage of initial choices.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction was found. The source complements [[ProductEngagementLadder]] by defining the orientation threshold before deeper product learning, while its preference for doing over explaining should not be read as a rule against meaningful user actions that create comprehension, agency, or trust.
- The article provides practitioner observations rather than controlled evidence. Its examples are historically situated, "lazy, vain, and selfish" is a deliberately provocative simplification, and the recommendation to allocate more than 30% of product energy to the first mile is not supported by comparative resource-allocation or outcome data.
