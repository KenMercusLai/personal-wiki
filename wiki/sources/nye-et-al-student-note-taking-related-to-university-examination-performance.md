## Summary
[[PaulineNye]], [[TerenceCrooks]], [[MelaniePowley]], and [[GailTripp]] analyzed lecture notes from 38 University of Otago introductory-psychology students and found that note quantity was strongly associated with final examination performance. The relationship was stronger among high-attendance students and for a broad multiple-choice paper than for a choice-based essay paper, suggesting that assessment coverage and lecture dependence shape the value of complete notes. The naturalistic design improves realism over short note-taking experiments but remains correlational, course-specific, and unable to show that writing more notes caused higher scores.

## Key Claims
- Across the full sample, total words recorded over ten target lectures correlated 0.72 with combined examination score, compared with 0.56 for target-lecture attendance and 0.52 for average words per attended lecture.
- Restricting the analysis to diligent attendees strengthened rather than weakened the association: among the 12 students attending at least 80% of lectures, total words correlated 0.87 with combined examination score.
- The top eight students recorded about 2.4 times as many total words as the bottom eight and about 62% more words per lecture attended.
- Note quantity related more strongly to the 210-question multiple-choice paper than to the four-of-twelve essay paper, plausibly because the multiple-choice assessment sampled lecture content more broadly and emphasized recall.
- Adding material after class showed smaller positive correlations with exam scores, while abbreviation, layout, underlining, and highlighting showed little consistent relationship.
- Students differed substantially in note quantity across both lectures and individuals; in half the target lectures, the most prolific student recorded more than nine times as many words as the least prolific.
- The results conflict with categorical study-guide advice to keep lecture notes very brief, but they do not establish that indiscriminate transcription is universally optimal.

## Key Quotes
> "The major finding was the strong positive relationship between the total number of words of notes and the three examination scores." - the authors' summary of the principal result.

> "This cannot be interpreted as showing that high lecture attendance and full notes lead to high examination scores." - the study's explicit causal qualification.

## Contradictions
- The findings challenge study guides that advised students to take only occasional words or phrases, because every very high-scoring student in this sample also recorded relatively extensive notes.
- The study does not resolve causality: motivation, prior aptitude, comprehension, handwriting speed, note-review behavior, and other unmeasured characteristics could influence both note quantity and examination performance.
- The sample contains only 38 volunteers drawn from reachable local students in one 1981 introductory psychology course, limiting representativeness and generalization to other subjects, institutions, teaching styles, and assessments.
- A course with limited textbook-lecture overlap and examinations oriented toward lecture recall may reward note completeness more than courses emphasizing synthesis, problem solving, or external reading.
