## Summary
[[BillBuxton]] argues that important technologies usually look sudden only because their long periods of invention, copying, adaptation, refinement, and commercialization are hidden. [[LongNoseInnovation]] reframes innovation as a decades-long accumulation process rather than a single invention moment. The reconstructed archive uses the computer mouse, multi-touch, and Butler Lampson's technology-history work to argue that organizations should value prospecting and refinement as much as original creation.

## Key Claims
- The visible "wow" moment of a technology is often preceded by a long, low-amplitude development history.
- The computer mouse moved from mid-1960s work by William English and Doug Engelbart through research labs and workstations before Windows 95 made it ubiquitous roughly 30 years later.
- Butler Lampson's National Research Council report suggests that 20 years from inception to billion-dollar industry is typical, and 30 years is not unusual.
- Technologies likely to matter over the next 10 years are often already at least 10 years old and visible to people who know where to look.
- Innovation is less about pure invention than about augmentation, adaptation, refinement, and the practical work of making an idea widely usable.
- Organizations undervalue refinement when universities, governments, or businesses expect invention and technology transfer to produce quick returns.

## Key Quotes
> "The bulk of innovation is low-amplitude and takes place over a long period." - thesis.

> "Innovation is not about alchemy or invention." - distinction between invention and refinement.
