## Summary
[[AndreStaltz]] argues that the open, browser-mediated [[WebCentralization|Web]] began losing practical relevance around 2014 as [[Google]], [[Facebook]], and [[Amazon]] concentrated traffic, discovery, commerce, hosting, and user attention. The article distinguishes the Web from the broader internet transport layer: the internet can keep growing while the open Web decays into a less necessary legacy surface. Its forecast is a gradual move toward app, assistant, voice, AR/VR, cloud, and ISP-mediated environments where a few large networks matter more than independent websites.

## Key Claims
- [[Google]] and [[Facebook]] gained direct influence over a majority of internet traffic, making publishers increasingly dependent on search, social feeds, AMP, and platform-hosted article formats.
- Around 2014, [[Facebook]] narrowed its strategy around social networking and messaging through WhatsApp, Messenger, Instagram, and the end of its Bing partnership.
- Around the same period, [[Google]] shifted from search toward AI, assistants, AMP, proprietary cloud hardware, and a "Search to Suggest" model that reduces the need to visit independent pages.
- [[Amazon]] is framed as the commerce counterpart: a market-leadership-seeking platform whose retail, cloud, app, and Echo channels can bypass ordinary website discovery.
- [[WebCentralization]] threatens the original multilateral publishing vision associated with [[TimBernersLee]] because independent sites, publishers, and small businesses lose equal practical footing.
- [[BrowserBypass]] is the common pattern across the giants: mobile apps, assistants, Echo, Google Home, AR/VR, and proprietary clouds use the internet while routing users around the open Web.
- The speculative [[Trinet]] scenario imagines internet infrastructure optimized mainly for Google, Facebook, and Amazon traffic, with independent access becoming expensive, emulated, or less socially necessary.

## Key Quotes
> "The Web's death will come as a gradual decay of its necessity" - on the article's decay thesis.

> "The internet will survive longer than the Web will" - on separating transport infrastructure from the Web.
