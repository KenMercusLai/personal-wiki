## Summary
[[JohnLilly]] reflects on [[SteveJobs]] after Jobs's death, combining gratitude for his design and product legacy with discomfort about the personal damage attributed to his management style. Drawing on Lilly's work at [[Apple]] ATG during Jobs's 1997 return, the essay describes Jobs as an unusually forceful motivator and operator while rejecting imitation or posthumous beatification in favor of individual purpose and sustained contribution.

## Key Claims
- Jobs made excellent, human-centered technology culturally desirable and demonstrated that design could create economic as well as cultural value.
- Apple products enabled creative people to make work of their own, extending the value of design beyond the devices themselves.
- Jobs could inspire extraordinary commitment, but Lilly argues that his dehumanizing and belittling treatment of people caused real harm that cannot be erased by results.
- In a 1997 employee talk, Jobs paired a defiant turnaround message with repriced options and a new three-year vest, asking employees to recommit or leave.
- Lilly recalls a rapid confidentiality crackdown in which four employees were terminated after leaking details despite an explicit warning.
- The essay treats Jobs's accomplishments as team achievements as well as evidence of his leadership and operating ability.
- Jobs's most transferable lesson is not to imitate his personality or methods, but to identify one's own core contribution and pursue it intensely.

## Key Quotes
> "He completely and utterly validated the view that design could be immensely valuable economically, not just culturally." - Lilly on Jobs's design legacy.

> "Don't try to be like Steve. Don't try to be like anyone." - Lilly's rejection of imitation and hero worship.

- [[Apple]] - company context for the 1997 turnaround speech and confidentiality crackdown.
- [[HumanCenteredDesign]] - Jobs is credited with making humane design commercially and culturally important.

## Contradictions
- The source holds two judgments together rather than resolving them: Jobs was an exceptional motivator and operator, and some of his treatment of people was dehumanizing and destructive.
- Lilly's 1997 speech and leak-crackdown accounts are recollections written years later; he signals uncertainty about the exact stock price, and the source supplies no company records or perspectives from the dismissed employees.
- Claims about Jobs's mistreatment rely partly on stories from Lilly's friends and broader reputation rather than incidents Lilly says he personally witnessed.
- The confidentiality episode records operational decisiveness but does not establish that employee-mail monitoring, termination, or the underlying process was proportionate, fair, or lawful.
- The sole local image was opened and omitted as a decorative author portrait, so no asset manifest was created.
