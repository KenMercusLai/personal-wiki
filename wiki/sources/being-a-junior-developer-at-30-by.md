## Summary
The author reflects on entering software development around age 30 after a disrupted earlier plan to become a linguist. The article argues that older career changers can bring prior management, life experience, confidence, and public-facing skills into junior developer work, while still facing comparison, imposter feelings, time pressure, and burnout risk. The inspected images support the public-speaking and tech-community claims: one shows a speaker addressing a large conference audience, and another shows a hackathon-winning group onstage with visible prize vouchers.

## Key Claims
- [[CareerPlanning]] can remain viable even when an earlier life plan collapses; the author moves from linguistics toward web design and programming after family illness and grief.
- [[JuniorEngineerLearning]] for an older career changer includes managing the mismatch between being the oldest person in a company and the most junior developer.
- Confidence grows through practice, finding a niche, asking questions, code review feedback, and communication with supportive bosses and teammates.
- [[TechCommunityParticipation]] can make software work unusually social through meetups, conferences, hackathons, event organizing, teaching, and public speaking.
- Community participation is valuable but can become too intense; the author reports nearly burning out before limiting meetups and protecting weekends.
- [[SkillStacking]] can help older entrants because prior experience and age may support teaching, public speaking, confidence, and cross-role maturity.

## Key Quotes
> "Changing careers is scary and can be very risky." - on the emotional cost of entering tech later.

> "Become good at something you enjoy" - on practice as a confidence source.

> "You are never too old to become happy and successful" - on age and career change.
