## Summary
[[HunterWalk]] argues that founders should distinguish useful challenge from an investor's unsupported demand to prove a market from scratch. His example founder ended a seed-fundraising meeting after deciding that the investor lacked relevant grounding, was asserting power rather than testing a substantive thesis, and was unlikely to become a desirable cap-table partner. The lesson is not to avoid disagreement, but to protect scarce time and energy by disengaging when the relationship is both non-critical and disproportionately stressful.

## Key Claims
- Founders naturally try to persuade skeptics, and substantive disagreement can stress-test assumptions and improve the company story.
- During fundraising, founders also need a stopping rule because every attempt to persuade consumes scarce time, energy, and emotional capacity.
- An investor's rejection is not automatically useful feedback; its value depends on the investor's relevant experience, reasoning, and willingness to engage substantively.
- A financing conversation evaluates the working relationship as well as the company, because an investor who joins the cap table becomes an ongoing partner.
- Ending an adversarial, low-information meeting can be rational when the investor is unlikely to invest and the founder would not want to work with them anyway.
- The broader operating heuristic is to identify non-critical employees, customers, or investors who create disproportionate stress and consider disengaging.

## Key Quotes
> “I told him I didn’t want to work that hard, and wrapped up the meeting.” — the founder's response to an unsupported demand that she prove the market would exist.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction was found. The article qualifies [[StartupVisionSelling]] rather than rejecting it: founders still need to persuade people before proof is complete, but not every unsupported challenge deserves unlimited effort.
- The argument rests on one successful founder's retrospective account as relayed by Walk. It does not provide the investor's perspective, a repeatable test for distinguishing probing questions from power plays, or evidence that early disengagement generally improves fundraising outcomes.
