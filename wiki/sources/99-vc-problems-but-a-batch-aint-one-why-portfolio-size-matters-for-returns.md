## Summary
[[DaveMcClure]] argues from [[500Startups]]' early portfolio experience that most venture funds are structurally too concentrated for a power-law startup market. Because billion-dollar outcomes are rare and dominate returns, the source says early-stage VC portfolios should be sized around the probability of capturing several large winners rather than around traditional board-seat capacity. The embedded "99 Problems" images are decorative; the spreadsheet screenshots are substantive but too low-resolution to recover reliable figures beyond the model assumptions and results stated in the prose.

## Key Claims
- Most VC funds fail to return capital or beat liquid public-market alternatives, and even top-quartile performance is hard to repeat.
- Startup returns are power-law distributed: many investments fail, some return 2-5X, a smaller group becomes "centaurs" above $100M, and about 1-2% become billion-dollar "unicorns."
- Concentrated VC portfolios can perform extremely well only if they contain at least one very large winner; otherwise small sample size creates a serious miss risk.
- If unicorns occur around 1-2% of the time, portfolios below roughly 50-100 companies amount to gambling with LP money unless the investor can reliably select unicorns at a much higher rate.
- The source recommends about 50-100 companies for later-stage funds and at least 100-200 companies, possibly up to 500, for early-stage seed portfolios.
- Traditional board-seat assumptions may be one reason VC portfolios stay undersized, because voting rights and control do not always require every investor to sit on every board.

## Key Quotes
> "Most VC funds are far too concentrated" - Thesis statement on industry portfolio construction.

> "If portfolio size is too small, you risk finding ZERO big wins." - Core risk claim behind the diversification argument.
