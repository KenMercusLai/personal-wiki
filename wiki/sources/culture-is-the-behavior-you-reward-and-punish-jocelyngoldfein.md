## Summary
[[JocelynGoldfein]] uses a 2008 [[VMware]] leadership exercise led by [[CharlesOReilly]] to distinguish declared values from the behavior an organization actually rewards and punishes. She argues that public promotions, access, assignments, praise, marginalization, and firing teach culture more strongly than private compensation or executive statements, especially during rapid growth when newcomers learn from inconsistent role models.

## Key Claims
- [[StartupCulture]] is revealed by the behavior that produces success or failure inside an organization, not by mission statements, social traditions, or professed values alone.
- New employees infer norms by observing successful and unsuccessful colleagues, then imitate or avoid the behaviors they see.
- Public rewards and consequences communicate expectations more powerfully than private compensation or discipline because coworkers can observe them.
- Positive cultural norms require consistent role models; tolerating even a few successful counterexamples can make a value ambiguous or unbelievable.
- Team members shape culture through praise, criticism, and stories; managers shape it through everyday assignments and access; founders and CEOs shape it through unusually visible example.
- Rapid growth makes culture fragile because newcomers may learn from colleagues who are not yet assimilated and urgent staffing decisions can make rewards inconsistent.

## Key Quotes
> "Your culture is the behaviors you reward and punish." - [[CharlesOReilly]]'s workshop conclusion.

> "The decisions may be nuanced, but perceptions won't be." - on how managerial choices become public signals.

## Connections

## Contradictions
- The source sharpens rather than directly contradicts existing culture pages: it treats designed rewards and consequences as inputs into an emergent culture, not proof that leaders can declare a culture into existence.
- Its positive-norm argument is asymmetrical and demanding: uniform role modeling may clarify expectations, but the essay offers practitioner reasoning rather than comparative evidence for how much consistency is necessary or how quickly norms change.
