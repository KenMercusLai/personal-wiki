## Summary
[[AdGuard]] reports a 2017 scan of the Alexa top 100,000 websites for [[CoinHive]] and JSEcoin code, finding 220 home pages that initiated [[BrowserCryptomining]] and estimating that those sites collectively reached 500 million people. The article estimates more than US$43,000 in mining revenue over three weeks, describes pirate-media, torrent, pornography, and streaming sites as early adopters, and argues that browser mining is ethically distinguishable from cryptojacking only when users receive clear notice, meaningful choice, and an opt-out. It presents warnings, blocking tools, and infrastructure enforcement by [[Cloudflare]] as ways to constrain undisclosed use while leaving room for consensual mining as an advertising alternative.

## Key Claims
- AdGuard searched the Alexa top 100,000 sites for CoinHive and JSEcoin code and found 220 sites, or 0.22%, that launched mining from their main pages.
- The detected sites had an estimated aggregate audience of 500 million people; this is a traffic-reach estimate, not a count of confirmed mining sessions or individually verified affected users.
- AdGuard estimated that the sites earned more than US$43,000 in three weeks, while acknowledging that meaningful mining revenue generally required a large audience and long browser sessions.
- Pirate-video sites, torrent trackers, pornography sites, The Pirate Bay, and Showtime properties appear as early examples, but the article does not establish that browser mining was limited to those categories.
- [[BrowserCryptomining]] uses visitors' CPU capacity and device energy as a monetizable resource, creating an ethical boundary around disclosure, affirmative permission, and the ability to refuse or stop it.
- CoinHive's recommendation that operators seek permission could not itself prevent stealth deployment; the article therefore points to browser warnings, ad blockers, antivirus tools, and Cloudflare account suspension as enforcement layers.
- AdGuard chose to warn users and let them allow or block mining rather than silently blocking every miner, framing informed control as preferable to a categorical ban.

## Key Quotes
> "The ethical way for a website to earn money by mining through its audience’s computers is to ask the audience for permission first" — the article's central consent boundary.

> "There are no good and bad tools and technologies, but there are good and bad ways to use them." — the argument against treating browser mining as inherently abusive.

## Connections
- [[AdGuard]] - company that conducted the scan and added allow-or-block mining warnings to its products.
- [[CoinHive]] - newly launched browser-mining service whose code was one of the scan's two detection signatures.
- [[BrowserCryptomining]] - website monetization mechanism and ethical issue analyzed by the article.
- [[Cloudflare]] - infrastructure provider reported as suspending sites that mined without permission.
- [[WebAdEconomics]] - browser mining is proposed as a possible alternative to advertising-funded publishing.
- [[AdBlocking]] - blockers and antivirus products are described as enforcement against undisclosed miners.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction was identified. The article is a company-authored October 2017 snapshot based on code detection and third-party traffic estimates; it does not establish current prevalence, unique affected people, session-level execution, device impact, profit accuracy, or whether opt-in mining later became viable.
