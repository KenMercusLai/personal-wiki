## Summary
[[ZouRunyang]] reflects on moving from software engineering and technical management into the lead HR role at a startup. He argues that systems thinking, product development, hypothesis testing, and failure-tolerant design can improve organization and people practices, but repeatedly limits the analogy: employees are not programmable components, human change is slow, trust and many people decisions are not readily reversible, and sincere observation and listening remain essential.

## Key Claims
- HR leaders help the CEO design organizational rules, so engineering habits can contribute to [[HumanResourcesGovernance]] beyond recruiting or administration.
- Treating the company as a product makes employees its users and requires HR to understand their lived experience rather than optimize only dashboards or formal modules.
- Systems thinking can expose interactions and bottlenecks that a six-module view of HR may miss, but an organization cannot be managed as if it were software.
- Hiring assumptions about education, large-company experience, job gaps, retention, performance, and startup adaptation should be stated and tested rather than accepted as labels or intuition alone.
- Erlang's “let it crash” analogy suggests bounded experiments, isolated blast radii, mentoring, recovery, and review instead of either zero-failure control or unprotected failure.
- Product-style iteration has a human limit: people change slowly, trust is hard to restore, and many rule or personnel decisions cannot be reverted like code.
- Organizational communication is low-bandwidth and lossy, so HR must combine careful listening, nonverbal observation, sincerity, and consistency between stated rules and actual conduct.

## Key Quotes
> “人不是代码，组织不是程序。” — the article's recurring limit on transferring software metaphors into HR.

> “真正能打动人的、改变人的，唯有真诚。” — on the human requirement beneath systems and product methods.

## Contradictions
- The article extends MVP and failure-tolerant software metaphors into people management while explicitly qualifying them: organizational interventions often have slower feedback, unequal power effects, emotional consequences, and irreversible trust costs.
- Its examples and mechanisms are a first-person practitioner argument, not comparative evidence that engineers generally outperform conventionally trained HR leaders or that the proposed hiring indicators predict retention, performance, or return on investment.
