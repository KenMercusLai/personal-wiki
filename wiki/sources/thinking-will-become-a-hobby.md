## Summary
[[DiomidisSpinellis]] argues that improving performance, falling cost, and continuous availability will let AI systems take over much of today's cognitive work. He predicts that thinking may lose its economic necessity and become an elective pursuit, while people face the harder social task of adapting, retaining purpose, and building rewarding lives beyond paid knowledge work. The essay is a personal forecast rather than a measured labor-market analysis, so its claims about dominance, resistance, regulation, and the eventual role of human thought remain speculative.

## Key Claims
- AI performance is advancing across perception, knowledge processing, creation, reasoning, judgment, and autonomous action while average human information-processing proficiency is described as low or declining.
- Falling model costs make replacement or augmentation of human cognitive labor financially attractive across a widening set of activities.
- Around-the-clock operation and planned data-center investment increase AI availability and capacity, while automation may also address demographic and skills-based labor shortages.
- Resistance may be especially strong because cognitive automation threatens knowledge workers whose expertise, information control, and decision authority confer institutional power.
- The author expects regulation and taxation to slow but not stop the transition, by analogy with earlier technological revolutions.
- Human thought may become optional in the way hunting, calligraphy, woodworking, and horse riding shifted from economic necessities toward chosen activities after technological substitution.
- Preparing for the transition requires keeping pace with AI, adapting through disruption, and developing purpose and satisfaction beyond employment.

## Key Quotes
> "AI will decouple human thinking from its economic purpose." - the essay's central prediction

> "making thinking part of a life worth living" - the author's personal framing of adaptation beyond paid cognitive work

## Contradictions
- The prediction is an argued personal view, not a forecast built from task-level adoption data, comparative cost curves, deployment reliability, organizational evidence, or measured labor-market outcomes.
- The essay moves from selected capability advances and investment projections to economy-wide cognitive dominance without establishing the timing, distribution, or completeness of substitution.
- Its historical analogies do not show that thought will follow the same path as hunting, calligraphy, woodworking, or horse riding; cognitive activity can remain economically complementary to machines even when particular tasks are automated.
- The claim that regulation and taxation will fail to stop the transition is not supported by comparative policy analysis and does not distinguish delay, redirection, safety constraints, redistribution, or prohibited uses from total suppression.
- The source acknowledges augmentation and a difficult transition but gives limited attention to new task creation, adoption friction, accountability, demand feedback, inequality, access, or domains where human relationships and legitimacy are part of the output.
