## Summary
[[AndrewChen]] argues that early founders often undersell themselves because normal social modesty conflicts with investor, employee, partner, and media pitching. The [[DinnerPartyJerkTest]] asks whether a founder's description would feel embarrassingly self-promotional at a friendly dinner; if not, the pitch may be omitting credentials, earned secrets, traction, ambition, and the future story that make [[StartupVisionSelling]] credible. The inspected header image is promotional rather than evidentiary: it repeats the phrase "Be the dinner party jerk" beside a celebratory dinner-party scene.

## Key Claims
- Early-stage company evaluation changes by stage: pre-seed investors bet heavily on team, seed on product, Series A on traction, Series B on revenue, and Series C on unit economics.
- Founders should deliberately enter pitch mode rather than dinner-party mode, naming credentials, achievements, unique team qualities, earned secrets, small proof points, and traction numbers.
- Self-promotional pitching feels socially awkward because conformity and shyness make people avoid exaggerating differences among peers.
- Strong startup pitches should emphasize the future, upside, customer love, market direction, team grit, wedge-to-huge-market logic, and what might happen if the company works.
- [[StartupVisionSelling]] is not only for investors: prospective employees, journalists, partners, event audiences, and the founder's own motivation also depend on an optimistic narrative.
- Pitch optimism should coexist with clear-eyed operating judgment about missing product-market fit, unit economics, marketing gaps, team gaps, and other unresolved problems.

## Key Quotes
> "there's pitch mode and dinner party mode" - on separating investor-facing communication from ordinary social modesty.

> "Of course I'm going to discount it and round down" - on why investors can still value an ambitious future story without taking every projection literally.
