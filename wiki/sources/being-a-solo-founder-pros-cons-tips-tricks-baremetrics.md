## Summary
This [[Baremetrics]] essay argues that solo founding trades speed, clear authority, and reduced co-founder drama for concentrated decision load, narrower perspective, weak idea-testing, loneliness, and greater exposure to bad judgment. It prefers a suitable co-founder when one is available but treats a poor match as worse than founding alone, then recommends peer community, team autonomy, varied non-startup inputs, and deliberate mental and physical care as compensating systems for solo founders.

## Key Claims
- [[SoloFounding]] can accelerate decisions and clarify company direction because one person holds final authority.
- The same concentration of authority produces decision fatigue, emotional burden, limited perspective, weak brainstorming, loneliness, and fewer checks on bad ideas.
- A co-founder is generally useful only when the relationship is a strong fit; internal feuding and disagreement over direction can erase the benefits of having one.
- Prospective co-founders should complete a test project, establish a clear decision hierarchy, and avoid treating a late-arriving partner as automatically equivalent to an original founder.
- Solo founders can reduce isolation through a small peer community whose members understand founder pressures and can offer practical advice.
- Giving employees more context, autonomy, and decision responsibility can distribute operating load without manufacturing a co-founder relationship.
- Hobbies, relationships outside technology, therapy, meditation, exercise, and other health practices can interrupt the startup echo chamber and protect judgment under pressure.

## Key Quotes
> "the only thing worst than having no co-founder is having a co-founder that's a bad fit" - on rejecting co-founding as a universal rule.

> "Set your team on the right path and let them bear the weight along with you." - on distributing decisions.

## Contradictions
- The essay favors a clear CEO hierarchy with final say, while its own case for co-founders emphasizes the value of genuinely different perspectives; authority clarity can prevent deadlock but can also weaken the corrective value of a partner.
- Its advice not to add a co-founder after the company has started is categorical and unevidenced; the more defensible underlying concern is late-stage misalignment of priorities, ownership, and authority.
