## Summary
[[NeilCybart]] argues that [[Apple]] could realistically retire half of its peak outstanding share count within three years by combining its existing buyback pace with repatriated foreign cash after U.S. corporate tax reform. The article frames [[ShareBuyback]] as a capital-allocation mechanism that can increase continuing shareholders' ownership percentage without directly improving operating performance. Its embedded chart shows AAPL shares outstanding peaking near 6.6 billion in 4Q12 and falling to about 5.3 billion by 1Q17, a 20% decline attributed to Apple's repurchase program.

## Key Claims
- [[ShareBuyback]] retires shares and raises remaining holders' ownership percentage, but it usually does not create operating value by itself.
- Apple had already repurchased about 20% of outstanding AAPL shares between its 4Q12 peak and 1Q17.
- Continuing to spend $30B to $35B per year on repurchases could reduce Apple's share count by another 16% by the end of 2019.
- Repatriating most of Apple's roughly $230B in foreign cash under tax reform could give management a much larger domestic cash pool for buybacks.
- Spending $150B of repatriated cash on repurchases could retire another 23% of shares while still leaving Apple with close to $100B in cash and strong annual cash generation.
- The path depended on stable iPhone cash flow, growing Services, flexible tax reform, board authorization, and a share price low enough for the math to work.
- Cybart rejects the claim that Apple was secretly going private because repurchased shares were retired rather than held by management.

## Key Quotes
> "Share buyback is the opposite mechanism of an IPO or secondary offering." - explanation of the finance mechanism.

> "Wall Street thinks Apple is bluffing." - Cybart's interpretation of investor skepticism about Apple's willingness to spend heavily on repurchases.
