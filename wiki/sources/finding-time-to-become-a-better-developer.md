## Summary
This practitioner essay presents developer time management as selective learning, lifecycle-aware code quality, realistic expectation setting, risk-sensitive refinement, and scheduled recovery. It argues that sustainable reliability matters more than following every technology trend or meeting impossible deadlines through overwork, while treating tests, iterative design, and deliberate breaks as investments in future capacity.

## Key Claims
- Developer learning should prioritize durable fundamentals, current versions of the tools used every day, and established technologies with credible adoption signals rather than every new framework announcement.
- A small scheduled learning block can compound; the article proposes 25 minutes of daily reading and experimentation as a practical example rather than a measured optimum.
- [[InternalSoftwareQuality]] should be evaluated across a feature's whole lifecycle, including later debugging, refactoring, and the cost imposed on adjacent work, not only the time until code first appears to work.
- [[IterativeRefinement]] combines test-driven development with “make it work, make it right, make it fast”: produce functioning code, improve its design, then optimize only where user experience warrants it.
- [[BurnoutPrevention]] requires expectation management and sustainable commitments; repeated heroic overwork can create an impossible performance baseline, damage health, and eventually reduce reliability.
- [[PersonalProductivity]] depends on return on investment: “right” means reliable and changeable rather than perfect, while “fast” means fast enough for the user experience rather than maximally optimized.
- Scheduled breaks replenish attention and make recovery legitimate instead of leaving downtime to arrive through exhaustion.

## Key Quotes
> "the real heroes are the ones who are consistently reliable" - on replacing unsustainable deadline heroics with expectation management.

> "make it work, make it right, make it fast" - the Kent Beck sequence used to frame iterative design.

> "right does not mean perfect" - on bounding refinement by practical value.

## Connections
- [[InternalSoftwareQuality]] - tests, design, debugging, and change costs are treated as one lifecycle investment rather than separate optional tasks.
- [[IterativeRefinement]] - working code becomes evidence for later design improvement, followed by selective performance optimization.
- [[BurnoutPrevention]] - realistic commitments and scheduled recovery are presented as prerequisites for dependable work.
- [[PersonalProductivity]] - learning, quality work, optimization, and rest are allocated according to long-run return rather than immediate busyness.
- [[KentBeck]] - credited by the article with the “make it work, make it right, make it fast” sequence.
- [[TestPyramid]] - adjacent testing strategy; the source advocates test-driven development but does not compare test types or suite shapes.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction found. The article aligns with the wiki's distinction between sufficient quality and perfection, while adding lifecycle cost and perceived user performance as allocation tests.
- Its claims are first-person practitioner advice rather than comparative evidence. It does not establish that test-driven development always reduces total effort, that fundamentals literally improve learning tenfold, that market-leader backing reliably predicts adoption, or that scheduled breaks alone can repair structurally impossible workloads.
