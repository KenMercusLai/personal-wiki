## Summary

This source uses the "boat versus mystery box" trope to frame startup equity as uncertain upside rather than cash-like compensation. The essay argues that venture investors can rationally keep taking mystery-box bets because they hold a portfolio, while employees usually make one career and salary tradeoff at a time.

The article's Slack example emphasizes hindsight bias. An early employee who sold shares before Tiny Speck became Slack may look wrong after Slack's later valuation, but at the time the company was a troubled game studio and the cash was real. The source contrasts that with failed or impaired startup equity outcomes such as Fab, Secret, and Square RSUs priced before a lower public-market valuation.

The central compensation claim is that employee equity has little present value unless it becomes liquid. The source describes equity as a salary negotiation device: companies use possible future upside to persuade employees to accept lower cash pay and preserve burn. If the equity cannot be spent, sold, liquidated, or exchanged for a current cash equivalent, the article treats it as speculative rather than equivalent to salary.

The essay also distinguishes employee and founder equity. Employees face vesting cliffs, possible termination before vesting, liquidation preferences that pay investors first, and limited access to secondary sales. Founders, by contrast, control the company-created equity, can trade it to investors for cash, and may sell portions in secondary transactions before ordinary employees have meaningful liquidity.

## Key Points

- Startup equity decisions are easiest to judge after the outcome is known; ex ante, most employee grants resemble a high-variance lottery ticket.
- Employee equity should not be treated as cash unless it can be converted into cash or traded for something of equal value.
- Vesting cliffs and liquidation preferences make nominal ownership different from secure employee wealth.
- Founder equity and employee equity have different practical economics because founders can allocate, sell, and use equity in ways employees usually cannot.
- Employees who doubt a company's prospects should negotiate for more cash instead of accepting speculative equity as a salary substitute.
