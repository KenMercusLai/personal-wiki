## Summary
[[RodneyBrooks]] argues that Level 4 and Level 5 vehicles must function as social actors in mixed urban traffic, not merely avoid collisions. Their inability to read and reciprocate informal pedestrian-driver signals could make them either privileged threats or over-cautious obstacles, while owners could use empty vehicles to externalize parking and congestion costs. Brooks therefore expects restricted operational domains and slower adoption than technical capability alone suggests, though the article's comment thread qualifies his ownership assumptions, proposes policy remedies, and disputes his dismissal of autonomous-vehicle ethics.

## Key Claims
- [[AutonomousDrivingSafety]] includes socially legible interaction: pedestrians look for acknowledgement, infer intent from speed and gaze, reward patient drivers, and rely on locally variable norms that a driverless vehicle may not understand or reciprocate.
- Highly cautious vehicles may be bullied, trapped by pedestrian flows, or resented for slowing human traffic, while vehicles that do not visibly yield could be treated as illegitimate occupants of shared streets.
- Empty autonomous vehicles can turn private convenience into public cost by circling instead of parking, waiting illegally, occupying scarce spaces early, or joining school queues without an accompanying adult.
- Social resistance is likely to restrict early Level 4 deployment to operational domains such as separated lanes, automated garages, controlled ride-hailing districts, and off-peak delivery, while broad Level 5 deployment arrives much later.
- Public tolerance may apply a stricter safety standard to machine-caused deaths than to the much larger baseline of human-caused deaths, slowing adoption even when automation reduces total harm.
- Brooks argues that trolley-style dilemmas are a distraction from practical perception failures and the long tail of real road cases, but commenters counter that everyday risk allocation, defensible design principles, and policy choices still have ethical content.
- Commenters also propose external intention indicators, common standards, restrictions on unoccupied travel, shared fleets, and pricing or parking rules as ways to reduce the social failures Brooks predicts.

## Key Quotes
> "Such is the root of anti-social behavior. Convenience for the individual ... versus over exploitation of the commons." - Brooks on empty vehicles circling instead of parking.

> "Imperfect improved safety should not be the enemy of improved safety." - Brooks in the comment thread on deploying systems before hypothetical moral edge cases are fully resolved.

## Contradictions
- Qualifies [[AutomobilitySecondOrderEffects]] by arguing that socially cautious vehicles and owner-directed empty trips can create congestion even when computer driving improves road capacity.
- Commenters challenge the owner-abuse forecast: shared fleets or rules restricting empty travel could prevent several parking and school-queue scenarios.
- Commenters challenge Brooks' dismissal of trolley problems by reframing ethics as ordinary risk allocation and the duty to defend design decisions, while Brooks maintains that unreliable perception makes many stylized distinctions operationally unavailable.
