## Summary
[[Homebrew]] presents startup compensation as an operating system that should align with company values, combine current salary and equity benchmarks with explicit levels, and constrain negotiation through consistent rules and exceptions. Its recommended package targets median cash and upper-quartile equity for early-stage hires, using ownership to compensate for startup risk while preserving enough cash and equity for employees and future hiring. The guide also covers founder pay, refresher grants, offer sequencing, references, candidate privacy, and closing, but its numerical examples are a 2015 survey snapshot and its broader recommendations are practitioner advice rather than measured outcomes.

## Key Claims
- A startup should define its [[StartupCompensationDesign]] early because improvised offers compound internal inequity and become harder to correct as headcount grows.
- Compensation practice should express company values: a company claiming transparency should be able to explain how salary, equity, level, exceptions, rewards, and promotion decisions were reached.
- Salary and equity bands tied to explicit levels can reduce subjectivity, provided market data, promotions, and exception approvals are refreshed and applied consistently.
- [[Homebrew]] recommends targeting the 50th percentile for cash and the 75th percentile for equity, presenting early ownership as payment for company, product, and leadership risk.
- A 2015 Engineering Manager example reports $139,000 median base salary and 0.64% upper-quartile equity among ten surveyed companies that had raised $0–10 million; these are historical segment observations, not current universal benchmarks.
- Equity refreshers should reflect performance and seniority and begin no earlier than two years in the stated policy; the guide suggests two years for directors and above and three years below director absent a title or performance change.
- A formulaic negotiation policy can protect peer equity by defining which offer components are adjustable, who can approve exceptions, and what happens when candidates counter.
- Two cash-equity packages can expose a candidate's risk preference, while a verbal agreement on compensation and start date before the written letter can reduce document churn.

## Key Quotes
> "Develop a compensation structure with pre-set levels and corresponding bands for salary and equity to minimize subjectivity." — the guide's core system-design recommendation.

> "Assume that everyone knows everything." — the guide's test for whether each compensation decision can be justified consistently.

## Contradictions
- The guide's 50th-percentile cash and 75th-percentile equity targets are policy choices supported by a historical private-company survey, not evidence that this mix optimizes attraction, retention, fairness, or company outcomes.
- The ten-company $0–10 million Engineering Manager segment is too small and dated to serve as a current salary or ownership benchmark; title, geography, capitalization, option terms, and survey methodology further limit comparison.
- “Over-equitizing” may align interests but does not remove [[EmployeeEquityRisk]] from illiquidity, dilution, vesting, exercise cost, tax, preferences, or failure.
- Backchannel references can add candid evidence but create privacy, consent, access, bias, and legal risks; the guide's confidentiality precautions do not establish fairness or legality across jurisdictions.
- Gifts, investor sell calls, family assistance, urgency, and a fixed offer expiry may support a candidate, but they can also create pressure or uneven treatment unless governed carefully.
- The article recommends outside-counsel review of offer letters and should not be read as legal, tax, or current compensation advice.
