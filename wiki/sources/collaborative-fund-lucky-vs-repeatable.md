## Summary
[[MorganHousel]] argues that "luck" is often a less useful learning frame than asking which parts of an outcome are not repeatable. Using [[JeffBezos]], [[Amazon]], [[WarrenBuffett]], [[JohnDRockefeller]], [[ElonMusk]], [[DollosLaw]], and a [[JasonZweig]] quote about post-dot-com speculation, the essay says learners should extract broad repeatable skills while avoiding narrow lessons tied to one-time market conditions, legal systems, timing, or competitive structure.

## Key Claims
- Calling an outcome lucky can be socially and psychologically loaded; asking what is not repeatable makes the learning problem more precise.
- Skill and non-repeatability can coexist: Bezos may have been visionary and capable while Amazon also depended on Internet growth, market conditions, competitors, politics, and regulation that cannot be recreated exactly.
- Learners should emulate repeatable skills, not the non-repeatable context around another person's success.
- [[DollosLaw]] is used as an analogy for historical path dependence: traits or outcomes can emerge from specific forces without those forces being reproducible later.
- Business and investing history should be mined for broad lessons about behavior, incentives, and conditions, not copied as a direct map of future details.
- [[JasonZweig]]'s dot-com and housing example warns that narrow postmortems can leave the underlying speculative pattern intact.
- Buffett, Rockefeller, Musk, and Bezos each offer useful repeatable lessons, but their specific market, legal, or competitive contexts are less transferable.

## Key Quotes
> "Maybe a better way to frame luck is by asking: what isn't repeatable?" - Housel on reframing luck as a learning question.

> "It's dangerous to learn narrow lessons." - Zweig on post-crash investors moving from Internet-stock speculation to housing speculation.
