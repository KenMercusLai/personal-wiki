## Summary
[[NeilCybart]] argues that [[Apple]] was using unexpectedly low entry prices for [[AirPods]] and [[AppleWatch]] to make its wearables mass-market products while still moving deeper into luxury-brand territory. The article frames this as [[ApplePricingStrategy]]: Apple combines scale, balance-sheet strength, vertical integration, and premium SKUs to widen access at the low end while preserving margin and brand power at the high end. The embedded AirPods image is substantive evidence because it shows Apple's own launch positioning for AirPods as available in late October at $159.

## Key Claims
- [[AirPods]] were underpriced relative to the early truly wireless headphone market, where most comparable products clustered between $199 and $300.
- [[AppleWatch]] Series 1 at $269 and Series 2 at $349 were aggressively priced against smartwatches and only modestly above some fitness trackers.
- Cybart rejects a simple "iPhone as hub" explanation; he argues Apple's pricing path began earlier with the $499 iPad and $329 iPad mini.
- Apple's better explanation is product-line segmentation: lower entry prices grow the user base while premium SKUs such as Hermès and Edition versions preserve high-end margin and luxury positioning.
- Apple's scale, supply-chain leverage, balance sheet, owned silicon, and vertical integration let it price personal technology below competitors without abandoning high margins.
- The source predicts lower entry prices and broader lineups for Apple Watch, AirPods, iPhone, and iPad, including a likely $199 Apple Watch and a future AirPods platform.
- The deeper strategic claim is that Apple is redefining luxury by making premium experiences accessible through mass-market gadgets rather than relying only on scarcity or exclusivity.

## Key Quotes
