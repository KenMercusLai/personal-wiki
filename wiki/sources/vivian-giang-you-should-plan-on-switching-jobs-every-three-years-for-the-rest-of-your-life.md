## Summary
[[VivianGiang]] argues that frequent job changes were losing their stigma as younger workers placed more value on continuous learning, development, and advancement than on lifetime loyalty to one employer. Drawing mainly on advice from [[PattyMcCord]], the article recommends considering a move every three to four years because a new company can create a steep learning curve, but it supplies no longitudinal evidence that this cadence improves most careers.

## Key Claims
- [[StrategicJobMobility]] can accelerate skill development by repeatedly placing workers in unfamiliar organizations, roles, and learning curves.
- [[PattyMcCord]] recommends that young workers consider changing companies every three to four years rather than assuming long tenure is inherently desirable.
- Short-tenure workers may be motivated to learn quickly, make an early contribution, and establish a strong reputation.
- The older association between job hopping, disloyalty, and poor fit was becoming less decisive as expectations around learning and advancement changed.
- The article cites a secondary claim that workers staying more than two years earn substantially less over time, but it does not explain the estimate's method or establish that changing jobs causes the difference.

## Key Quotes
> "You build skills faster when changing companies because of the learning curve." - [[PattyMcCord]] on the proposed development mechanism.

## Connections
- [[VivianGiang]] - author presenting the job-mobility argument.
- [[PattyMcCord]] - former Netflix talent executive whose advice supplies the article's central three-to-four-year heuristic.
- [[StrategicJobMobility]] - the source's argument that deliberate employer changes can create learning and bargaining opportunities.
- [[CareerPlanning]] - job tenure becomes a revisable career decision rather than an automatic loyalty commitment.
- [[WorkplaceLearning]] - unfamiliar companies and responsibilities are presented as catalysts for rapid learning.
- [[MarketBasedCompensation]] - external job moves may expose a worker to current market pricing, although the article does not isolate this mechanism.
- [[Netflix]] - McCord's former employer and the culture context used to establish her authority.

## Contradictions
- The source challenges the older assumption that frequent moves necessarily signal disloyalty or poor performance, but it does not show that a fixed three-to-four-year cadence fits every occupation, labor market, visa status, benefit structure, caregiving situation, or valuable long-tenure role.
