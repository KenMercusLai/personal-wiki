## Summary
[[JoannaWiebe]] argues that landing-page social proof is not optional because visitors interpret both visible approval and the absence of approval. The article treats testimonials, share counts, reviews, comments, and celebrity associations as conversion evidence only when they help the prospect see relevant people trusting the offer. Its screenshots show how [[Microsoft]], Avaya, Unbounce, Marie Forleo, QuickSprout, Help Scout, Copyblogger, Social Triggers, and Jack White present different kinds of positive, negative, hidden, or deliberately inverted social proof.

## Key Claims
- [[SocialProof]] can be positive, neutral, or negative; zero tweets, missing testimonials, weak endorsements, or visible low activity can signal that nobody else trusts the page.
- Testimonials work best when they are specific, natural, and prospect-relevant rather than generic praise or corporate jargon.
- Famous-name association can backfire when the audience cannot see itself in the people displayed.
- Share buttons, subscriber counts, comment totals, and post dates should be hidden or delayed when they would make a page look unloved, stale, or poorly matched to the conversion goal.
- Embedded tweets, customer-story collection, and carefully chosen relatable examples can substitute for formal testimonials while a site builds traction.
- Anti-testimonial swagger can work only for exceptional brands or artists whose audience already reads contrarian proof as part of the appeal.

## Key Quotes
> "A lack of a reaction IS a reaction." - on visible low engagement.

> "You are proving something with your use of - or failure to use - indicators of social approval." - on social proof as an unavoidable signal.
