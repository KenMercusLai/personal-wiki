## Summary
Francesco Bertelli traces why calculator and telephone keypads use inverted number orders: calculator layouts descend from key-driven calculators, cash-register experiments, Comptometers, Dalton adding machines, and David Sundstrand's 1914 10-key design, while telephone layouts were chosen through mid-century AT&T human-factors testing for push-button dialing. The source argues that there was no single purely logical layout; mechanical constraints, trained-user efficiency, compactness, familiarity, and software reuse all helped turn historical designs into durable [[NumericKeypadLayoutConventions]].

## Key Claims
- Calculator keypads did not copy modern phones; they descend from adding-machine and cash-register histories that eventually stabilized around [[DavidSundstrand]]'s 3x3-plus-0 layout.
- Early key-driven calculators such as [[JeanBaptisteSchwilgue]]'s 1844 prototype and later [[DorrFelt]] Comptometers show that numerical keyboards predate both modern calculators and touch-tone phones.
- The 9-to-1 Comptometer layout was plausibly shaped by mechanical linkages and trained-operator efficiency rather than modern beginner-friendliness.
- AT&T's push-button telephone research tested many possible shapes, including calculator-like layouts, and selected a compact top-to-bottom 1-2-3 layout after user-performance studies.
- The images are part of the evidence: they show side-by-side phone and calculator inversion, early row and price-key layouts, multi-column 9-to-1 machines, Dalton and Sundstrand 10-key layouts, AT&T test arrangements, smartphone numeric keyboards, and Oculus Go's calculator-like VR numeric pad.
- Modern digital products often preserve inherited keypad conventions even when the original mechanical or network constraints no longer apply.
