## Summary
[[HubertHoran]] argues that [[Uber]]'s 2012-2016 financial record did not resemble a software startup growing into profitable scale, but a transportation business using investor subsidies to sell rides below cost. The article frames Uber's valuation and dominance thesis as an economic-welfare claim that would require sustainable profits, lower costs or higher quality, hard-to-copy advantages, and consumer benefit sharing. The embedded financial tables show persistent negative EBITAR and GAAP margins, while reader comments add anecdotal driver-side evidence about depreciation, deadhead miles, surge dependence, and weak driver economics.

## Key Claims
- Uber reported or leaked financial data showed large operating losses rather than rapid margin improvement: Exhibit 2 shows a roughly $2.0 billion GAAP loss on $1.4 billion revenue for the year ending September 2015.
- Passenger fares were heavily subsidized: Horan argues riders paid about 41% of the actual trip cost while investor capital covered the rest.
- The 2016 improvement in EBITAR margin is explained as driver-pay compression rather than operating leverage: Exhibit 3 shows driver share falling from roughly 83% to 77%, and a counterfactual 83% driver share would have made EBITAR margin worse than 2015.
- Horan rejects Uber's comparison to digital platforms with near-zero marginal cost because drivers, vehicles, and fuel dominate urban car-service costs and do not scale down like software distribution.
- Uber China is treated as an inadequate explanation for Uber Global's losses because of its separate ownership structure and limited inclusion in global operating numbers.
- The comments reinforce the driver-economics critique by pointing to car depreciation, non-revenue miles, idling, guarantees, surge dependence, and uncertainty around autonomous fleet costs.

## Key Quotes
> "Uber is currently a staggeringly unprofitable company." - Horan's conclusion about the financial evidence
