## Summary
[[MelonieFullick]] argues that poor university websites are not merely visual-design failures: their navigation, search, content quality, and audience priorities reproduce institutional silos, internal politics, unclear ownership, and unequal resourcing. The appended reader comments deepen that [[UniversityWebsiteGovernance]] diagnosis by describing understaffed web teams, distributed maintenance without support, homepage competition, and the tendency to fund recruitment marketing more readily than everyday information services. The result is a communication medium that makes a complex institution harder to navigate for current students, staff, prospective students, and the public.

## Key Claims
- University sites frequently bury basic tasks and information behind excessive clicks, link loops, large menus, weak search, PDFs, and structures that presume users already understand the institution.
- Audience buckets such as current students, staff, and prospective students can fail because real users cross those categories and seek tasks or services rather than the office that owns them.
- [[InformationHierarchy]] often mirrors internal organizational charts and political priorities instead of users' questions, making institutional silos visible in the interface.
- Missing dates, incomplete directories, obsolete pages, contradictory details, and unstable URLs are content-governance failures as much as design defects.
- Recruitment and prestige marketing can displace practical information for people already at the institution, even though promotional users may dislike the result too.
- Unclear responsibility and insufficient staffing create a maintenance trap: central teams lack capacity, while departments may receive publishing responsibility without time, skills, access, or support.
- A university website should help diverse audiences understand and navigate a complex institution; accessibility, mobile compatibility, feedback, measurement, and sustained maintenance are therefore core service requirements.

## Key Quotes
> "The website should be seen as an opportunity to help people make sense of, and navigate, a complex institution." - Fullick's service-oriented standard.

> "You can't 'set it and forget it.'" - a reader comment on continuous content maintenance.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction found. The source broadens [[InformationHierarchy]] from page- and product-level ordering to institution-wide information architecture shaped by governance.
- The appended comments qualify any design-only explanation: they attribute many visible defects to scarce staffing, distributed authority, inaccessible data, and political competition rather than lack of web expertise.
- The article and comments are qualitative and largely anecdotal; the source does not measure task completion, compare institutions systematically, or test whether centralization alone improves outcomes.
