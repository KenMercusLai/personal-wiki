## Summary
This [[SiliconValleyProductGroup]] essay argues that healthy product culture requires [[WorkplaceCollaboration]] but not unanimous approval. Product, design, and engineering should exchange information, debate trade-offs, and seek agreement, while explicit decision authority prevents risk aversion, delay, diluted products, and responsibility-eroding escalation. The source applies that distinction to [[ProductManagement]], especially user-experience versus time-to-market decisions within [[CrossFunctionalProductTeams]].

## Key Claims
- Consensus cultures can become slow, risk-averse, indecisive, and prone to design by committee even when leaders intend inclusion.
- Collaboration requires effective communication, passionate disagreement, negotiation, and integration across customer value, usability, design, and technology; it does not require everyone to agree.
- Anyone with relevant information should be expected to bring it to the product team, and people should be heard without receiving veto power.
- Explicit roles such as responsible, approving, consulted, and informed can help companies separate participation from decision authority.
- Product managers, lead designers, and lead engineers should seek agreement while retaining authority in their own domains.
- When cross-domain trade-offs remain unresolved, the product manager should make the final call and accept responsibility for the outcome.
- Escalating ordinary product trade-offs to senior management can disempower the product owner and weaken accountability.

## Key Quotes
> "these companies are confusing consensus with collaboration." - on the central cultural diagnosis.

> "Working closely certainly means communicating effectively, arguing viewpoints passionately, and negotiating trade-offs." - on collaboration without unanimity.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction found. The source strengthens the wiki's existing distinction between consultation and approval while qualifying inclusive culture: being heard and supplying relevant information do not imply unanimous consent or veto authority.
- The claim that the product manager should decide unresolved cross-domain trade-offs is a practitioner prescription, not a universal governance rule; team topology, risk, expertise, and regulated decision rights may justify other accountable owners.
