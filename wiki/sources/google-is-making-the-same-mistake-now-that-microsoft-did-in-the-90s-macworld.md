## Summary
[[JasonSnell]] argues that cross-platform applications should respect the conventions of the operating system on which they run rather than impose the vendor's home-platform design language. He compares Google's 2016 use of [[MaterialDesign]] in iOS applications with Microsoft's Windows-derived Office 6 for Mac, treating both as cases where vendor-wide consistency displaced local familiarity and provoked user resistance. The article is an opinionated historical analogy rather than usability research, but it supplies a clear argument for [[PlatformNativeDesign]] and notes early signs that Google was restoring iOS-specific icons.

## Key Claims
- Cross-platform consistency is not automatically user-centered: a uniform vendor interface can conflict with the learned conventions of each host operating system.
- Microsoft's 1993 Mac Office release replaced Mac-oriented applications with Windows-codebase ports; the author says users resisted Word 6 and that Office 98 later returned toward Mac conventions.
- Google's iOS applications used recognizable [[MaterialDesign]] choices such as a floating action button, vertical overflow dots, and card-style menus rather than iOS-native equivalents.
- [[PlatformNativeDesign]] does not forbid innovation; it distinguishes new interactions that solve a platform-relevant problem from importing another operating system's conventions for brand consistency.
- Apple Music's use of Android-specific share and overflow icons is offered as a positive cross-platform comparison, while Google Play Music is criticized for looking substantially the same on iOS and Android.
- The author observed Google replacing Android's share icon with the iOS share icon in some iOS applications, suggesting that product-wide identity and platform fit can be rebalanced.

## Key Quotes
> "once they've chosen a platform, they deserve consistency" - the article's central case for respecting host-platform conventions.

> "what Google's doing with its iOS apps isn't innovation" - the author's distinction between interaction invention and cross-platform transplantation.

## Contradictions
- The source conflicts with a strong interpretation of cross-platform design-system consistency: it argues that consistency across a vendor's products can be less valuable than consistency with the host operating system.
- The argument relies on one practitioner's historical memory and interface judgment; it provides no usability study, adoption data, or evidence that every user prefers native conventions.
- The three in-article comparison images for Google Docs, Apple Music, and Google Play Music resolve to the same 1x1 transparent PNG in the supplied archive. Their captions were readable, but the pixels could not be inspected and no visual claim depends on them.
