## Summary
[[JenniferTang]] argues that small, task-level responses can distinguish an enjoyable product from a frustrating one because people need evidence that a system noticed and acted on their input. Drawing on [[DanSaffer]]'s four-part model, the article frames [[Microinteractions]] through triggers, rules, feedback, and loops or modes, with feedback serving assurance, instruction, delight, and brand memory.

## Key Claims
- A microinteraction is the product response surrounding one small task, and its acknowledgment can make an interface feel more understandable and human.
- A microinteraction comprises a trigger, rules, feedback, and loops or modes: an event starts it, rules govern the response, feedback makes the result perceptible, and loops or modes define repetition or altered behavior.
- Feedback reduces uncertainty by showing that an action was received, work is being processed or saved, or an error needs correction.
- Contextual, continuous feedback can fit an action better than a disconnected confirmation dialog, as when swipe motion visibly tracks an email-deletion gesture.
- Feedback can do more than confirm state: it can teach system behavior, create delight, and make an interaction memorable enough to shape a brand or wider design convention.
- Microinteractions should be considered throughout design and development rather than added after the main product is built.
- Live use and iteration reveal experiential gaps that static mockups and wireframes may conceal.

## Key Quotes
> "Humans have a very real need to feel acknowledged." - on feedback as validation rather than decoration.

> "Your product needs to learn ways to be a good listener." - on making system response legible.
