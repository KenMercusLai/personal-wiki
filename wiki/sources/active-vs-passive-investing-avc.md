## Summary
Fred Wilson distinguishes passive investing from active investing by whether the investor merely supplies capital or also applies time, energy, intellect, and control. He argues that passive investing works best in liquid markets where exit is possible, while active investing often involves illiquid commitments that require concentrated attention. The essay concludes that investors should choose between being active and concentrated or passive and diversified, rather than trying to scale active investing while pretending every investment receives active attention.

## Key Claims
- Passive investing means buying assets or joining deals without taking an operating, governance, or lead role.
- Active investing means pairing capital with work, influence, and responsibility, such as board seats, development work, or lead-investor behavior.
- Passive investors should prefer liquid markets because liquidity gives them an exit when the thesis weakens.
- Active investing is harder to scale because each investment requires time, energy, and intellect.
- Investors should choose between active/concentrated and passive/diversified models instead of trying to be both at once.

## Key Quotes
> "Both models work" - Wilson emphasizes that the distinction is not a claim that only active investing can succeed.

> "It is hard to scale \"active investing.\"" - Wilson's core warning against pretending concentrated investor effort can expand indefinitely.
