## Summary
[[FirstRoundReview]] presents [[DavidLieb]]'s product lesson from [[Bump]], [[Flock]], and [[GooglePhotos]]: users can reject a technically smoother product when it makes them work too hard to understand what is happening. The article defines [[CognitiveOverheadInProductDesign]] as the logical-connection burden users must cross to contextualize a product, then argues that product teams can reduce it through visible user control, familiar patterns, consistency, and sometimes deliberate slowness. The embedded image is a speaker-stage photograph, so it adds attribution context but no separate product evidence.

## Key Claims
- [[CognitiveOverheadInProductDesign]] can matter more than interface simplicity because users need to understand what the product is doing and why.
- [[Flock]] failed partly because predictive photo sharing removed users too far from the sharing decision, while [[Bump]]'s high-friction phone bump made control and action obvious.
- Automation can increase cognitive overhead when users cannot see when, why, or how decisions are being made.
- Familiarity drives adoption: products can introduce novelty more successfully when they begin from patterns users already recognize.
- Consistency supports habit formation better than scattered feature variety, as shown by the contrast between [[WhatsApp]]'s focused messaging identity and [[TwitterMoments]] clouding [[Twitter]]'s purpose.
- Speed is not always the user goal; visible processing time can build trust when it helps users understand the work being done.
- Young, old, distracted, or impaired testers can reveal cognitive-overhead problems that product teams miss.

## Key Quotes
> "Cognitive overhead is the number of logical connections that your brain has to make to contextualize and understand what it sees." - definition Lieb uses for the product-design problem.

> "Just because something seems simpler doesn't mean it is." - Lieb's warning about automation and visible user control.
