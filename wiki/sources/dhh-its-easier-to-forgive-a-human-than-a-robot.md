## Summary
[[DavidHeinemeierHansson]] argues that matching or even outperforming average human accuracy may not be enough for AI adoption because people, firms, and legal systems may judge machine-caused errors more harshly than comparable human mistakes. Using autonomous driving, medical diagnosis, and customer support, he frames [[AutomationErrorTolerance]] as a psychological and accountability constraint alongside technical performance.

## Key Claims
- Automated systems may need to be nearly perfect rather than merely better than humans because their failures are less socially forgivable.
- [[AutonomousDrivingSafety]] depends on legitimacy as well as aggregate crash reduction: a company could face intense blame for many machine-caused deaths even if automation reduced total deaths.
- AI diagnosis could outperform an average physician yet remain commercially vulnerable if its residual mistakes concentrate malpractice liability on the system provider.
- Customer-support automation has lower physical stakes, but a small rate of confidently wrong answers may still damage trust and word of mouth.
- Familiarity could eventually make people more sympathetic to machine errors, but the author leaves that possibility unresolved.

## Key Quotes
> "The math is easy, the human element is hard" - on why aggregate safety gains do not settle adoption or liability.

> "Maybe fallible humans have an inherent advantage over AI by being forgivable?" - the article's central question.

## Contradictions
- Qualifies performance-only accounts of AI adoption: lower aggregate error may not be sufficient when blame, liability, trust, and perceived agency are distributed differently.
- The article offers thought experiments and company experience rather than comparative behavioral, legal, medical, or deployment evidence, so its proposed error thresholds should not be treated as measured requirements.
