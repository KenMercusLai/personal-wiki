## Summary
The author argues that leaders often praise failure abstractly while hiding, externalizing, or denying their own specific mistakes. A failed webcast provides the central case: only after disclosing the poor turnout and hearing friends' criticism did the author identify missing community buy-in, change the organizing approach, and help create a successful Iowa Creativity Summit. The article connects candid failure ownership to team connection, learning, tolerance for experimentation, and resilience after later setbacks, while a reader comment extends the incentive problem to classrooms where grades punish the failures that educators claim to value. This file duplicates the article already represented by [[dont-be-a-hypocrite-about-failure-2]], apart from export anchors and image-path naming.

## Key Claims
- Praising other people's failures is easier than admitting one's own, so general pro-failure rhetoric does not demonstrate [[FailureOwnership]].
- Concealing a failed event delayed access to outside perspectives; disclosing it exposed the missing community buy-in and supported a different organizing approach.
- Leaders who admit concrete mistakes can appear more credible and approachable than leaders who perform total competence.
- Failure produces learning only when people examine it, accept relevant responsibility, seek outside feedback, and change subsequent action.
- A leader's visible discomfort with staff failure can suppress reporting and experimentation even without formal punishment, weakening [[PsychologicalSafety]].
- Remembering earlier failures as survivable can make later setbacks easier to process without denying their cost.
- A reader's education example highlights an incentive contradiction: saying failure supports learning while attaching unavoidable grade or accountability penalties can discourage creative risk-taking.

## Key Quotes
> "Praising failure in the abstract is one thing. It's very different when you are the failure." - on the gap between approving failure and owning it personally.

> "Failure is only positive when you learn something important from it and then make the necessary adjustments." - on disclosure and changed action as conditions for learning.
