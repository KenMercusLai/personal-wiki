## Summary
[[BenFoxRubin]] tours [[AmazonFallRiverFulfillmentCenter]], a 1.2-million-square-foot non-sort facility designed for large, heavy, low-volume, and irregular products. The article shows [[OversizedEcommerceFulfillment]] as a distinct operating system: fewer conveyors and no warehouse robots, more human handling and powered industrial trucks, randomized storage adapted to awkward inventory, and box-on-demand equipment that cuts corrugated cardboard to measured dimensions. Amazon's operation and [[Wayfair]]'s dedicated delivery network illustrate how online retailers build specialized capacity to extend convenience into categories that are costly and complex to store, pack, deliver, carry inside, or assemble.

## Key Claims
- Amazon's Fall River non-sort facility handled big and bulky inventory through specialized storage, forklifts, hands-on labor, and custom packaging rather than the conveyor- and robot-heavy design of a standard sort fulfillment center.
- The 1,300-employee warehouse reportedly shipped 80,000 to 100,000 boxes per day, with peak-holiday volume capable of nearly doubling.
- Three box-on-demand machines operated continuously and could construct up to 200 custom boxes per hour by measuring items and cutting one of three corrugated-cardboard sizes to fit.
- Oversized e-commerce extends beyond warehouse picking: scheduled delivery, apartment carry-in, unboxing, and assembly make the last mile more complex and expensive than doorstep parcel delivery.
- Online retailers responded through specialized carrier partnerships, compact packaging, dedicated delivery networks, freight charges, and in-store pickup rather than one universal fulfillment model.
- Amazon's broad selection can strengthen customer habit and seller access even when individual bulky products are low-volume and expensive to ship.
- [[StandSteady]] illustrates the seller-side value of outsourced fulfillment: founder Day Martin said transferring storage and shipping to Amazon in 2014 was necessary to grow beyond packing and moving 20-pound desks herself.

## Key Quotes
> "It was the only way for me to grow the business" - Day Martin on outsourcing Stand Steady fulfillment to Amazon.

> "Consumers are getting more and more used to that level of convenience" - Wayfair's Jane Carpenter on expanding delivery expectations.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction found. The source qualifies broad warehouse-automation narratives: Amazon's standard sort centers used conveyors and mobile robots, while Fall River's non-sort model relied on substantially more human handling because item size and shape resisted the same automation pattern.
- The article is a May 2018 operational snapshot. Its facility count, staffing, throughput, retailer networks, e-commerce share, and service descriptions should not be treated as current.
- Both remote CNET images were unavailable during ingest. The captioned Rich Hanna portrait appears to repeat the prose and was omitted as non-evidentiary; the generic title image could not be independently interpreted, so no image-derived claim or retained asset was added.
