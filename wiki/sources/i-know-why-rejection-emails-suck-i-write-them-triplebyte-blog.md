## Summary
[[KelseyPiper]] reflects on writing more than 3,000 individualized rejection emails for [[Triplebyte]] and argues that their rarity is driven less by technical-feedback liability alone than by the labor, process maturity, and difficult candidate reactions involved. Triplebyte reportedly improved responses by describing what candidates demonstrated rather than claiming what they knew, emphasizing decision-relevant weaknesses, resolving apparent contradictions, and pairing criticism with current free resources. The result is a conditional case for [[CandidateRejectionFeedback]] inside a standardized [[HiringSystemDesign]], not evidence that every rejected applicant should receive detailed feedback.

## Key Claims
- Useful individualized feedback requires detailed interviewer evidence, review for relevance and appropriateness, tailored advice, maintained learning resources, and repeated process iteration.
- Standardization and a dedicated editor can reduce the workload, but even Triplebyte limited individualized emails to candidates who completed a two-hour interview rather than everyone who took its online quiz.
- The author argues that truthful feedback about demonstrated technical performance need not create the legal risk companies often assume, while raw, improvised, discriminatory, or bias-signaling notes can create real exposure.
- Detailed rejection can make candidates angrier when it confirms that they felt misunderstood, appears contradictory, or turns a limited interview observation into a broad judgment of ability.
- Wording should distinguish failure to demonstrate knowledge during the interview from lack of knowledge, because interview performance can reflect vocabulary, question interpretation, or on-the-spot communication as well as subject mastery.
- Feedback across the whole interview should identify which observations actually drove the decision so candidates do not infer that every noted weakness was disqualifying.
- Triplebyte reportedly moved from frequent angry replies to mostly positive responses after revising its language and specificity, but the article supplies no response counts, comparison group, legal outcomes, or independent candidate evidence.

## Key Quotes
> "No one enjoys getting rejected, and getting rejected in more detail is not significantly better." - on the emotional limit of information alone.

> "It made quite a difference just to phrase the feedback in a way which acknowledged all those possibilities." - on separating observed interview performance from inferred ability.

## Contradictions
- The source does not directly contradict the wiki's candidate-centered hiring framework, but it qualifies the assumption that more transparency automatically improves candidate experience: detailed feedback can deepen anger when evidence, wording, and decision logic are weak.
- The legal discussion is the author's account of one conversation with an unnamed employment lawyer, not jurisdiction-specific legal advice or outcome evidence. The article's safer claim is comparative: reviewed technical-performance feedback differs materially from forwarding raw notes that mention protected characteristics or imply biased decision-making.
- Reported improvement from angry to mostly positive replies is observational and self-selected. Candidates who respond may not represent all recipients, and the article does not report complaint rates, later interview performance, hiring outcomes, or the cost per email.
