## Summary
[[AndrewChen]] argues that every extra click, field, download, tutorial, or signup demand in a critical product flow spends scarce user intent. The source frames [[ProductFlowFriction]] as a tradeoff rather than a universal evil: teams should remove steps that delay the product's "magic," but they may add friction when it improves the eventual experience, preserves a reactivation channel, or brings collaborators into the product. The inspected header image is illustrative only, showing tap/cursor/click icons rather than additional evidence.

## Key Claims
- Critical flows should minimize clicks, required fields, tutorials, splash screens, and delayed signup paths because large portions of users drop at each ask.
- Early [[Uber]] signup improved when credit-card collection was moved later and phone-number signup became possible.
- The value of onboarding is getting users to the product's core experience quickly, not making them watch explanations or complete forms.
- Friction can be justified when it improves the user's later experience, such as installing a richer app, creating a notification channel, inviting teammates, or capturing a way to return.
- Lowering signup friction can increase raw signups while reducing average intent, so more signups do not necessarily mean proportionally more paying customers.

## Key Quotes
> "every click drops off a huge number of users" - on spending user intent carefully.

> "the only acceptable forms of friction are ones that ultimately enhance the users ability to have a great experience" - on when friction is justified.
