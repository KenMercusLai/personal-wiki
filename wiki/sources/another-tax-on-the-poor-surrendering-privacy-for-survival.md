## Summary
This Fast Company article argues that privacy is distributed unequally: poor and marginalized people often must surrender more data, accept more monitoring, and absorb more concrete harm in order to access benefits, work, housing, credit, or basic services. Through [[MicheleGilman]], [[MaryMadden]], [[ElissaRedmiles]], [[DataSociety]], and [[OurDataBodies]], it connects welfare home visits, benefits verification, workplace monitoring, data brokers, opaque algorithms, scams, and reputation-management markets into a broader [[PrivacyPovertyDivide]]. The inspected illustration shows apartment towers under oversized watching eyes, visually reinforcing the source's frame that poverty is lived under surveillance.

## Key Claims
- [[PrivacyPovertyDivide]] makes privacy a material class issue rather than only a consumer preference or abstract civil liberty.
- [[WelfareSurveillance]] has deep historical continuity, from overseers of the poor to home visits, biometric collection, drug tests, fingerprinting, and database checks for public benefits.
- [[DataBrokerPersistence]] means expunged, outdated, or incomplete records can keep affecting housing, jobs, and opportunities after state records are corrected.
- [[AlgorithmicDecisionOpacity]] makes welfare, lending, employment, and benefits denials hard to contest because affected people and lawyers may not be able to inspect or challenge the decision logic.
- [[SurveillanceGap]] shows that exclusion from data systems can also be harmful when people lack records, addresses, documentation, or formal employment proof needed to claim rights and resources.
- [[PrivacyProtectionResourceInequality]] means low-income users face higher time, skill, money, and advice costs when trying to use security protections, scrub records, or avoid scams.

## Key Quotes
> "I can't cross-examine an algorithm." - Gilman on opaque benefits decisions.

> "There is attention to people who are marginalized, who experience privacy differently." - Gilman on privacy legislation.

> "There's this direct trade-off between the amount of time and the resources you have and the extent to which you can protect certain information." - Madden on privacy self-defense.
