## Summary
[[GarryTan]] argues that co-founder conflict becomes dangerous when founders either avoid necessary disagreement or fight constantly without clear decision boundaries. Drawing on his [[Posterous]] experience, advising startups, and coaching language from [[CameronYarbrough]] and [[JohnGottman]], the article frames healthy conflict as maintained relationship infrastructure rather than a sign that the company is broken. The two remote TechCrunch image references are variants of the same inspected illustrative conflict photograph and did not add independent factual evidence.

## Key Claims
- Startup success can hide co-founder relationship problems until growth slows and the company most needs coordinated decision-making.
- Prior friendship or long shared history is useful but insufficient; founders have to actively maintain the current working relationship.
- Avoiding conflict by reducing contact, withholding disagreement, or preserving the appearance of harmony can make founder rifts worse.
- Constant conflict often signals unclear ownership, so founders should assign explicit decision domains and stop debating after the owner decides.
- Fair conflict is collaborative, data-based, and team-oriented; it requires speaking up without bulldozing other viewpoints.
- Outside help from trusted friends, investors, mentors, executive coaches, or group support can preserve founder partnerships before disputes become company-killing.
- Co-founder disputes are described as a leading early startup killer when too little or too much conflict remains unresolved.

## Key Quotes
> "history is not enough" - on why prior friendship does not replace relationship maintenance

> "Successful co-founders actually embrace conflict" - on the article's preferred conflict norm

> "Co-founder disputes have historically been one of the top reasons startups fail" - on startup risk
