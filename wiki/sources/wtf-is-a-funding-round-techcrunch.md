## Summary
TechCrunch's explainer answers the basic questions about a [[StartupFundingRound]] in Q&A form: investors hand a startup cash in exchange for an ownership stake, the money is not a loan and is never repaid, and the payoff arrives only if the company is acquired or goes public. It walks through how [[StartupValuation]] is set for early and later companies, why the highest possible valuation is not automatically good, what equity compensation means for employees, how seed and later lettered rounds differ, and how [[AngelInvesting]] differs from the pooled funds described by [[VentureCapitalFundStructure]]. It closes with the practical founder questions - what the money is spent on, how often to raise, how to reach and choose investors - and a blunt base-rate warning that most startups fail even after raising millions.

## Key Claims
- A funding round is an ownership exchange rather than a loan: investors receive a stake and lose their money if the company fails, while the startup carries no repayment obligation.
- Equity is the default because young companies have a hard time qualifying for bank loans, even though loans preserve ownership and are sometimes taken alongside equity from lenders such as Silicon Valley Bank or Square 1 Bank, which may also take a small equity stake.
- Valuation is how much the company is judged to be worth, and for pre-revenue startups it is mostly a bet on estimated market size; later valuations weigh revenue, growth trajectory, and the likelihood of an IPO or acquisition.
- The highest possible valuation is not necessarily desirable, because it raises the outcome the company must deliver: a startup that raises at a $500 million valuation and sells for $200 million loses money for the investors who bought at the higher price and hurts employees who joined at that valuation.
- Employee equity carries the same upside and risk as investor equity: the payoff is greatest for early joiners, holdings are usually paper money until a liquidity event, and some startups let employees sell shares in secondary offerings priced off the latest valuation.
- Rounds are stage-labeled - seed is the first round and each later round takes the next letter - and many investors specialize in a single stage as seed-stage or late-stage funds.
- Venture capital is pooled money: a VC firm manages a fund supplied by LPs such as financial institutions, high-net-worth individuals, pension funds, and university funds, typically across a ten-year fund life, and returns are earned through exits such as IPOs and M&A with the firm keeping a portion of the upside.
- Fund proceeds are meant to accelerate growth through hiring, sales and marketing, and production costs, and many startups raise roughly every one to two years, often before the cash is urgently needed.
- Investor access depends heavily on references and networks rather than only on the quality of the business plan, and investors describe the decision as being about the people they can trust through unforeseeable obstacles.
- Investor choice matters after traction arrives: firm reputation can help with partnerships and hiring, board members with industry or scaling experience can help with problem-solving and introductions, and celebrity investors can add publicity.

## Key Quotes
> "Investors are giving the startup money in exchange for an ownership stake. They are hoping that the share price will go up as the startup grows into a larger company." - on why startups take equity rather than debt.

> "Many investors will tell you that their decision to invest is 'all about the people.'" - on what founders are really being evaluated for.

> "While some startups make billions of dollars, the majority of them fail." - on the base rate behind the whole asset class.

## Contradictions
- Qualifies [[EmployeeEquityRisk]] and [[StartupEquityTransparency]] by noting that secondary offerings can give employees a partial liquidity path before an IPO or acquisition, and by framing valuations as generally rising over time so that early joiners capture more of the upside.
- Reinforces [[StartupFailurePatterns]] and [[VentureCapitalPortfolioSizing]] on base rates: TechCrunch says most startups fail, and that the rare winners must cover the fund's losses.
- The source does not reconcile its "no payback" framing with the fact that a bank loan taken alongside equity still must be repaid with interest, and it does not mention venture debt, revenue-based financing, or any instruments besides equity and conventional loans.
