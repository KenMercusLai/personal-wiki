## Summary
Late Night Coding argues that first-wave customer-service chatbots overpromised because they hid ordinary interface structure behind brittle conversation. The source qualifies optimistic [[ConversationalUI]] and [[ProductFlowFriction]] arguments: chat can reduce app-install or typing effort, but support contexts need adaptation, context, visible affordances, and escalation that scripted bots often lack. It separates Siri- and Alexa-style voice assistants from customer-support bots by noting that voice is useful for low-stakes, hands-free, repeatable tasks even when recognition occasionally fails.

## Key Claims
- [[ConversationalUI]] fails in customer support when bots mimic human service but cannot improvise, adapt to context, or handle non-standard problems.
- Chatbots can reproduce the frustration of phone-tree support by forcing users through scripted paths while still often ending at human escalation.
- Visible menus, forms, and pages can outperform chat because users can scan available options, understand required information, and decide whether to engage.
- Quick-reply buttons partly repair chat's hidden-interface problem, but they also concede that pure conversation is not enough.
- [[VoiceAssistantUX]] works better for Siri- or Alexa-like tasks because speech removes typing and enables multitasking, especially when the stakes are low and retrying is cheap.
- The article leaves room for future AI progress but argues that 2017 chatbots were too immature for many customer-facing support roles.

## Key Quotes
> "The problem is that it doesn't work." - on the gap between chatbot marketing promises and customer-service reality.

> "conventional UIs give their users a huge advantage over chats - you can see them." - on why hidden conversational interfaces can be worse than menus or forms.

## Connections
- [[ConversationalUI]] - central critique of customer-service chatbots as brittle, hidden user interfaces.
- [[VoiceAssistantUX]] - Siri and Alexa are treated as more defensible because voice reduces typing and supports hands-free low-stakes tasks.
- [[ProductFlowFriction]] - qualifies low-friction chatbot arguments by showing how hidden options and precision demands can create new friction.
- [[FacebookMessenger]] - Messenger quick replies are cited as a platform response to user confusion about what bots can do.
- [[MessagingAsPlatform]] - qualifies the first-wave platform thesis by arguing that bot interfaces often only replicate website or app features worse.

## Contradictions
- Qualifies [[chat-is-the-new-browser-ted-livingston-medium]]: Livingston's 2016 source treats chatbots as a promising low-friction platform, while this 2017 source argues that customer-service bots often hide useful UI, fail to adapt, and make support worse.
- Qualifies [[ConversationalUI]]: later LLM-era interfaces may solve more natural-language understanding problems, but this source documents why pre-LLM scripted bots were poor customer-service substitutes.
