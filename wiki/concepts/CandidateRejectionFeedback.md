## Definition
[[CandidateRejectionFeedback]] is the communication of individualized, decision-relevant observations and improvement guidance to an unsuccessful job candidate after an assessment or interview.

## Current Synthesis
The Triplebyte case makes rejection feedback a system capability rather than a courtesy added at the end of an interview. Responsible feedback depends on structured interviewer evidence, a reviewer who can turn that evidence into an appropriate explanation, clear separation between observations and inferences, maintained learning resources, and enough time to revise the process when recipients expose ambiguity or contradiction. Without those conditions, greater detail can amplify rather than repair a candidate's sense of being misunderstood.

The account supports bounded language: explain what the candidate did not demonstrate in a particular interview instead of declaring what the person does not know. When reporting several dimensions, distinguish context from causes by emphasizing the observations that actually drove the decision. Specificity also matters when one area contains both strengths and weaknesses; unexplained mixed judgments can look like inattentive boilerplate even when both are true.

Legal risk is conditional in this account. Reviewed, truthful technical-performance feedback is presented as different from raw notes that mention protected characteristics or expose a biased process. That distinction is plausible but rests on an unnamed lawyer's view and one employer's practice, so it is not jurisdiction-independent legal guidance. The broader current judgment is operational: offer detailed rejection feedback only at stages where the organization has evidence quality, review capacity, and learning loops sufficient to do it responsibly.

## Key Claims
- Detailed feedback is useful only when supported by structured evidence, editorial review, and enough capacity to maintain advice and improve the process.
- Feedback should describe what was demonstrated in a bounded assessment rather than infer a candidate's total knowledge or ability.
- Messages should identify decision-relevant weaknesses and explain apparently mixed evidence so recipients can distinguish causes from incidental observations.
- More information does not automatically create a better candidate experience; detail can intensify anger when it confirms misunderstanding or appears inconsistent.
- Legal exposure depends on content and process: reviewed technical observations differ from careless notes that reveal or imply discriminatory criteria.
- A tiered policy can reserve individualized feedback for candidates who completed substantial assessment when universal coverage would exceed available resources.
- Reported positive reactions after iteration are encouraging process evidence, not proof of candidate benefit, predictive validity, or legal safety.

## Evidence
- Production requirements: [[i-know-why-rejection-emails-suck-i-write-them-triplebyte-blog]] describes interviewer ratings, subjective notes, central compilation, appropriateness review, tailored advice, and periodic resource maintenance.
- Scope and capacity: [[i-know-why-rejection-emails-suck-i-write-them-triplebyte-blog]] says Triplebyte sent individualized feedback after a two-hour interview but not to every online-quiz participant.
- Observation boundaries: [[i-know-why-rejection-emails-suck-i-write-them-triplebyte-blog]] reports fewer negative reactions after wording shifted from poor understanding to failure to communicate strong understanding during the interview.
- Decision and consistency: [[i-know-why-rejection-emails-suck-i-write-them-triplebyte-blog]] says messages improved when they emphasized decisive sections and specified how communication could be strong in one respect and weak in another.
- Failure modes: [[i-know-why-rejection-emails-suck-i-write-them-triplebyte-blog]] describes angry replies, disputes over the decision, perceived contradiction, and confirmation that the interviewer had misunderstood the candidate.
- Legal boundary: [[i-know-why-rejection-emails-suck-i-write-them-triplebyte-blog]] distinguishes reviewed technical feedback from ad hoc release of notes about appearance, gender, race, or other bias-signaling material.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is grounded in one 2018 first-person article from the employer operating the process. Its author reports more than 3,000 messages and improved reactions, but provides no message corpus, time accounting, response denominator, candidate survey, complaint rate, demographic analysis, later performance, reapplication outcome, or comparison with a concise rejection. Replies are self-selected and may overrepresent strongly positive or negative reactions.

Even carefully bounded feedback can be wrong because interview evidence is incomplete and interviewer interpretation can be biased. Free learning resources do not establish that a candidate lacked the relevant skill, and positive replies do not establish fairness. Legal obligations and litigation risks vary by jurisdiction and fact pattern; the source's unnamed-lawyer conversation is a hypothesis about one kind of technical feedback, not legal advice.

## What Changed
- Established rejection feedback as a process-maturity and evidence-quality problem, not merely a choice between transparency and silence.
- Added bounded wording, causal emphasis, contradiction resolution, and tiered coverage as design requirements.
- Separated reviewed technical-performance feedback from raw notes that can expose bias or protected-characteristic commentary.
