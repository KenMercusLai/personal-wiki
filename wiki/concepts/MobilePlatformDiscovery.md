## Definition
[[MobilePlatformDiscovery]] is the problem of how users find, choose, and return to services when traffic, curation, search, app stores, notifications, operating systems, and social graphs all shape attention.

## Current Synthesis
The Evans source treats discovery as one of mobile's unresolved strategic bottlenecks. Search lets users retrieve things they already know they want, while print, retail, and portals once provided demand generation and filtering. In mobile, that function is fragmented across app stores, OS-level surfaces, notifications, messaging, maps, social feeds, and platform owners. The Mobile Dev Memo source adds advertiser-facing discovery inside messaging: promoted chats, Discover icons, Moments placements, sponsored feed units, and sticker stores let brands appear where users already spend attention. The Sensor Tower source adds a durability warning for app-store chart discovery: even reaching No. 1 on U.S. iPhone downloads usually did not preserve high placement, because most first-time No. 1 apps in 2015-2016 left the top 25 within a month.

## Key Claims
- Search solves intent retrieval better than demand generation or awareness.
- Mobile platforms are not neutral because operating systems and app stores can shape what services users discover.
- User acquisition depends on traffic routing as much as on service quality.
- New runtimes such as messaging, assistants, maps, and notifications are partly attempts to own discovery.
- The app-versus-web decision has a product-discovery test: whether users want a service on their home screen.
- Messaging apps can sell discovery surfaces for brands through tabs, feeds, content channels, and sticker stores.
- App-store chart position can create intense but short-lived visibility unless the product has durable demand or incumbent-scale brand strength.

## Evidence
- Search limits: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] says search can only give users what they already knew they wanted.
- Lost filters: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] contrasts older print and retail discovery with the internet's abundance problem.
- Platform power: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] argues OS control matters because the OS can shape service discovery more than the desktop web did.
- Runtime search: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] frames Siri, Google Now, messaging, maps, and notifications as possible new discovery models.
- App test: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] says brands and publishers should ask whether users want their icon on the home screen.
- Brand discovery: [[advertising-models-in-mobile-messaging-apps-mobile-dev-memo]] says [[Kik]], [[Snapchat]], [[WeChat]], [[Tango]], and [[LINE]] expose brands through promoted chats, Discover, Moments, sponsored content, and stickers.
- Chart volatility: [[74-of-no-1-iphone-apps-dont-last-a-month-in-the-top-25]] reports that 74% of first-time No. 1 U.S. iPhone apps in 2015-2016 left the top 25 within 30 days.
- Incumbent pressure: [[74-of-no-1-iphone-apps-dont-last-a-month-in-the-top-25]] says massive apps such as [[Facebook]], [[Instagram]], [[Snapchat]], and [[FacebookMessenger]] made long-term top-chart presence difficult for most other apps.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources identify discovery as strategically central but do not settle which discovery model wins. The Sensor Tower evidence is limited to first-time No. 1 U.S. iPhone download-chart apps in 2015 and 2016, so it should not be generalized to all countries, categories, stores, paid rankings, revenue rankings, or later App Store eras. The older sources also predate later shifts in recommendation feeds, short video, AI assistants, privacy controls, messaging commerce, and app-store regulation.

## What Changed
- Created the concept for mobile-era discovery and user-acquisition control.
- Added messaging-app ad placements as concrete brand-discovery surfaces.
- Added app-store chart volatility as evidence that top-ranking acquisition does not automatically create durable visibility.
