## Definition
[[MarketBasedCompensation]] sets salary and equity through an employer's estimate of external role demand, individual capability, company stage, and current labor-market conditions rather than through tenure or fixed historical rules alone.

## Current Synthesis
The sources describe market-based compensation at two levels. eShares' 2015 framework makes marketability the stated basis of salary plus equity: it rejects automatic annual raises, proposes a market review after nine months and annually thereafter, permits pay to move down as well as up, and targets the 75th percentile. Wilson's employee-equity framework narrows the question to grant sizing after a startup's first exceptional hires. It groups roles, multiplies base salary by a role-specific factor to produce a target equity value, and converts that value into fully diluted shares using a current company value.

Together, the methods treat compensation as a periodically recalibrated system rather than a one-time negotiation, but neither makes pay objective by declaration. Role definitions, comparison groups, geography, market datasets, company stage, valuation, skill assessment, salary history, and negotiation power all shape the output. Wilson's explicit warning that his 2010 multipliers became below-market illustrates why fixed tables decay. Salary-linked equity can also compound prior salary inequity. A credible system therefore needs current benchmark data, transparent leveling and valuation assumptions, pay-equity and bias review, consistent governance, and a clear process for challenging assessments.

## Key Claims
- External role demand, individual capability, and company stage can inform compensation more directly than automatic tenure increments.
- Periodic market review is necessary because labor-market prices and company conditions change.
- A role multiplier can translate base salary into a target equity-grant value, but it is a policy input rather than an enduring fact.
- Learning and career capital may strengthen an employee value proposition but do not replace fair current pay.
- Market data still requires choices about role matching, geography, percentile, company valuation, equity terms, and skill evaluation.
- Transparency, consistent leveling, bias controls, pay-equity analysis, and appeal mechanisms are necessary if market pricing is meant to reduce favoritism.

## Evidence
- Pay basis and cadence: [[eshares-101-owners-manual-blog-by-carta-medium]] says eShares based salary and equity on a person's skill market and level, with review after nine months and every twelve months thereafter.
- Market position: [[eshares-101-owners-manual-blog-by-carta-medium]] reports a 75th-percentile target and argues that learning opportunities help attract people who are not optimizing only for immediate salary.
- Equity translation: [[employee-equity-how-much-avc]] multiplies salary by a role-bracket factor to produce a target dollar value before calculating the number of shares.
- Stage boundary: [[employee-equity-how-much-avc]] treats the first several key hires as bespoke and moves to a formula only after a core operating team exists.
- Market drift: [[employee-equity-how-much-avc]] warns that its 2010 multipliers became below-market, with the largest movement among senior-team grants.
- Governance boundary: [[employee-equity-how-much-avc]] excludes CEO and COO grants from the ordinary framework and assigns them to the board.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources offer policy rationales but no representative pay data, employee perspectives, retention outcomes, bias audits, or demographic fairness analysis. Downward salary adjustment can transfer market volatility to employees, while salary-linked equity can reproduce unequal negotiation histories. Wilson's "best value" is an internal company estimate rather than a liquid common-share price, and his formula omits strike price, tax, vesting loss, dilution, preferences, debt, and liquidity probability. The claim that strong candidates prioritize learning over pay risks moralizing different financial needs, and stale multipliers show that a transparent formula can still produce systematically wrong outcomes.

## What Changed
- Extended the concept from recurring salary-and-equity review to stage-sensitive employee grant sizing.
- Added role multipliers, company value, and fully diluted shares as explicit but contestable compensation inputs.
- Added market drift and salary-equity propagation as reasons that formulaic pay still requires periodic fairness review.
