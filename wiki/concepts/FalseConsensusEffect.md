## Definition
[[FalseConsensusEffect]] is the tendency to assume that other people think, believe, or behave more like oneself than they actually do.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats false consensus as both a bias and a useful survey signal. Because people partly project their own views when predicting others, indirect questions about what peers believe can reveal views or behaviors respondents might not admit directly. The same projection also helps make respondents with better metaknowledge stand out, because they can anticipate disagreement more accurately.

## Key Claims
- People often use their own answer as an anchor when predicting the crowd's answer.
- That projection can reveal hidden beliefs, preferences, or behaviors.
- False consensus makes poor metaknowledge visible when respondents cannot imagine credible disagreement.
- The bias is not always irrational, but it becomes misleading when one's own view is the only reference point.

## Evidence
- Self-anchoring: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] says respondents in a Metaculus Sanders experiment tended to predict crowd probabilities close to their own estimates.
- Hidden preference signal: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] uses examples of socially embarrassing preferences and corruption accusations to show indirect revelation.
- Disagreement blindness: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] says people who wrongly thought Chicago was Illinois's capital struggled to imagine other answers, while those who knew Springfield better predicted classmates' errors.
- Qualified rationality: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] notes that starting from oneself can be reasonable as long as it does not become the only reference point.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source uses false consensus instrumentally rather than treating it as purely harmful. Its usefulness depends on survey design that can compare self-reports, predictions about others, and actual response distributions.
