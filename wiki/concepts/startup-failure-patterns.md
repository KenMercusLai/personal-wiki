Startup failure patterns are the repeated failure modes that appear across shutdown notices and post-mortems. The CB Insights compilation shows that startup death is rarely explained by a single mistake. A company can have good technology, press attention, venture backing, and sincere customer enthusiasm while still failing if the business cannot convert those signals into repeat use, revenue, financing, and operational control.

The clearest pattern is dependence on external capital before the business has proven enough economics to survive without it. Many companies shut down after a financing round, acquisition, or strategic investment collapsed late, leaving no runway for a new plan. This makes fundraising risk part of the product strategy: if a company needs the next round to manufacture, expand, or finish the product, missed timing can become fatal.

Another pattern is false demand. Several companies learned that people admired the idea but did not repeat the behavior, pay often enough, or change habits. This turns product-market fit into a behavioral claim, not a sentiment claim. A startup needs evidence that users will keep acting when the novelty fades and when cheaper, easier, or already-installed alternatives are available.

The source also highlights market and ecosystem timing. Hardware, AR/VR, healthcare, solar, battery, and other deep-tech companies often needed more market maturity, regulatory clarity, manufacturing capacity, or project finance than their runway allowed. Being early can look like vision while the company is alive and like mis-timed capital allocation after it dies.

Finally, the compilation shows why startup risk is often systemic. Platform policy changes, dominant incumbents, legal disputes, licensing costs, worker-classification rules, healthcare billing scrutiny, food safety rules, and distribution-channel dependence can invalidate a strategy even when execution improves. Useful post-mortems therefore ask not only what the team did wrong, but which dependency made the company fragile.

## Pattern Checklist

- The company needs outside financing before the core business can sustain itself.
- Users like the product but do not repeat, pay, or recruit others at the required rate.
- Growth spending happens before retention, unit economics, or operational quality are stable.
- The market requires education, infrastructure, or regulation to change faster than the company can wait.
- A single platform, channel, supplier, partner, or regulator can change the company's survival odds.
- The product creates operational complexity that software alone cannot absorb.
