## Definition
[[ProgrammingAsArt]] is the claim that programming or software can function as art when aesthetic experience or expressive meaning is a primary purpose, rather than whenever implementation merely requires creativity or permits stylistic choice.

## Current Synthesis
Dietrich separates a work's purpose from the creativity of producing it. On his aesthetic-purpose test, some programming clearly becomes art when the software sells an experience, with entertainment-oriented video games as the main example. Most business software instead automates work, lowers cost, or improves efficiency; code can be elegant and its construction highly creative without aesthetic response becoming the product's governing end.

The preserved discussion makes the boundary less tidy. Commenters propose self-expression, metaphor, beauty in clear algorithms, and collaborative style as artistic dimensions of functional code. Others describe ordinary enterprise programming as craft or engineering because it uses learned tools under requirements, standards, correctness constraints, and team conventions. The strongest synthesis is therefore contextual: “art,” “craft,” “science,” and “engineering” identify different purposes and practices that may coexist, but none describes all programming.

## Key Claims
- Open-ended choice and creativity are insufficient by themselves to classify programming as art.
- Programming most plausibly functions as art when aesthetic or expressive experience is a primary end of the software.
- Functional software can contain elegance, style, and self-expression without being primarily an artwork.
- Craft and engineering often describe routine software work better because requirements, correctness, maintainability, risk, and repeatable practice constrain the result.
- One software project can combine artistic, scientific, engineering, craft, and routine production dimensions.

## Evidence
- Purpose test: [[is-programming-art-daedtech]] defines art through aesthetic purpose and distinguishes entertainment from instructional or efficiency-seeking artifacts.
- Artistic case: [[is-programming-art-daedtech]] identifies video games as software sold substantially through visual, behavioral, and entertainment appeal.
- Instrumental case: [[is-programming-art-daedtech]] uses a corporate SharePoint plugin to show software justified by labor savings rather than aesthetic response.
- Alternative classifications: [[is-programming-art-daedtech]] and its comments describe programming as algorithmic delegation, optimization, craft, engineering, science, and sometimes art.
- Boundary challenges: [[is-programming-art-daedtech]] preserves arguments from self-expression, metaphor, code beauty, correctness, standardization, and safety-critical rigor.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept rests on one author's working definition and a self-selected comment thread, not an empirical or philosophical consensus about art. An aesthetic-purpose test may exclude architecture, design, craft, literature with instructional aims, and other functional artifacts commonly treated as art. Video games also mix entertainment with education, competition, sociality, and commerce. Conversely, calling elegant implementation “art” can conflate the maker's experience, the maintainers' judgment, the user-facing product, and the artifact's primary purpose.

## What Changed
- Created a purpose-based synthesis that distinguishes creative method from artistic end.
- Preserved craft, engineering, expression, correctness, and mixed-purpose challenges to an aesthetics-only boundary.
