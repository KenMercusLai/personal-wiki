## Definition
The [[PeakEndRule]] is the tendency for retrospective evaluation of an experience to depend disproportionately on its most intense moment and its ending rather than on a time-weighted average of the whole experience.

## Current Synthesis
The available essay presents the rule as a consequence of reconstructive memory. People do not replay every instant of a trip, story, project, or relationship with equal weight; salient moments become anchors from which an overall narrative is rebuilt. A peak can be joy, pain, anger, shock, or grief, and the effective ending can be the last lingering question or feeling rather than the literal final event.

The source also separates two uses. In elective entertainment, a person seeking an emotional experience may reasonably screen for peaks and endings they are willing to inhabit. In work, the same shortcut can misgrade a project or colleague when a late failure, polished presentation, or memorable rescue eclipses the longer record. Precommitting evaluation criteria, retaining process evidence, scoring distinct dimensions, waiting for acute emotion to settle, and looking for disconfirming examples are proposed as countermeasures.

## Key Claims
- Recalled experience is reconstructed from selected moments rather than calculated as a complete experiential average.
- Emotional intensity, whether pleasant or painful, gives a moment disproportionate influence over later judgment.
- An ending can dominate memory even when it is not the experience's literal chronological endpoint.
- Meaning mediates response: difficult experiences may remain valued when their discomfort supports an intelligible purpose.
- Peak-and-end preferences can guide personal entertainment choice but can distort evidence-sensitive workplace evaluation.
- Precommitted multidimensional criteria and contemporaneous records reduce reliance on vivid retrospective summaries.

## Evidence
- Mechanism and scope: [[peak-end-rule]] explains peaks as emotionally intense moments of either valence and endings as the final psychologically persistent state.
- Narrative case: [[peak-end-rule]] uses Joel's death, the playable Abby section, and Ellie's bleak ending in The Last of Us Part II to illustrate how a few moments can dominate memories of a much longer game.
- Everyday illustration: [[peak-end-rule]] contrasts an otherwise pleasant trip spoiled by its final day with a mediocre experience elevated by an unusually strong close.
- Work countermeasures: [[peak-end-rule]] recommends criteria fixed before the outcome, process records, separate dimensions, delayed judgment, and deliberate search for counterexamples.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The current evidence inventory contains one reflective essay rather than the underlying experimental literature. Its examples demonstrate how the author applies the rule but do not measure causal effect, prevalence, individual differences, duration neglect, or the conditions under which an average, beginning, repeated exposure, or later reinterpretation dominates instead. Entertainment preference should also remain distinct from artistic merit: an unwanted ending may be an intentional and valuable part of a work.

## What Changed
- Created the concept with separate implications for elective entertainment and evidence-sensitive work evaluation.
- Added practical countermeasures based on prospective criteria, recorded process evidence, decomposed scoring, delay, and counterexamples.
