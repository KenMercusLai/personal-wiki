## Definition
[[ChokepointInvesting]] is a source-described investment approach that maps an expanding industry's hard-to-substitute dependencies, identifies underfollowed companies controlling those dependencies, acts before broad consensus, and revises or rotates positions as evidence and bottlenecks change.

## Current Synthesis
The method described in [[serenity-qi-shi-lu]] joins structural industry research with active portfolio management. Academic papers, bills of materials, patents, customer commitments, yields, substitution paths, and insourcing plans help distinguish a genuine constraint from a merely fashionable supplier. The investor then moves through thesis, execution, continuing diligence, and rotation rather than waiting for complete certainty or holding indefinitely.

The durable idea is to connect demand growth to the narrowest replaceable capacity or capability and then test whether a listed company can capture the resulting economics. Scarcity alone is insufficient: the investment case also depends on duration, supplier power, capacity response, customer concentration, valuation, liquidity, and downside. The source does not demonstrate that the named investor's claimed results came from this method or that rapid post-thesis execution outperforms fuller pre-trade diligence.

## Key Claims
- Supply-chain maps can reveal indispensable materials, equipment, or technologies whose importance is obscured behind prominent end-product companies.
- Papers and bills of materials are starting points for identifying physical dependencies, but company-level evidence must test ownership, substitutability, capacity, yield, and customer behavior.
- Acting before market consensus can create upside only when the structural thesis is both correct and not already reflected in price.
- Continuing diligence should test the causal thesis rather than using favorable price action as confirmation.
- Rotation treats bottleneck status as dynamic, but comparing opportunities requires more than choosing the thesis with the greatest subjective confidence.
- A viable investment process must add valuation, position sizing, liquidity, correlation, costs, and explicit downside controls to the source's research loop.

## Evidence
- Dependency mapping: [[serenity-qi-shi-lu]] proposes academic papers, bills of materials, and global supply-chain diagrams as the initial research base.
- Company selection: [[serenity-qi-shi-lu]] favors overlooked “hidden champions” controlling hard-to-bypass components over already prominent technology platforms.
- Thesis testing: [[serenity-qi-shi-lu]] names patents, long-term contracts, and superior yields as supporting signals, and substitution, customer insourcing, and dishonest management as invalidating signals.
- Portfolio adaptation: [[serenity-qi-shi-lu]] describes rotation toward the bottleneck judged currently most active rather than permanent buy-and-hold ownership.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sole source is an uncited essay about an anonymous investor. It supplies no complete thesis, supply-chain map, contemporaneous trade, failed case, benchmark, drawdown, or risk-adjusted result. Bottlenecks can disappear through substitution, redesign, new capacity, vertical integration, demand collapse, policy change, or inventory cycles; they can also be economically important without allowing a supplier to capture attractive returns. Small-cap securities add liquidity, disclosure, governance, manipulation, and concentration risks. Early execution followed by diligence can amplify confirmation bias, while frequent rotation can add spread, impact, tax, timing, and opportunity costs. The article's reported returns therefore do not validate the method.

- Added the missing bridge from technical scarcity to supplier economics and security valuation.
- Made substitution, capacity response, liquidity, risk control, and evidentiary limits explicit.
