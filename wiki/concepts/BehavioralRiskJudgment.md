## Definition
[[BehavioralRiskJudgment]] is the practice of making risk decisions with awareness that incentives, confirmation, expectations, overconfidence, denial, regret, and narrow experience distort how people interpret uncertainty.

## Current Synthesis
Housel's beliefs article frames risk judgment as less a calculation problem than a behavioral calibration problem. People crave confirmation, defend incentive-shaped beliefs, confuse what happened with what should have happened, and prefer black-and-white certainty even when reality is probabilistic. His endurance essay adds an experience gap: an investor can endorse a risk in advance yet abandon the strategy when a large share of net worth disappears quickly, so tolerability must be judged by sustained participation rather than stated comfort alone. Wulc's Zhang Xiaoyu course summaries add investing-specific distortions: formative market memories create asset preferences, familiarity creates home bias, macro stories create false confidence, professional incentives can make even correct investors act at the wrong time, lucky profits can be mistaken for skill, and advice can be distorted by the adviser's incentives or emotional need to comfort. The practical response is humility plus process: know what you are incapable of, expect plans to fail, study history more than forecasts, write down the thesis before acting, compare outcomes against the original logic, preserve independent decision space, and choose exposure that remains psychologically survivable through actual drawdowns.

## Key Claims
- Confirmation and incentives can make false or incomplete beliefs feel durable.
- Overconfidence grows when past performance is mistaken for ability or when narrow experience is treated as universal.
- Risk should be judged through regret, optionality, and sustained financial and psychological survivability, not only through expected upside.
- Probability-driven reality is often misread through binary stories, stale expectations, forecasts, news, and temporary events.
- Formative experience and familiarity can make one asset class or country feel safer than evidence warrants.
- Incentive pressure can cause bad risk decisions even when the investor's market diagnosis is directionally right.
- Investors should separate luck from skill and filter advice by recording their thesis, reviewing outcomes, and checking the adviser's incentives and relationship to the investor.

## Evidence
- Belief distortion: [[blog-morgan-housel-collab-fund-a-few-beliefs]] says confirmation is addictive, incentives can make people defend almost anything, and denial can masquerade as patience.
- Overconfidence controls: [[blog-morgan-housel-collab-fund-a-few-beliefs]] recommends defining what one is incapable of, surviving calamity before claiming proof, and remembering how little of the world one has personally experienced.
- Risk calibration: [[blog-morgan-housel-collab-fund-a-few-beliefs]] links investing to future regret, need-versus-want risk, pessimistic saving, optimistic investing, and plan failure.
- Probability and context: [[blog-morgan-housel-collab-fund-a-few-beliefs]] contrasts probabilistic reality with binary thinking and warns that something can be factually true yet contextually nonsensical.
- Attention hygiene: [[blog-morgan-housel-collab-fund-a-few-beliefs]] recommends reading more history than forecasts and asking whether a news item will matter a year later.
- Asset-preference bias: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] says youth-era market experience can shape later investor preferences in ways that miss future winners.
- Familiarity bias: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] describes home bias as an overweighting of familiar domestic assets despite uncertain country-level returns.
- Incentive pressure: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] uses technology-bubble cases to show that client and career pressure can force professionals into or out of positions at bad times.
- Luck-versus-skill audit: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-2-tou-zi-gong-ju-yu-zi-wo-ju-xian]] warns that bull-market gains can make investors misattribute luck to ability and recommends recording investment logic before results are known.
- Thesis invalidation: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-2-tou-zi-gong-ju-yu-zi-wo-ju-xian]] uses Edward Thorp's pause-and-stop-loss method as a way to notice when market behavior does not validate the original logic.
- Advice incentives: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-2-tou-zi-gong-ju-yu-zi-wo-ju-xian]] uses Wall Street analyst incentives and emotionally comforting advice from friends as examples of why recommendations must be filtered through context.
- Experienced-loss calibration: [[morgan-housel-keep-it-going]] warns that predicted tolerance can fail when a large share of net worth disappears quickly, ending a strategy psychologically before capital is exhausted.
- Participation horizon: [[morgan-housel-keep-it-going]] treats sustainable exposure and continued participation as more important to lifetime compounding than maximizing annual return.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources provide useful heuristics rather than a formal behavioral-science model. Housel's claims do not specify when optimism, pessimism, patience, or caution should dominate, and the endurance article does not measure the gap between predicted and experienced drawdown tolerance. Wulc's sources illustrate biases through selected market histories, course examples, and investing anecdotes rather than controlled studies.
