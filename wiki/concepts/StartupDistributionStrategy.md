## Definition
[[StartupDistributionStrategy]] is a startup's theory for how product use, audience behavior, and an evolving portfolio of acquisition channels will produce traction from the first relevant users through scaled demand.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats distribution as a co-equal product hypothesis rather than a launch-stage marketing add-on. The strongest design makes use generate exposure or invitations: shared folders, collaborative meetings, creator publishing, and socially visible services can recruit future users through the product experience. That natural mechanism still needs an initial audience and a sequence of channels. Early channels should be small enough to escape incumbent competition and relevant enough to respond, but founders must expect them to change and saturate; growth then becomes an iterative move toward larger, less responsive, and more expensive channels. Product-channel fit matters throughout: episodic demand favors high-intent discovery, collaboration can favor referral, and commerce often supports paid or creator acquisition. At scale, distribution and fit converge because strong organic use and word of mouth subsidize competition in costly channels.

## Key Claims
- Distribution and product-market-fit hypotheses should be designed and tested together.
- Product mechanics that expose, share, or invite can make acquisition a consequence of use.
- Early channels trade volume for relevance and responsiveness, while scaled channels trade responsiveness for reach and competition.
- Channel choice should follow product cadence and user behavior rather than a universal preference for free virality.
- Founders need an experimental channel portfolio because individual early channels change, saturate, and lose advantage.
- Strong product value becomes more important at scale because organic demand lowers blended acquisition cost.

## Evidence
Product-embedded acquisition:
- [[andrew-chen-startups-need-dual-theories-on-distribution-and-product-market-fit]] uses Dropbox sharing, Uber's social visibility, Substack publishing, and Zoom collaboration as mechanisms where use can expose or invite another user.

Channel progression and fit:
- [[andrew-chen-startups-need-dual-theories-on-distribution-and-product-market-fit]] models early channels as low-volume and high-responsiveness, then describes movement toward scaled, competitive channels selected according to product cadence.

Scaled economics:
- [[andrew-chen-startups-need-dual-theories-on-distribution-and-product-market-fit]] argues that word of mouth and natural usage reduce the paid marketing burden when companies enter expensive mass channels.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The framework comes from one investor-operator essay and supplies examples rather than comparative acquisition data. It does not quantify responsiveness, saturation, organic lift, payback periods, or the proposed small set of scaled channels. New-platform tailwinds may look like product-embedded distribution while the platform itself is doing the acquisition, and product mechanics that invite others can create spam, privacy, or incentive problems. Channel fit also changes with geography, regulation, price, sales motion, competition, and product maturity; “natural” distribution is not necessarily free or durable.

## What Changed
- Established distribution as a hypothesis to co-design with product-market fit.
- Added the relevance-to-volume channel progression and its saturation constraint.
- Added product cadence as a boundary on channel choice and virality.
- Connected scaled channel economics back to product quality and organic demand.
