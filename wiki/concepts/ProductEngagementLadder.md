## Definition
[[ProductEngagementLadder]] is a product-growth framework that breaks a user's path from first value to deeper expertise into staged skills, tasks, and behaviors that can be taught over time.

## Current Synthesis
[[JoshElman]] frames the ladder as the post-adoption learning journey inside a product. Before a team designs the ladder, it should define the product's purpose, target users, hook, adoption requirements, and natural habit frequency. The funnel then runs from never having heard of the product to becoming locked into the right use habit, while the ladder explains what users need to learn after setup.

The journey begins with the orientation threshold that [[ScottBelsky]] calls "The Zone." A [[FirstMileProductExperience]] gets newcomers there when they understand why they are present, what they can accomplish, and what to do next without needing full product mastery. Templates, defaults, useful empty states, familiar patterns, and immediate personal utility can reduce the work required to cross that threshold. The two frameworks are complementary: first-mile design establishes orientation and early success, while the engagement ladder sequences later skills and behaviors.

The Twitter example shows the ladder's sequencing value. Early onboarding should focus on the minimum behaviors that make the product useful: understand what a tweet is, follow enough interesting people to create a timeline, read the timeline, and install mobile apps if cross-device use matters. Later product work can encourage deeper behaviors such as replies, retweets, favorites, posting, search, and building a following. The ladder is not a demand that every user become an expert; it is a way to decide what belongs in adoption, what can wait, and how product education should advance step by step.

## Key Claims
- Product teams should model purpose, users, discovery hook, adoption requirements, and habit frequency before designing a growth funnel.
- The hook that brings people in may differ from the product purpose that keeps them.
- Adoption should guide users through the setup needed before the product can deliver value.
- Newcomers need purpose, achievable value, and a clear next action before comprehensive product instruction.
- Engagement ladders separate first-value behaviors from deeper expert behaviors.
- Not every user must reach the top of the ladder for the product to be valuable.
- Ladder sequencing and first-mile design can guide onboarding, education, feature prioritization, and later engagement work.

## Evidence
- Growth-model inputs: [[building-your-growth-model-and-ladder-of-engagement]] asks teams to define purpose, users, inception, adoption, and habit before building the funnel.
- Hook-to-purpose transition: [[building-your-growth-model-and-ladder-of-engagement]] contrasts [[Uber]], where hook and purpose are nearly the same, with [[Twitter]], where news and celebrity discovery may need to transition into a personalized value proposition.
- Adoption setup: [[building-your-growth-model-and-ladder-of-engagement]] says adoption continues until the product is set up and ready to use, and that onboarding or a learn flow can guide and educate users.
- First-mile threshold: [[crafting-the-first-mile-of-product-positive-slope-medium]] says newcomers reach "The Zone" when they understand purpose, possibility, and their next action.
- Early assistance: [[crafting-the-first-mile-of-product-positive-slope-medium]] recommends templates, strong defaults, active empty states, immediate utility, and familiar patterns before explanation-heavy teaching.
- Twitter ladder sequence: [[building-your-growth-model-and-ladder-of-engagement]] lists basic tweet comprehension, following accounts, timeline reading, and mobile app installation as early steps before participation, search, posting, and audience-building.
- Changing cohorts: [[crafting-the-first-mile-of-product-positive-slope-medium]] argues that onboarding must change as products move beyond early adopters into new demographic, device, and experience contexts.
- Expert-use qualification: [[building-your-growth-model-and-ladder-of-engagement]] says users do not all need to reach the top of the ladder to get value.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are practitioner frameworks from 2016 rather than controlled studies of onboarding or retention. The ladder should be adapted to the product's natural frequency and value path: a weekly movie site, a daily communication app, and a shopping app used a few times per month need different habit expectations. Teams can overload onboarding by pushing later skills too early, while strong defaults and proactive setup can reduce agency or trust when the system guesses incorrectly. Belsky's selected historical examples and proposed 30% first-mile allocation do not establish a universal optimum.

## What Changed
- Added Belsky's first-mile orientation threshold before deeper skill progression.
- Added proactive assistance and changing newcomer cohorts as inputs to ladder design.
