## Definition
[[UnitEconomics]] evaluates whether the revenue or contribution generated by one customer, order, or other business unit exceeds the acquisition and variable service costs attributable to that unit.

## Current Synthesis
[[GuyShachar]] presents unit economics as a viability test that should precede confidence in scale: expected [[CustomerLifetimeValue]] must exceed [[CustomerAcquisitionCost]], while the costs of fulfilling the service also remain visible. His 2016 on-demand-service critique adds that smartphone convenience, fundraising, and transaction growth are not substitutes for this test. If every incremental order loses money, volume can enlarge the loss unless pricing, acquisition, retention, utilization, or delivery cost changes.

The useful principle is narrower than the essay's rhetoric. Negative economics during experimentation can be intentional, and LTV and CAC are estimates shaped by cohorts, attribution, retention, margins, and time. Unit economics therefore diagnoses the mechanism that must improve; it does not prove from current losses alone that improvement is impossible.

## Key Claims
- Customer value must be compared with acquisition and variable service cost before growth is treated as economically durable.
- Retention and virality assumptions can make projected economics look healthier than observed behavior supports.
- Convenience, demand, fundraising, and valuation are not evidence that each additional transaction creates value.
- Volume magnifies the prevailing per-unit contribution unless scale changes price, utilization, acquisition, or delivery cost.
- A real customer problem is not a viable company opportunity unless it can be served through a sustainable economic mechanism.

## Evidence
- Core comparison: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] says expected LTV should exceed CAC and describes selling a dollar for less than a dollar as the underlying failure.
- Forecast risk: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] warns that assumed indefinite retention and effortless virality can disguise weak economics.
- Scale limit: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] argues that “Uber for X” companies cannot rely on order volume alone to cure losses on most orders.
- Opportunity boundary: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] separates finding a recurring pain from building a viable business around it.
- Visual scope: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] retains an illustration spanning food, laundry, valet, beauty, healthcare, handyman, grocery, and taxi services, showing how broadly the on-demand template was being applied.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source offers a simplified practitioner rule, not a complete accounting specification. LTV should normally reflect contribution margin, retention, discounting, servicing costs, refunds, cohort variation, and uncertainty; CAC depends on attribution boundaries and organic effects. Temporary negative unit economics can fund learning, liquidity, geographic launch, or network formation when a credible path to improvement exists. The essay gives no company-level calculations and does not show that the named on-demand businesses shared one cost structure or could not improve it.

## What Changed
- Created a general unit-economics concept connecting LTV, CAC, service cost, and the limits of volume-led growth.
