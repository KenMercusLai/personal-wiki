## Definition
[[UnitEconomics]] evaluates whether the revenue or contribution generated by one customer, order, or other business unit exceeds the acquisition and variable service costs attributable to that unit.

## Current Synthesis
[[GuyShachar]] presents unit economics as a viability test that should precede confidence in scale: expected [[CustomerLifetimeValue]] must exceed [[CustomerAcquisitionCost]], while the costs of fulfilling the service also remain visible. His 2016 on-demand-service critique adds that smartphone convenience, fundraising, and transaction growth are not substitutes for this test. The [[Groupon]] restaurant example makes the same principle concrete from a merchant's perspective: a $40-face-value redemption can deliver only $9.30 in merchant proceeds against an estimated $10–$16 food cost, before labor and displaced full-price demand.

If every incremental order loses money, volume can enlarge the loss unless pricing, acquisition, retention, utilization, or delivery cost changes. Yet a deliberately loss-making first transaction may function as acquisition spend rather than prove the whole relationship is unviable. Its defensibility depends on incrementality, capacity that would otherwise go unused, breakage, add-on contribution, and observed profitable repeat behavior. Unit economics therefore diagnoses the mechanism that must improve; a simplified negative first-order margin does not by itself prove that improvement is impossible.

## Key Claims
- Customer value must be compared with acquisition and variable service cost before growth is treated as economically durable.
- Retention and virality assumptions can make projected economics look healthier than observed behavior supports.
- Convenience, demand, fundraising, and valuation are not evidence that each additional transaction creates value.
- Volume magnifies the prevailing per-unit contribution unless scale changes price, utilization, acquisition, or delivery cost.
- A discounted first transaction should count its loss as customer-acquisition investment and test whether incremental contribution and later behavior recover it.
- A real customer problem is not a viable company opportunity unless it can be served through a sustainable economic mechanism.

## Evidence
- Core comparison: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] says expected LTV should exceed CAC and describes selling a dollar for less than a dollar as the underlying failure.
- Forecast risk: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] warns that assumed indefinite retention and effortless virality can disguise weak economics.
- Scale limit: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] argues that “Uber for X” companies cannot rely on order volume alone to cure losses on most orders.
- Redemption example: [[groupon-a-bad-deal-for-restaurants-and-everyone-else-including-groupon-odell-restaurant-consultings-blog]] estimates $9.30 in restaurant proceeds against $10–$16 of food cost for a $40-face-value voucher, before other incremental and opportunity costs.
- Acquisition recovery: [[groupon-a-bad-deal-for-restaurants-and-everyone-else-including-groupon-odell-restaurant-consultings-blog]] argues that customers who follow discounts rather than return at full price fail to repay the initial promotion loss.
- Opportunity boundary: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] separates finding a recurring pain from building a viable business around it.
- Visual scope: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] retains an illustration spanning food, laundry, valet, beauty, healthcare, handyman, grocery, and taxi services, showing how broadly the on-demand template was being applied.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources offer practitioner arguments rather than complete accounting specifications. LTV should normally reflect contribution margin, retention, discounting, servicing costs, refunds, cohort variation, and uncertainty; CAC depends on attribution boundaries and organic effects. Temporary negative unit economics can fund learning, liquidity, geographic launch, network formation, or customer acquisition when a credible and measured path to recovery exists. The on-demand essay gives no company-level calculations. The Groupon article's worked example is arithmetically coherent under its stated assumptions but omits breakage, add-on purchases, tips, spare capacity, repeat visits, negotiated platform shares, and whether the redeemer was genuinely incremental; its reader comments are not representative outcome evidence.
