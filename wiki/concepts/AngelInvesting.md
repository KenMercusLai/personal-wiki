## Definition
[[AngelInvesting]] is direct early-stage startup funding by individuals who put their own personal money into a company, in contrast to venture capitalists who deploy capital pooled from limited partners.

## Current Synthesis
TechCrunch reduces the angel-versus-venture-capital distinction to a single question: whose money is it? An angel invests personal capital. A VC manages a fund supplied by LPs, with a typical ten-year life and an expectation of returns through exits. Both are equity investors who take ownership stakes and lose their money if the company fails, so the difference is not risk appetite in kind but the capital source, the time horizon, and the set of people the investor answers to.

A practitioner account from [[JoelGascoigne]] shows how one time-constrained operator deployed that personal capital. Partial [[Buffer]] share liquidity funded a relationship-led first check after he had advised CloudPeeps and learned the founder, product, and culture. He standardized initial checks at $10,000, made himself available for calls and email, and generally intended to follow on when affordable. The model substitutes prior operating contact, simple sizing rules, and hands-on support for a professional investor's extensive analysis, but Gascoigne labels it a hobby and provides no evidence that the method produced superior returns.

The two sources together separate definition from motivation and practice. Gascoigne treats the asset as highly risky and completely illiquid, invests only capital he can lose, and uses other methods for ordinary wealth growth. His return function also includes personal fulfillment, learning, social contribution, founder freedom, and wellbeing. Those aims can shape investor behavior, but they do not remove ownership risk or governance tension: his follow-on rule relies on an acknowledged imperfect signal, and at publication he reported no realized return after more than three years.

## Key Claims
- The defining difference is the capital source: angels invest their own personal money, while VCs manage a fund.
- Both are equity investors who receive ownership rather than a repayment obligation, so they share the loser's downside if the startup fails.
- Angel exposure is unusually illiquid and failure-prone, so Gascoigne sizes it as money he can lose rather than as his main wealth-building allocation.
- A relationship-led angel can combine capital with advice, introductions, and founder availability; Gascoigne's first check followed years of knowing and advising the founder.
- Simple rules can make part-time investing tractable: Gascoigne used fixed initial checks and generally favored follow-ons when affordable, while acknowledging that subsequent fundraising is an imperfect success signal.
- Angel objectives may include learning, personal fulfillment, social impact, and founder wellbeing alongside financial return, but those preferences are investor-specific rather than properties of the asset class.

## Evidence
- Core distinction: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says angels invest their own personal money and VCs manage a fund.
- Shared equity risk: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says investors who fund a startup receive an ownership stake and lose out if the company goes bust.
- Risk boundary: [[i-am-an-investor-in-9-companies-how-and-why-i-started-angel-investing]] says startup checks are completely illiquid, most new companies fail, and Gascoigne must be comfortable losing all invested capital.
- Relationship and support model: [[i-am-an-investor-in-9-companies-how-and-why-i-started-angel-investing]] traces the first investment through prior advising and says Gascoigne reserves time for founder calls and email.
- Operating rules: [[i-am-an-investor-in-9-companies-how-and-why-i-started-angel-investing]] reports a standard $10,000 initial check and a general preference for affordable follow-ons to resist dilution.
- Broader return function: [[i-am-an-investor-in-9-companies-how-and-why-i-started-angel-investing]] describes fulfillment, learning, positive impact, founder freedom, and wellbeing as intended outcomes alongside financial gain.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The TechCrunch definition implies a clean angel-versus-VC divide that blurs when angels invest through funds, VCs write personal checks, or one seed round includes both. Gascoigne's essay is a first-person retrospective from one operator with company-share liquidity, relevant advising experience, a fixed check size, and only nine reported investments. It supplies no cap tables, rejected-deal comparison, due-diligence record, instrument or valuation detail, tax or accreditation treatment, portfolio-return benchmark, or realized-return evidence; at publication he reported none after more than three years. His claim that a later raise is a useful success signal is explicitly not watertight, and his fixed-check and follow-on rules may fit neither investors with different means nor founders trying to limit small cap-table entries. Social-impact and founder-friendly intentions also do not by themselves resolve conflicts over survival, governance, follow-on allocation, or liquidity.

## What Changed
- Expanded the concept from a capital-source definition into one operator's relationship-led, fixed-check, follow-on, and founder-support practice.
- Added explicit risk budgeting, illiquidity, nonfinancial motives, and the absence of realized returns as boundaries on the practitioner model.
