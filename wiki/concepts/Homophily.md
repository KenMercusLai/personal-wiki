## Definition
[[Homophily]] is the tendency for people to trust or prefer others who seem similar to themselves.

## Current Synthesis
The Airbnb trust source treats homophily as a social bias that can shape marketplace decisions when strangers lack other evidence. Its key contribution is product-relevant: reputation information can counteract similarity bias when enough positive reviews make reliability visible.

The 645 Ventures source broadens the mechanism from marketplace trust to institutional selection. Investors may judge founders more favorably when their education, career, identity, or behavior resembles familiar success patterns or the investors themselves. Network-dependent sourcing can reinforce the same effect before evaluation begins, and a homogeneous investment committee can amplify it because fewer members recognize different forms of capability or customer demand. In both settings, the practical response is to make decision-relevant evidence visible and broaden who contributes judgment.

## Key Claims
- Similarity can bias trust decisions in stranger-to-stranger marketplaces.
- Reputation information can reduce the need to rely on similarity as a trust shortcut.
- Review systems can therefore support both transaction confidence and bias mitigation.
- Similarity bias can shape both who enters a venture deal funnel and how unfamiliar founders are assessed.
- Homogeneous decision groups can compound homophily when no member supplies an alternative founder or customer perspective.

## Evidence
- Similarity bias: [[building-for-trust-airbnb-engineering-data-science-medium]] says Airbnb found evidence of homophily among travelers in a collaborative Stanford study.
- Reputation counterweight: [[building-for-trust-airbnb-engineering-data-science-medium]] reports that enough positive reviews can counteract homophily.
- Marketplace use: [[building-for-trust-airbnb-engineering-data-science-medium]] connects this effect to Airbnb's reputation system and booking trust.
- Founder evaluation: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] argues that investors may seek founders who resemble themselves or familiar historical winners.
- Network and committee effects: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] links homogeneous deal flow and investment groups to the under-recognition of female and minority founders.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The Airbnb source says the Stanford publication was forthcoming at the time and does not provide the study design, sample, effect sizes, or later peer-reviewed result. The 645 Ventures essay offers retrospective cases rather than a measured comparison of investment decisions, so it cannot establish how much similarity bias caused any specific pass. Reputation, structured evidence, and group diversity may mitigate homophily while leaving other forms of discrimination, platform or process bias, unequal evidence accumulation, and intersectional effects unresolved.

## What Changed
- Extended homophily from marketplace booking decisions to founder selection, deal access, and investment committees.
- Added network diversity and decision-group composition as upstream conditions shaping similarity bias.
