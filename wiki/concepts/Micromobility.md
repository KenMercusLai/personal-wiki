## Definition
[[Micromobility]] is short-haul personal transportation using lightweight vehicles such as shared electric scooters to cover trips that are too far to walk conveniently but poorly served by cars, ride-hailing, or fixed transit.

## Current Synthesis
The Bird source frames micromobility around a concrete trip pattern: roughly one- to three-mile urban movement where walking is slow, cars sit in congestion, public transit does not match the point-to-point route, and bikes may be more useful for longer distances. The strategic claim is that scooters are not merely novelty hardware; when deployed densely and connected to mobile payments, location data, charging, maintenance, and city planning, they can become a new transportation service category.

## Key Claims
- Micromobility serves the short urban trips that sit between walking, transit, bikes, and cars.
- Dockless electric scooters can function as last-mile commuting infrastructure.
- Mobile app discovery and per-minute pricing make the vehicle a service rather than a personal purchase.
- City usefulness depends on deployment density, weather, safety practices, and local operations.
- If adoption broadens, scooters may reduce congestion and emissions by substituting for some short car trips.

## Evidence
- Last-mile use case: [[all-the-questions-you-wanted-answered-about-bird-scooters-and-their-recent-300-million-funding]] says scooters help when a destination is 1.6 miles away, traffic blocks cars, or transit does not match the route.
- Commuter bridge: [[all-the-questions-you-wanted-answered-about-bird-scooters-and-their-recent-300-million-funding]] cites Caltrain, Amtrak, and LA Metro-style first/last-mile contexts.
- Distance distinction: [[all-the-questions-you-wanted-answered-about-bird-scooters-and-their-recent-300-million-funding]] says scooters were beating bikes for short-haul trips under three miles in Santa Monica, while bikes remain better for five-plus-mile trips.
- Climate and seasonality: [[all-the-questions-you-wanted-answered-about-bird-scooters-and-their-recent-300-million-funding]] says some markets may work year-round while others may work only nine or ten months.
- Image evidence: [[all-the-questions-you-wanted-answered-about-bird-scooters-and-their-recent-300-million-funding]] includes an inspected beach-path photo showing many Bird scooters staged near a pedestrian and bike environment, supporting the source's account of dense local use.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a bullish investor essay rather than a mobility study. It does not quantify substitution from cars, lifecycle emissions, rider injuries, sidewalk conflicts, regulatory responses, or accessibility effects. It also acknowledges that helmet behavior is imperfect and that weather limits some markets.
