## Definition
[[BusinessCaseMethod]] is a guided learning practice in which a researched, multi-perspective business situation becomes material for questioning, discussion, and judgment under ambiguity rather than a recipe or proof of one correct answer.

## Current Synthesis
The source distinguishes a formal case from the looser success stories, anecdotes, mentoring examples, and business-book narratives often called cases. A formal case investigates a situation from inside the organization, seeks voices across roles and functions, and exposes structure, resource allocation, competing perspectives, and difficult choices. Its teaching value is completed in discussion: questions force students to analyze the relationships among facts and revisit answers rather than passively receive a conclusion.

Misuse begins when a reader turns a context-bound system into a transferable artifact. Tools, scorecards, reporting formats, job roles, org structures, campaigns, spending choices, and acquisitions are attractive because they are visible and implementable. But their results depend on culture, incentives, informal practices, resources, personalities, history, and on what old process the organization abandons. Learning therefore requires reconstructing those interactions and differences, not matching a familiar surface pattern.

## Key Claims
- Cases are prompts for analysis and judgment, not definitive answers to managerial problems.
- Multi-perspective organizational evidence is more useful than executive-only, single-function, or public-relations narratives.
- Questioning and classroom discussion are part of the learning mechanism, not optional packaging around the written case.
- Organizational practices are system components whose effects depend on culture, incentives, resources, people, and other formal and informal processes.
- Success and failure outcomes distort causal attribution in opposite directions.
- Transfer is safest at the level of questions, tensions, and mechanisms rather than copied artifacts or organizational forms.

## Evidence
- Teaching purpose: [[use-and-misuse-of-business-case-studies-learning-by-shipping]] says HBS cases surface complexity and lack a single definitive answer, with value emerging through questions and collaborative discussion.
- Research breadth: [[use-and-misuse-of-business-case-studies-learning-by-shipping]] describes cases as cross-functional investigations that include multiple organizational voices, resource allocation, and internal dynamics.
- System dependence: [[use-and-misuse-of-business-case-studies-learning-by-shipping]] argues that teams combine interrelated people and processes whose substance cannot be reproduced by copying visible form.
- Copying failures: [[use-and-misuse-of-business-case-studies-learning-by-shipping]] identifies tools, job roles, org changes, spending, campaigns, and M&A as tempting but context-dependent solutions.
- Attribution bias: [[use-and-misuse-of-business-case-studies-learning-by-shipping]] says success makes all visible attributes look right, while failure makes all of them look wrong.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a practitioner essay informed by Sinofsky's HBS and Microsoft experience, not a comparative evaluation of learning outcomes. It praises the formal HBS method and therefore reflects an admitted institutional bias. Thorough cases can still omit decisive facts, participant review may constrain candor, classroom discussion can reproduce group or instructor bias, and some artifacts do transfer when adopters understand the mechanism and local constraints. The argument is strongest as a warning against naive copying, not as evidence that organizations cannot learn from analogies or tested practices.
