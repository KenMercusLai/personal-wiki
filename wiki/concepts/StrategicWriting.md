## Definition
[[StrategicWriting]] is the practice of writing plans, decisions, and strategic choices in enough depth that their rationale, assumptions, tradeoffs, audience implications, and execution logic can be inspected and reused.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames strategic writing as a company operating discipline: writing is the medium in which plans become shared, debatable, and durable. A plan written for a real audience forces its author to ask why the proposal should work, what prior experience supports it, what alternatives and side effects matter, and how affected teams will react. Once execution begins and local decisions diverge from the original plan, written rationale gives people a common framework for making consistent tradeoffs without constantly returning to the author or relying on slide-title interpretation.

Fortune supplies a contemporaneous operating example. Amazon managers prepared detailed narratives for annual planning, and Blue Origin adopted Bezos's six-page narrative before meetings after initial resistance from its president. In both cases the document sat inside a review cadence: writing exposed assumptions and progress so a leader could audit, teach, and refine without owning every operational decision. This strengthens the mechanism while preserving Sinofsky's warning that an artifact alone is not a writing culture.

The first source is also explicit about the limits of writing culture. Memos take time, most writers get only a small number of serious draft readers, and written formats can become corrupted when organizations reward the artifact rather than the decision quality. The useful distinction is therefore function over form: writing should carry thinking, context, and corporate memory, not become a ritual copied from a successful company.

## Key Claims
- Writing forces strategy authors to expose reasoning that can stay hidden inside slides, speeches, or slogans.
- Written plans make execution faster at scale by giving decentralized teams shared context for daily tradeoffs.
- Strategic writing creates corporate history by preserving why decisions were made.
- Writing for an audience encourages consultation with real stakeholders before the decision is finalized.
- Slide decks are weak strategic artifacts when they optimize for selling takeaways rather than explaining alternatives, costs, side effects, and rationale.
- Writing culture can decay when memo production becomes a status target, when constraints are gamed, or when leaders stop doing their own writing.
- Narratives are most useful when embedded in decision and review practices that change action, not treated as standalone deliverables.

## Evidence
- Planning and agility: [[writing-is-thinking-an-annotated-twitter-thread-learning-by-shipping]] argues that agility comes from planning and that shared plans require writing.
- Reasoning pressure: [[writing-is-thinking-an-annotated-twitter-thread-learning-by-shipping]] says writing a difficult product, code, positioning, pricing, or go-to-market plan forces the author to explain why the answer should work.
- Execution context: [[writing-is-thinking-an-annotated-twitter-thread-learning-by-shipping]] says detailed plans help people making micro-decisions preserve the framework, rationale, and logic behind a choice.
- Corporate memory: [[writing-is-thinking-an-annotated-twitter-thread-learning-by-shipping]] says written strategic choices build short- and medium-term corporate history and reduce repeated mistakes.
- Stakeholder consultation: [[writing-is-thinking-an-annotated-twitter-thread-learning-by-shipping]] says writing for an audience leads authors to consider marketing, sales, and other affected readers before publishing.
- Deck limitations: [[writing-is-thinking-an-annotated-twitter-thread-learning-by-shipping]] argues that decks often omit depth on alternatives, side effects, costs, and rationale and let readers invent meaning from bullets or titles.
- Process decay: [[writing-is-thinking-an-annotated-twitter-thread-learning-by-shipping]] warns that six-page memos can become goals, be evaluated as artifacts, or be gamed through smaller fonts and margins.
- Amazon planning: [[bezos-prime-fortune]] says detailed annual narratives enabled Bezos to audit work across a company too large to micromanage.
- Blue Origin transfer: [[bezos-prime-fortune]] says its president resisted six-page narratives, adopted them after repeated requests, and became a convert within the company's review process.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are operator accounts rather than controlled studies of writing cultures. Their strongest claims apply to strategic plans and organizational decisions beyond very small teams, where informal context no longer carries reliably. Writing has real costs, gives limited opportunity for deep draft feedback, and does not lend itself to MVP-style iteration. Sinofsky warns against causal over-attribution: when [[Amazon]] is successful, outsiders and insiders may treat every visible process as a cause of success even when processes can later become hollow or counterproductive. Fortune shows adoption and executive approval, not comparative proof that six-page narratives improved outcomes.
