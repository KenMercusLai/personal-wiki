## Definition
[[PrivacyPovertyDivide]] is the unequal distribution of privacy protection, privacy burden, and privacy harm across class and marginalization.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that privacy is not experienced as a uniform digital-consumer problem. Low-income people often face intrusive collection as a condition of welfare benefits, employment, housing, credit, research participation, prison communication, or access to basic services, while wealthier people receive government transfers and market opportunities with fewer verification burdens. The divide also runs in both directions: marginalized people may be over-surveilled when they need resources and under-documented when records are necessary to prove wage theft, vote, work legally, or receive policy attention.

## Key Claims
- Privacy loss can operate as an additional tax on poverty when survival requires intrusive disclosure.
- Poor and marginalized groups are often early targets for surveillance techniques that later normalize elsewhere.
- Data privacy harms are more concrete for low-income people because records affect benefits, housing, jobs, credit, scams, and predatory offers.
- Privacy self-defense is unequal because time, money, skills, and authoritative advice are unevenly distributed.
- Too little data can also be harmful when people lack records needed to claim rights, resources, or political visibility.

## Evidence
- Survival disclosure: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] describes welfare applicants accepting home visits, personal questions, biometric collection, and database checks to receive benefits.
- Poor-first surveillance: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] cites [[MicheleGilman]]'s warning that surveillance techniques are typically used first on poor people.
- Concrete harms: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] links personal data to benefits denials, housing and job exclusion, predatory financial marketing, and scams.
- Resource inequality: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] uses [[MaryMadden]] and [[ElissaRedmiles]] to show that privacy protection consumes scarce time, skill, money, and advice access.
- Too little data: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] explains that undocumented immigrants, day laborers, homeless people, and people with convictions may be harmed by lack of records or formal visibility.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source focuses on U.S. poverty, welfare, employment, and privacy law as of 2019. It does not compare later state privacy regimes, later benefits administration changes, or non-U.S. welfare systems. It also does not deny that higher-income people face privacy harms; its claim is that the stakes, exposure, and available defenses are stratified.
