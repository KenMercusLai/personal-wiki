## Definition
[[DesignBusinessLiteracy]] is a designer’s practical ability to understand how a company creates value, operates, competes, allocates resources, and grows well enough to direct design toward consequential business and customer problems.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats business literacy as the context that makes design craft strategically useful. Brand consistency, polished interfaces, prototyping, testing, coding fluency, and style guides remain necessary capabilities, but none determines whether a team is solving the company’s most important problem or over-investing in polish. Designers improve that judgment by learning how sales sees the market, why fulfillment misses deadlines, what quarterly projections prioritize, how financing and ownership work, and which problems occupy senior leaders.

This is not a demand that every designer earn an MBA or abandon craft. The practical model is cross-functional inquiry: learn enough economics, business models, team management, competitive analysis, projections, fundraising, and operations to connect user and design evidence with company viability. That fluency can make design recommendations more credible and expand design’s influence, but the article does not establish that business study alone causes company success.

## Key Claims
- Design quality is more valuable when designers can relate craft decisions to customer value and business success.
- Business literacy helps designers distinguish consequential design work from excessive polish.
- Practical learning can come from sales, operations, planning, finance, economics, and executive problem contexts rather than only formal business education.
- User research and analytics provide an existing bridge from design judgment to organizational credibility.
- Designers can gain influence by contributing to core business conversations instead of limiting their language to design craft.
- Business orientation complements, rather than replaces, continuing design-craft development.

## Evidence
- Strategic prioritization: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] argues that business understanding reveals where craft matters and where it becomes excessive.
- Cross-functional learning: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] recommends learning from sales, shipping and fulfillment, quarterly projections, economics, fundraising, cap tables, business models, management, and competitive analysis.
- Credibility mechanism: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] says user research, analytics, and informed business conversations give design recommendations more organizational weight.
- Executive problem orientation: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] asks designers to understand CEO and vice-president concerns and apply design to those problems.
- Craft boundary: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] explicitly says designers must continue improving craft and should not ship poorly designed experiences.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is one short 2016 practitioner essay. Its examples of design-led companies are assertions rather than comparative cases, and it supplies no measures showing that designer business study caused company performance, design influence, or better product outcomes. Business priorities can also be short-term, internally contested, or harmful to users; literacy should improve a designer’s ability to question and shape those priorities, not require uncritical alignment with executives or quarterly targets.
