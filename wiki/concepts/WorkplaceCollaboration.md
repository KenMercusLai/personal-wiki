## Definition
[[WorkplaceCollaboration]] is the practice of coordinating with colleagues, stakeholders, and counterparties so shared work moves through communication, trust, role clarity, and purposeful interaction rather than defensive positioning or empty procedure.

## Current Synthesis
The Manelius source contributes a trust-centered view of workplace collaboration. In a project-budget crisis, collaboration deteriorated as both sides became defensive about contracts, responsibility, and financial exposure. Chris's request to assume positive intent reframed the interaction from blame protection toward problem solving. The source therefore treats collaboration as partly an attribution problem: when people default to distrust, they spend energy on self-protection; when they can extend bounded trust, they can move faster. Barnett adds a meeting-design qualification: collaboration is not the same as filling calendars. Scheduled meetings need a substantive why, while informal conversations can be valuable when they continue only as long as they help the work.

## Key Claims
- Collaboration can break down when project stress turns stakeholders toward defensive self-protection.
- Shared assumptions about good faith can reduce friction in hard conversations.
- Trust-based collaboration can be faster than contract-heavy coordination when the relationship is healthy.
- Boundaries remain necessary when the environment is low-trust or a person has already violated trust.
- Scheduled interaction should be justified by a clear purpose rather than by the appearance of rational process.
- Informal interaction can support collaboration when it remains useful and stops once value disappears.

## Evidence
- Project crisis: [[assume-positive-intent-rick-manelius]] describes a client-budget conflict where both sides became irritable and defensive.
- Reframing move: [[assume-positive-intent-rick-manelius]] says Chris asked colleagues to assume positive intent during a tense call.
- Efficiency claim: [[assume-positive-intent-rick-manelius]] argues that distrust creates second-guessing, combativeness, and slower coordination.
- Boundary condition: [[assume-positive-intent-rick-manelius]] warns that trust should change after violations or in unsafe contexts.
- Meeting-purpose test: [[bill-barnett-delete-all-meetings]] argues that formal meetings often omit the question "why?" and become a solution looking for a problem.
- Informal value filter: [[bill-barnett-delete-all-meetings]] says informal talks continue only when they matter and are cut short when they do not.
- Chance encounter design: [[bill-barnett-delete-all-meetings]] cites [[SteveJobs]]' Apple campus vision as an attempt to increase random informal interactions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are personal essays rather than broad empirical studies. Trust can be misplaced in environments with coercive power, repeated deception, legal exposure, or unequal ability to absorb risk. Collaboration may also require explicit agreements, incentives, resources, scheduled decision forums, and decision rights, not only better assumptions about intent or informal conversation.
