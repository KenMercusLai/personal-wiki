## Definition
[[AugmentedReality]] is real-time computational alteration of a camera-mediated view or a user's relationship to physical space, including overlays, transformations of captured people or environments, and location-bound digital experiences.

## Current Synthesis
The sources broaden AR beyond information or objects superimposed on external space through glasses. [[Snapchat]] Lenses make the face and camera image an augmentation surface for playful, expressive communication. Danco adds a presence interpretation: location-bound experiences such as Pokémon Go can make “here” and “now” part of the product, while Snapchat filters provide a mass-market camera wedge. Evans connects those phone-mediated forms to a head-worn possibility: after smartphones absorbed physical tools into apps, mature AR could place virtual objects and application surfaces back into perceived physical space. These are complementary forms—transformation, overlay, situated experience, and spatial placement—not evidence that one permanently defines the category.

## Key Claims
- AR can modify the perceived base layer rather than only add graphics above an unchanged scene.
- The front-facing camera makes the self a primary augmentation target and interaction input.
- Real-time processing can turn transformed images into expressive social messages.
- Location-bound AR can make physical place and temporal urgency part of a digital experience.
- Camera effects may provide a consumer adoption wedge without determining who captures later platform value.
- Head-worn AR could shift interaction from looking through a phone toward perceiving virtual objects in physical space, but this remains a forecast in the supplied evidence.

## Evidence
- Base-layer transformation: [[forget-drones-and-spaceships-the-snapchat-dogface-filter-is-the-future]] describes Lenses smoothing, reshaping, and transforming faces in real time.
- Interface role: [[forget-drones-and-spaceships-the-snapchat-dogface-filter-is-the-future]] connects image-sensor input, processing, messaging, and repeat use.
- Situated presence: [[from-pull-and-push-to-here-and-now-the-grand-bargain-of-facebook-and-the-feed-is-unraveling-what-comes-next]] uses Pokémon Go's location and urgency to illustrate a literal “here and now.”
- Platform wedge: [[forget-drones-and-spaceships-the-snapchat-dogface-filter-is-the-future]] and [[from-pull-and-push-to-here-and-now-the-grand-bargain-of-facebook-and-the-feed-is-unraveling-what-comes-next]] treat Snapchat filters as an early mass-market AR form while stopping short of proof that Snap would control the broader category.
- Spatial inversion: [[imaging-snapchat-and-mobile-benedict-evans]] contrasts phones absorbing physical objects into apps with head-worn AR placing virtual objects and app surfaces into perceived physical space.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Three 2016 essays cannot establish AR's dominant or durable form. Face filters, spatial overlays, navigation, games, industrial guidance, and head-worn interfaces can coexist. The sources provide no comparative adoption, retention, revenue, safety, privacy, accessibility, technical-performance, or wellbeing evidence. Danco's Pokémon Go example is illustrative; his forecast that AR would capture a new media bottleneck and Evans's forecast of convincingly present virtual objects are not tested.

## What Changed
- Added the proposed transition from phone-mediated effects to head-worn virtual objects in physical space.
- Preserved the distinction between an interaction forecast and evidence of adoption or platform ownership.
