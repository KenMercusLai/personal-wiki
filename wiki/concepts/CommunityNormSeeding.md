## Definition
[[CommunityNormSeeding]] is the deliberate use of early participant support, selection, examples, curation, and product constraints to establish behaviors that later members can observe, copy, teach, and adapt.

## Current Synthesis
Kickstarter's early community did not become self-regulating through non-intervention. [[CassieMarketos]] says the small team worked closely with initial creators, selected projects they regarded as strong uses of the platform, kept in contact, and publicly featured projects whose storytelling expressed the platform's creative purpose. Later creators treated those campaigns as a guidebook, contacted successful predecessors, wrote tutorials, ran workshops, and brought their experience as backers into project feedback. Norms therefore scaled through examples and peer transmission, reducing the need for identical staff instruction in every case.

A discovery community can seed norms through admission and recognition as well as exemplary content. In the [[ProductHunt]] case, [[LoicLeMeur]] says a small mailing-list group preceded the platform, contribution privileges stayed restricted, influential early members received direct outreach, and makers or contributors gained profiles, credit, replies, gifts, and offline contact. Open communication about the startup coexisted with controlled participation: the visible community could attract newcomers without allowing every newcomer to shape the feed immediately. This broadens norm seeding from exemplary content to admission, status, and recognition design, while exposing a stronger exclusion risk.

The same account shows the boundary. Examples can guide ordinary participation without resolving mission drift, delivery failure, overfunding, or category conflict. Kickstarter still added creator resources and reward caps, asked for greater transparency, and debated whether hardware projects crowded out its artistic mission. Norm seeding is therefore a governance mechanism, not a substitute for accountable platform decisions.

## Key Claims
- Early participant selection and intensive support can make desired behavior concrete before formal rules are mature.
- Publicly visible examples let later participants infer standards and adapt them without receiving identical staff instruction.
- Peer communication, how-to material, workshops, and experience in multiple roles can turn staff-seeded norms into community knowledge.
- Editorial curation communicates who belongs and what level or style of participation is attainable.
- Access rights, contributor credit, and personal recognition can reinforce early norms before participation broadens.
- Self-regulation remains partial because scale, harmful incentives, delivery risk, and mission conflict still require product and policy intervention.

## Evidence
- Early examples: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] says Kickstarter worked closely with initial creators so their projects demonstrated strong uses of the product.
- Peer replication: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] says later creators used early projects as templates, contacted successful creators, published advice, and ran workshops.
- Backer feedback: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] reports that creators often had backer experience and changed projects in response to community feedback.
- Editorial signaling: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] says newsletter choices emphasized compelling storytelling and mixed large successes with modest attainable projects.
- Intervention boundary: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] connects overfunding and delivery problems to added creator resources and reward caps, and connects hardware growth to explicit mission debate.
- Selective access: [[loic-le-meur-10-lessons-from-product-hunts-success]] says Product Hunt limited who could submit, vote, or comment while founders contacted early users one by one.
- Recognition loop: [[loic-le-meur-10-lessons-from-product-hunts-success]] describes contributor profiles, maker-success stories, public replies, shirts, stickers, dinners, and meetups as rewards for participation.
- Founding context: [[loic-le-meur-10-lessons-from-product-hunts-success]] traces Product Hunt to a mailing list whose members already shared an interest in discovering and discussing new products.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence consists of one former employee's Kickstarter retrospective and one outside observer's Product Hunt account; neither compares communities seeded differently. Early selection can encode staff taste, exclude unconventional participants, conceal arbitrary gatekeeping, or produce conformity around examples that later become stale. Product Hunt's reported focus on Twitter reach and Silicon Valley importance makes this status-reproduction risk concrete. Recognition can motivate contribution while also shifting effort toward visibility, and a self-help layer can transfer support labor and risk to participants without meaningful authority. The sources do not quantify whether selection, examples, access, or rewards caused quality, delivery, inclusion, retention, or trust.

## What Changed
- Added selective access, direct recruitment, public contributor credit, and personal recognition as norm-seeding mechanisms.
- Sharpened the tradeoff between quality-preserving curation and exclusion or inherited status bias.
