## Definition
[[AssetAllocation]] is the practice of spreading investment exposure across asset classes, geographies, and portfolio roles so returns and drawdowns do not depend on one favored market, timing call, or product choice.

## Current Synthesis
The Zhang Xiaoyu course notes treat asset allocation as both risk control and opportunity capture. The first note emphasizes behavioral diversification: people extrapolate from formative market memories, so a single favored asset or home market can cause them to miss the next source of returns. The portfolio-construction note makes the implementation more concrete: asset allocation is the main lever for controlling volatility and maximum drawdown while still pursuing long-term return. Equities, government bonds, cash, gold, commodities, real estate trusts, and global stock segments play different roles across model portfolios such as the Permanent Portfolio, All Weather allocation, and Swensen-style allocation. Rebalancing then turns the allocation into a repeatable discipline rather than a one-time mix.

## Key Claims
- Investors often form asset preferences from youth-era market memories.
- Recent or personally vivid returns should not be extrapolated into permanent portfolio beliefs.
- Asset-class leadership rotates over long cycles that can cover a large share of an individual's working life.
- Diversification can increase the chance of participating in unexpected winners, not only reduce risk.
- Geographic diversification belongs inside allocation because domestic economic growth and domestic market returns can diverge.
- Asset allocation is presented as the dominant driver of portfolio volatility and maximum drawdown over long horizons.
- Model portfolios trade off expected return, drawdown control, volatility, and implementation complexity through different asset-class roles.

## Evidence
- Memory bias: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] says early market experience strongly shapes later investment preferences.
- U.S. stock example: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] says investors marked by the weak 1970s U.S. market could miss the later 1980s and 1990s bull markets.
- Japan housing example: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] says optimism from Japan's growth era contributed to property overexposure before the bubble burst.
- Rotation: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] argues stocks, housing, bonds, oil, gold, and other assets can rotate in leadership across long cycles.
- Geography: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] uses overseas allocation and global ETFs as implementation examples for diversification beyond the home market.
- Volatility and drawdown lever: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-3-tou-zi-zu-he-gou-jian]] cites portfolio-performance research to argue that allocation explains most portfolio-return volatility, while timing and product selection contribute far less under long-term diversified conditions.
- Asset-class roles: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-3-tou-zi-zu-he-gou-jian]] classifies investable assets as equities, fixed income, commodities, real-estate trusts, and cash-like products, then distinguishes bond credit risk, interest-rate sensitivity, and commodity drawdown risk.
- Model portfolio tradeoffs: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-3-tou-zi-zu-he-gou-jian]] compares the Permanent Portfolio, All Weather allocation, and Swensen-style allocation by return, maximum drawdown, volatility, Sharpe ratio, and complexity.
- Rebalancing discipline: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-3-tou-zi-zu-he-gou-jian]] presents annual or threshold-based rebalancing as a way to restore target weights, trim winners, add to laggards, and reduce emotional chasing.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources argue for diversification but do not define an optimal allocation, currency policy, tax-aware implementation, jurisdiction-specific product list, or household-liquidity rule. The model portfolios and return comparisons are historical illustrations, not proof that every diversified portfolio will outperform every concentrated one or that old U.S.-market data will transfer cleanly to every investor's market.
