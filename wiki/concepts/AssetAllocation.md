## Definition
[[AssetAllocation]] is the practice of spreading investment exposure across asset classes, geographies, and portfolio roles so returns and drawdowns do not depend on one favored market, timing call, or product choice.

## Current Synthesis
The Zhang Xiaoyu course notes treat asset allocation as both risk control and opportunity capture. They emphasize behavioral diversification, maximum-drawdown control, distinct roles for equities, bonds, cash, gold, commodities, real estate, and global exposure, and rebalancing as the rule that makes a target mix repeatable. Wilson adds a first-person ruin-avoidance case: after concentrated venture-capital and Internet-stock wealth fell roughly 90% during the dot-com collapse, pre-crash real-estate purchases preserved the remainder. The combined synthesis is that allocation must exist before stress, because waiting for recovery, continued declines, illiquidity, or bankruptcy can defeat a plan to sell after a crash. Example portfolios clarify tradeoffs but do not establish one universal set of weights.

## Key Claims
- Recent or personally vivid returns should not be extrapolated into permanent portfolio beliefs.
- Diversification can increase participation in unexpected winners while reducing dependence on one asset, geography, or speculative thesis.
- Asset allocation is presented as a primary lever for portfolio volatility, maximum drawdown, and survival through severe losses.
- Allocation must precede a crash because behavioral hesitation and assets that become unsaleable or worthless can defeat reactive exit.
- Different asset classes and portfolio models trade off return, drawdown control, liquidity, volatility, leverage, and implementation complexity.
- Rebalancing or deliberate pre-crash profit-taking turns allocation from a static intention into a repeatable capital-preservation practice.

## Evidence
- Memory bias: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] says early market experience strongly shapes later investment preferences.
- U.S. stock example: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] says investors marked by the weak 1970s U.S. market could miss the later 1980s and 1990s bull markets.
- Japan housing example: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] says optimism from Japan's growth era contributed to property overexposure before the bubble burst.
- Rotation: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] argues stocks, housing, bonds, oil, gold, and other assets can rotate in leadership across long cycles.
- Geography: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] uses overseas allocation and global ETFs as implementation examples for diversification beyond the home market.
- Volatility and drawdown lever: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-3-tou-zi-zu-he-gou-jian]] cites portfolio-performance research to argue that allocation explains most portfolio-return volatility, while timing and product selection contribute far less under long-term diversified conditions.
- Asset-class roles: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-3-tou-zi-zu-he-gou-jian]] classifies investable assets as equities, fixed income, commodities, real-estate trusts, and cash-like products, then distinguishes bond credit risk, interest-rate sensitivity, and commodity drawdown risk.
- Model portfolio tradeoffs: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-3-tou-zi-zu-he-gou-jian]] compares the Permanent Portfolio, All Weather allocation, and Swensen-style allocation by return, maximum drawdown, volatility, Sharpe ratio, and complexity.
- Rebalancing discipline: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-3-tou-zi-zu-he-gou-jian]] presents annual or threshold-based rebalancing as a way to restore target weights, trim winners, add to laggards, and reduce emotional chasing.
- Crash-survival case: [[diversification-aka-how-to-survive-a-crash-avc]] says two real-estate holdings that represented about 10% of pre-crash net worth became Wilson's family's remaining net worth after its concentrated venture and Internet-stock exposure collapsed.
- Reactive-exit limit: [[diversification-aka-how-to-survive-a-crash-avc]] says investors may initially expect recovery while selling continues and some holdings become worthless, so protection should be established before the break.
- Personal target: [[diversification-aka-how-to-survive-a-crash-avc]] proposes roughly equal cash-like, blue-chip-stock, low-leverage income-real-estate, and high-risk buckets as Wilson's desired mix, not as a universal allocation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources argue for diversification but do not define an optimal allocation, currency policy, tax-aware implementation, jurisdiction-specific product list, or household-liquidity rule. Wilson's 25% buckets, 5% estimated crypto exposure, and remembered loss are personal, historically situated examples rather than audited evidence or individualized advice. The model portfolios and return comparisons are historical illustrations, not proof that every diversified portfolio will outperform every concentrated one or that old U.S.-market data will transfer cleanly to every investor's market. Diversification can reduce concentration risk without eliminating correlated declines, inflation, leverage, custody, liquidity, or counterparty risk.
