Reversible decision delegation is the practice of separating decisions by reversibility before deciding how much founder or CEO involvement they need. In Mathilde Collin's Front advice source, Jared Smith describes one-way door decisions as choices the company must live with for a long time, while two-way door decisions can be changed later.

The source's examples of one-way decisions include raising money, modifying the compensation plan, buying a company, and rewriting a product. These choices deserve more founder involvement because the downside of a mistaken call can persist even after the organization realizes the mistake.

Two-way decisions are different. If a path can be reversed, the founder should become less involved over time, letting teams build judgment through action instead of sending every call back up the hierarchy. This preserves scarce leadership attention for commitments that are expensive to unwind.

The same source connects delegation to executive onboarding. Collin describes granting too much autonomy to an early executive before enough founder context had been transferred. Her later practice was to spend the first two months closely onboarding executives so they could absorb founder instinct before operating with broader autonomy.

## Signals

- The team explicitly asks whether a decision can be reversed before escalating it.
- Founder time is reserved for financing, compensation, acquisition, architecture, or product-direction commitments that are costly to undo.
- Reversible experiments can proceed without waiting for founder approval.
- Executive autonomy follows deliberate context transfer rather than title alone.
