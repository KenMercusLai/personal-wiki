## Definition
[[ProblemPersistence]] is the discipline of staying loyal to a customer and a problem while treating the current solution as disposable, so that iteration changes what is built rather than what the team is trying to fix.

## Current Synthesis
The sources separate two things that founders often conflate: commitment and attachment. Commitment belongs to the problem, the customer, and the willingness to keep working; attachment belongs to the solution, and it is what turns iteration into betrayal. [[MichaelSeibel]] states the distinction most sharply — foolish stubbornness is not working on the same problem too long but refusing to iterate on the solution — and explains that passion for the problem is what makes fast pivoting possible, because founders will not run through many candidate solutions for a problem they do not care about. [[AhmadIqbal]] reaches the same place from an operator's route: loving the problem is what kept his team testing manual, cheap tactics before building software, and building before validating the problem is what killed the handwritten-card app. [[building-products-the-year-of-the-looking-glass-medium]] adds the team-dynamics version, treating "fall in love with the problem" as a rule that reduces role-boundary friction because members optimize for the problem rather than for their own part of the solution.

## Key Claims
- Persistence belongs to the problem and the customer; the solution is the part that is supposed to change.
- Stubbornness is defined by refusal to iterate on the solution rather than by staying with the same problem for a long time.
- Attaching to an early product or metaphor is the failure mode, because it converts iteration into a personal loss.
- Passion for the problem is the mechanism that makes rapid solution changes sustainable rather than demoralizing.
- Problem-first framing sets the order of product work: establish the problem and the audience before choosing what to build.
- Teams that optimize for the problem rather than for their own slice of the solution coordinate better across role boundaries.

## Evidence
- Stubbornness definition: [[y-combinator-ceo-if-you-are-not-drowning-in-demand-you-dont-have-product-market-fit-capital-growth-blog]] says foolish stubbornness comes not from working on the same problem but from an unwillingness to iterate on the solution.
- Keep-problem-change-solution: [[y-combinator-ceo-if-you-are-not-drowning-in-demand-you-dont-have-product-market-fit-capital-growth-blog]] argues more startups would succeed if founders stayed bull-headed about the customer and the problem while changing the solution.
- Passion as pivot fuel: [[y-combinator-ceo-if-you-are-not-drowning-in-demand-you-dont-have-product-market-fit-capital-growth-blog]] says founders are unlikely to pivot quickly through many problems if they do not care deeply about the initial one.
- Operator version: [[building-apps-for-shopify-fall-in-love-with-the-problem-not-the]] frames the whole founder story as falling in love with the problem rather than the solution, and treats the market problem as the thing to understand before committing years of effort.
- Build-before-validate failure: [[building-apps-for-shopify-fall-in-love-with-the-problem-not-the]] says the handwritten-card product failed because software and hardware were built before merchant demand and operating feasibility were tested.
- Problem-first sequencing: [[building-products-the-year-of-the-looking-glass-medium]] says product work should begin with a clear problem, audience, and reason the problem is worth solving.
- Team effect: [[building-products-the-year-of-the-looking-glass-medium]] reports that teams which fall in love with the problem, rather than the solution, have more successful outcomes and fewer role-boundary conflicts.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Persistence is only as good as the problem: none of the three sources explains how to tell when the problem itself is too small, unrepeatable, or unprofitable, and the wiki's other startup material shows cases where staying with a problem delayed an unavoidable strategic choice. Seibel's version is a selection heuristic from an accelerator, not a tested prescription for how many solution changes are enough. Iqbal's evidence is one Shopify-app company, and his surviving products were free or lightly validated for part of their life, so problem loyalty did not by itself prove willingness to pay. The phrase also risks becoming unfalsifiable: any failure can be narrated as insufficient love for the problem, which makes it a weak diagnostic unless paired with explicit evidence about the customer and the market.
