## Definition
[[HomeBias]] is the tendency for investors to overweight familiar domestic assets even when global diversification would better reflect opportunity and risk.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents home bias as a comfort problem and an empirical risk. Investors naturally prefer assets from the country where they live, spend, and understand institutions, but domestic familiarity does not guarantee future returns. The article emphasizes that stock-market returns do not mechanically follow GDP growth: high growth can be priced in, profits from global production can accrue to companies in other countries, and the best-performing markets can be geographically surprising. A modest global allocation through broad funds is therefore framed as accessible to ordinary investors rather than only wealthy ones.

## Key Claims
- Investors tend to allocate too much to their own country because familiar assets feel safer.
- Domestic economic growth does not guarantee domestic stock or property returns.
- Valuation and globalization can break the intuitive link between national GDP growth and investor return.
- Global diversification protects against being wrong about which country will lead future market returns.
- Ordinary investors can access global markets through broad funds rather than needing bespoke wealthy-client products.

## Evidence
- Domestic preference: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] says Japanese and U.S. investors overweight their home markets relative to global market capitalization, and Chinese investors are likely especially familiar with domestic assets.
- GDP-return disconnect: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] cites long-run research across major markets to argue that stock returns and real GDP growth are not directly linked.
- Valuation channel: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] says rapid growth can still produce poor returns if investors paid too much for it.
- Globalization channel: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] uses globally produced goods to show that GDP can accrue in one country while corporate profits accrue elsewhere.
- Implementation: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] points to global-market funds such as ACWI-style exposure as an accessible overseas-allocation path.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not settle currency risk, tax treatment, capital controls, fund availability, or how much overseas allocation is appropriate. It argues for considering global diversification, not for ignoring local spending needs or local financial constraints.
