## Definition
[[WebAdEconomics]] describes the web funding model in which users receive content or services without direct payment while advertising, tracking, analytics, and data collection monetize their attention and behavior.

## Current Synthesis
The sources describe web advertising as an implicit exchange whose incentives differ from direct transactions and direct sponsorship. Users often experience content as free while attention, tracking, scripts, privacy exposure, and per-view inventory produce revenue. Platform concentration and automated content cases show value moving toward intermediaries or cheap attention rather than necessarily toward the publisher that funded the work. Searls sharpens the mechanism by separating [[Adtech]], which may follow a targetable person across inventory, from [[BrandAdvertising]], which deliberately sponsors a media environment and its reputation. The alternatives are plural rather than settled: subscriptions and browser payments make value exchange explicit, Brave retains ads with local matching and shared rewards, and direct sponsorship preserves advertising while reducing audience-chasing intermediation.

## Key Claims
- The web has been built around both free information ideals and commerce, so users who experience content as free usually pay indirectly through attention, behavioral data, tracking, page weight, privacy exposure, and third-party script risk.
- Ad blocking makes the implicit bargain harder to ignore because it removes a major revenue path without automatically replacing it; it can also signal rejection of surveillance rather than rejection of every form of advertising.
- Mobile platforms have normalized small direct payments, and browser-integrated payments could make web transactions explicit while preserving free access where publishers and users prefer it.
- Advertising networks can grow through inventory, reach, demand, and targeting improvements, while preserving platform value may require mediating rather than strengthening advertisers' durable direct customer relationships.
- Platform-controlled advertising markets can weaken professional publishers, while audience-first automation can also detach advertisers from media context; responses include fairer platform payment, direct sponsorship, commerce, subscriptions, licensing, studio work, and other revenue lines.
- Per-view advertising rewards whoever attracts the views, not whoever paid to produce the content, so near-zero-cost automated video can earn from volume at rates that would not sustain the original reporting.
- Because attention is rewarded independently of conscious choice or informational value, ad-funded systems can mistake compulsion for demand and favor speed and volume over depth.

## Evidence
- Free-versus-fee continuum: [[402-payment-required-david-humphrey-medium]] says web use is rarely completely free because tracking and analytics monetize activity.
- Technical cost: [[402-payment-required-david-humphrey-medium]] connects ads to bloated downloads, security risks, and privacy invasions.
- Payment normalization: [[402-payment-required-david-humphrey-medium]] cites post-iPhone app stores, in-app payments, Amazon, Netflix, and Google Play as evidence that users pay when flows are trusted and easy.
- Long-tail problem: [[402-payment-required-david-humphrey-medium]] contrasts the New York Times' internal paywall capacity with smaller creators' lack of comparable infrastructure.
- Publisher-platform split: [[9-boxes]] argues that Google and Facebook capture most digital ad revenue while professional content creators receive too little value.
- Diversification response: [[9-boxes]] says BuzzFeed is building revenue from commerce, advertising, platform revenue, show development, licensing, and merchandise.
- Subscription countermodel: [[above-avalon-above-avalon-subscriptions-turn-three]] says Above Avalon avoided dependency on ads, sponsors, or other revenue streams.
- Page-view incentive: [[above-avalon-above-avalon-subscriptions-turn-three]] argues that subscription funding reduces pressure to publish sensational traffic-seeking articles.
- Per-view rate: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] estimates video ad income at roughly $1 to $20 per 1,000 views with about $7 as a commonly cited average.
- Volume economics: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] ties a channel's estimated $3,000 in two months to more than 225,000 views in a single month rather than to any expensive production.
- Free-riding on authored work: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] describes ad-bearing videos assembled from other outlets' reporting and images, with no payment to the original producers.
- Ad-versus-transaction incentives: [[why-were-paying-for-content-these-days-hunter-walk]] argues that Google and Facebook can improve ad revenue without taxing consumers or requiring creator conversion work, while transaction businesses must raise price or conversion.
- Niche revenue gap: [[why-were-paying-for-content-these-days-hunter-walk]] says advertising yields only a few dollars annually from even enthusiastic niche users, making a purchase or recurring payment potentially much more valuable.
- Intermediary complexity: [[your-next-browser-will-pay-you-by-daniel-colin-james]] includes a 2018 display-advertising landscape and a programmatic delivery diagram with agencies, servers, SSPs, exchanges, DSPs, networks, and publishers.
- User costs and publisher pressure: [[your-next-browser-will-pay-you-by-daniel-colin-james]] combines an ad-block adoption chart, a slow-page audit, and a newspaper advertising-revenue chart to argue that users and publishers both lose under the incumbent model.
- Browser-ad alternative: [[your-next-browser-will-pay-you-by-daniel-colin-james]] proposes optional locally matched ads, BAT rewards, publisher shares, and automatic creator contributions.
- Attention incentive: [[words-still-matter-3-min-read]] argues that monetized attention rewards capture regardless of quality or context and can make compulsive behavior look like preference.
- Editorial pressure: [[words-still-matter-3-min-read]] links falling ad revenue per reader to quantity, speed, clicks, weaker privacy, and less room for research, fact-checking, nuance, or polish.
- Open-paywall alternative: [[words-still-matter-3-min-read]] presents Medium subscriptions, an ad-free product, and member-value-based partner earnings as an attempted incentive realignment.
- Reach and targeting pressure: [[continuations-by-albert-wenger-from-advertising-to-subscriptions-and-the]] argues that abundant publishing fragmented attention and depressed ad prices, rewarding large audiences and detailed user knowledge.
- Advertiser-side conflict: [[continuations-by-albert-wenger-from-advertising-to-subscriptions-and-the]] argues that a platform weakens its own intermediary value if advertisers establish lasting direct customer relationships outside it.
- Consumer-subscription alternative: [[continuations-by-albert-wenger-from-advertising-to-subscriptions-and-the]] presents renewal-dependent revenue and no per-use charge as a more customer-aligned model.
- Sponsorship distinction: [[doc-searls-brands-need-to-fire-adtech]] contrasts tracking-based audience pursuit with advertisers deliberately buying space or time in reputable media.
- Externality mechanism: [[doc-searls-brands-need-to-fire-adtech]] connects cheap impression-seeking to brand-safety failures, weak publisher control, privacy loss, fraud, malware, and low-quality content incentives.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Humphrey's source is a product and protocol proposal, not evidence that user payment can replace advertising at web scale. BuzzFeed's memo does not prove that platform revenue will scale or that diversification can replace weak ad economics. Above Avalon and Medium do not provide enough outcome data to generalize subscription alignment. Walk's and Wenger's accounts offer incentive hypotheses without comparative platform or customer evidence. The YouTube payout range is reported hearsay, while the Brave/BAT article is commissioned promotional writing with unverified historical promises. Searls's direct-sponsorship alternative is also asserted rather than comparatively measured; its near-zero adtech-effectiveness claim and GDPR "extinction event" forecast should not be treated as established outcomes, and contextual programmatic buying complicates its binary model.

## What Changed
- Added direct media sponsorship as an advertising alternative to surveillance-led audience chasing.
- Added brand safety and lost contextual association to the user, publisher, and editorial costs already synthesized.
- Clarified that ad blocking can reject tracking terms without rejecting all advertising.
