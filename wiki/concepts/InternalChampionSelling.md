## Definition
[[InternalChampionSelling]] is a B2B sales practice that frames a product through both the organizational result it can produce and the goals, incentives, authority, and downside of the person advocating for it internally.

## Current Synthesis
An account is not only a company logo or projected contract value; adoption usually requires people to investigate, endorse, implement, and defend a decision. Tunguz's useful contribution is to make that personal stake explicit. A credible seller learns how the prospective champion is evaluated, identifies a product outcome that advances an important organizational priority, and asks whether the improvement is large and provable enough to justify implementation, reliability, political, and reputational risk. This can reveal urgency and product priority, but “selling promotions” is too narrow as a universal description: buyers may be motivated by duty, team welfare, compliance, cost, mission, job security, or risk reduction, and personal incentives may diverge from company value.

## Key Claims
- B2B adoption depends on an individual or coalition willing to spend attention and reputation on the decision.
- Effective value framing connects a measurable company outcome with the champion's real goals and evaluation context.
- Champion strength depends on expected benefit, authority, priority, implementation capacity, and personal as well as organizational downside.
- An absent champion can signal weak priority or poor risk-reward, but can also reflect procurement structure, diffuse ownership, or an incomplete stakeholder map.
- Order-of-magnitude improvement is a useful challenge for risky replacement decisions, not a validated universal threshold.

## Evidence
- Personal stake and value alignment: [[tom-tunguz-youre-in-the-business-of-selling-promotions]] argues that the person behind an account advocates for software under reputational exposure and may connect successful outcomes to career advancement.
- Goal discovery: [[tom-tunguz-youre-in-the-business-of-selling-promotions]] recommends asking about goals, priorities, rewards, and internal culture to understand what makes advocacy worthwhile.
- Risk-reward test: [[tom-tunguz-youre-in-the-business-of-selling-promotions]] uses risky hospital infrastructure replacement to argue that expected improvement must compensate for the champion's downside.
- Priority signal: [[tom-tunguz-youre-in-the-business-of-selling-promotions]] treats the absence of a willing risk owner as possible evidence that the problem or product is not important enough.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept currently rests on one short 2016 practitioner essay with no deal sample, buyer interviews, promotion outcomes, conversion measures, cycle-time comparison, or deployment results. Promotion is an illustrative incentive rather than a complete buyer model, and overemphasizing it can ignore professional duty, team benefit, mission, procurement rules, collective decisions, and conflicts between personal advancement and organizational welfare. A missing champion does not by itself invalidate a market: authority may be distributed, ownership may emerge later, or regulation and purchasing structure may require a coalition. The essay's ten-times rule has no defined baseline or measurement method and should be treated as a high-bar question, not a numerical law. Ethical use requires truthful outcomes, visible risk, appropriate stakeholder participation, and no attempt to exploit ambition to bypass safeguards.

## What Changed
- Created the concept with a qualified model joining individual incentives, organizational value, and adoption risk.
