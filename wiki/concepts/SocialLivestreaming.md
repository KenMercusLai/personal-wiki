## Definition
[[SocialLivestreaming]] is mobile livestreaming organized around real-time interaction between personal broadcasters and viewers, especially stranger-to-stranger entertainment streams where chat, gifts, recognition, and presence are part of the show.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents social livestreaming in China as a new mobile entertainment format rather than a simple continuation of webcam shows, gaming streams, or scheduled performances. Its defining shift is from outward capture to inward capture: the phone's front camera turns live video into a selfie-like, always-available form of self-expression. The stream does not need a scripted performance because viewers co-produce the entertainment through questions, gifts, requests, leaderboards, and competition for broadcaster attention.

## Key Claims
- Social livestreaming made personal mobile broadcasts a mainstream entertainment category in China.
- The front-facing camera changed livestreaming from documenting the world toward performing the self.
- Real-time interaction supplies content that traditional talent or planning once had to provide.
- Stranger-to-stranger networks fit digital gifting better than existing-friend broadcasts.
- The format can operate as low-effort companionship, especially during late-night hours.

## Evidence
- Market scale: [[16-observations-on-livestreaming-in-china-andreessen-horowitz]] says roughly 46% of China's internet population used a livestreaming app in June 2016 and that more than 150 apps launched after Chinese entrepreneurs saw Meerkat's 2015 growth.
- Format shift: [[16-observations-on-livestreaming-in-china-andreessen-horowitz]] contrasts older gaming or performance streams with Inke-style mobile social chatrooms.
- Front-camera logic: [[16-observations-on-livestreaming-in-china-andreessen-horowitz]] argues that inward capture makes livestreaming feel like video selfies rather than news documentation.
- Viewer co-production: [[16-observations-on-livestreaming-in-china-andreessen-horowitz]] describes questions, gifts, requests, leaderboards, and top-fan competition as ways audiences shape the stream.
- Companionship use case: [[16-observations-on-livestreaming-in-china-andreessen-horowitz]] reports peak usage around midnight and interprets the behavior as entertainment-as-companionship.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The article is a 2016 market snapshot and relies on third-party reports, platform examples, and investor interpretation. It does not prove that China's behavior would transfer unchanged to the United States or other markets, and it distinguishes social livestreaming from more utility-centered verticals such as education or news.
