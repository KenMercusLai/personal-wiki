## Definition
[[HumanPremiumServices]] are goods or services whose value increases when customers perceive direct human attention, craft, care, presence, or social interaction as part of what they are buying.

## Current Synthesis
The Vox source reframes automation as a value sorter rather than only a job destroyer. When machines make standardized production cheaper, human effort can become more visible and valuable in domains where customers want provenance, individualized care, personal attention, or social participation. The article's examples range from craft marketplaces and coffee service to therapy, caregiving, pregnancy-related health care, and college education.

The key distinction is category change. A vending-machine coffee, app-guided therapy session, or recorded lecture may be useful, cheaper, and scalable, but it is not simply the automated version of the human service. It removes features many customers treat as central: a barista's visible care, a therapist's undivided attention, or a campus's mentoring and peer environment.

## Key Claims
- Automation can increase the relative premium on human labor when standard goods become cheaper and more abundant.
- Visible inefficiency can be valuable when it signals attention, care, craft, provenance, or social connection.
- Human-premium services appear across consumer goods, food and drink, health care, therapy, caregiving, and education.
- Partial automation can expand access, but it may create a different service category rather than replace the original one.
- Richer economies may allocate more spending toward labor-intensive services whose value depends on human interaction.

## Evidence
- Non-mass production: [[automation-is-making-human-labor-more-valuable-than-ever-the-new-new-economy]] cites [[Etsy]], small farms, wineries, and breweries as cases where the lack of automation is part of the selling point.
- Coffee performance: [[automation-is-making-human-labor-more-valuable-than-ever-the-new-new-economy]] says [[Starbucks]] and independent coffee shops retain demand because customers value personally prepared coffee and are willing to pay for elaborate human preparation.
- Care and therapy: [[automation-is-making-human-labor-more-valuable-than-ever-the-new-new-economy]] argues that app- or video-mediated therapy can help but is worse than a face-to-face meeting with undivided human attention.
- Education experience: [[automation-is-making-human-labor-more-valuable-than-ever-the-new-new-economy]] says online course materials do not replace professors, peers, clubs, social networks, mentoring, and other campus experiences.
- Service-sector growth: [[automation-is-making-human-labor-more-valuable-than-ever-the-new-new-economy]] points to Labor Department projections for therapists, aides, assistants, home health aides, nurse-midwives, genetic counselors, and sonographers as examples of growing personal-service demand.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is an explanatory essay, not a full labor-market model. Its examples show why some services resist direct automation, but they do not prove that all displaced workers can move into human-premium work, that wages will rise evenly, or that customers can always afford human care. Semi-automated services may also be valuable when access, cost, geography, or disability makes the human-premium version unavailable.
