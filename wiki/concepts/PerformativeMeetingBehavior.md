## Definition
[[PerformativeMeetingBehavior]] is meeting conduct or ritual designed mainly to signal intelligence, creativity, seniority, helpfulness, authority, or rational management rather than to improve the group's understanding or decision.

## Current Synthesis
Cooper's satire frames brainstorming meetings as a stage for low-cost status moves. The recurring pattern is to substitute visible signs of contribution for contribution itself: leave helpfully, sketch complexity, make vague analogies, challenge the level of questioning, invoke strategy jargon, claim proximity to executives, or close the discussion with borrowed decisiveness. Barnett adds the organizational mechanism beneath that theater: recurring meetings can also perform [[ProceduralRationality]], making work look orderly even when the meeting lacks a clear problem, agenda, or substantive output.

Bosworth adds the non-satirical product-review version of the pattern. Piling on, pedantry, pontificating, prescription, permission capture, pessimism, and interruptions can all look like active senior participation, but they often make presenters defensive, slow the meeting, or shift responsibility away from the team. The difference between useful critique and performance is partly whether the behavior improves the group's judgment while preserving presenter agency.

## Key Claims
- Visible helpfulness can become a way to exit responsibility while still earning social credit.
- Ambiguous artifacts such as sketches or flowcharts can imply complexity before the speaker has understood the problem.
- Meta-questions and broad analogies can sound profound while avoiding testable claims.
- Jargon and idioms can reject ideas without offering a clear alternative.
- Executive references and launch language can simulate authority in meetings.
- Meetings themselves can become performative when they symbolize rational management before anyone has established why the meeting should exist.
- In review meetings, senior-looking behaviors such as repeated critique, nitpicking, tangents, prescribed fixes, permission transfer, pessimism, and interruptions are performative when they reduce judgment, ownership, or presenter agency.

- Prescription and permission: [[boz-the-p-rules]] says leaders should avoid taking solution ownership or decision responsibility away from the team.
- Presenter agency: [[boz-the-p-rules]] says interruptions leave people feeling powerless and presenters should be allowed to finish and answer fully.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Cooper's source is satire, so it intentionally collapses useful meeting practices into their hollow lookalikes. Barnett's source is a polemical essay, so it can understate cases where scheduled meetings create real alignment, decisions, or documentation. Bosworth's rules come from one leader's product-review practice, so they are not universal meeting law. Real analogies, frameworks, sketches, executive context, critique, meeting rituals, or shipping decisions can be substantive when tied to evidence, ownership, and next steps.
