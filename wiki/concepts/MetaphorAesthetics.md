## Definition
[[MetaphorAesthetics]] is the account of metaphor as an aesthetic event in which sensuous qualities are transferred to a withdrawn object and completed through the reader's imaginative participation.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that metaphor is not simply a decorative comparison or an illumination of a hidden essence. When a metaphor says the pine is a dead flame or the sea is wine-dark, the named object continues to withdraw and cannot fully receive the metaphor's sensual qualities. The reader therefore becomes the substitute real object that performs the metaphor: aesthetic experience becomes theatrical participation rather than detached observation.

## Key Claims
- A metaphor links sensuous qualities from one thing to another without fully revealing the named object's essence.
- The named real object remains withdrawn even under vivid metaphorical description.
- Because the withdrawn object cannot fully appear, the reader or experiencer substitutes for it in the aesthetic event.
- Metaphorical understanding is theatrical because the reader performs the role of the object under the borrowed qualities.
- Examples such as "wine-dark sea" show metaphor replacing ordinary perception with an imaginative role that ordinary sensual experience cannot bear.

## Evidence
- Limited illumination: [[cong-hai-de-ge-er-de-chui-zi-dao-yin-yu-de-xi-ju-xing]] says even elaborate metaphor can only light up one corner of the object.
- Readerly substitution: [[cong-hai-de-ge-er-de-chui-zi-dao-yin-yu-de-xi-ju-xing]] states that the reader serves as the real object in Harman's account of metaphor.
- Theatrical performance: [[cong-hai-de-ge-er-de-chui-zi-dao-yin-yu-de-xi-ju-xing]] frames metaphor as a private drama in which the reader performs the object.
- Wine-dark sea example: [[cong-hai-de-ge-er-de-chui-zi-dao-yin-yu-de-xi-ju-xing]] uses Homer's phrase to show ordinary sea-experience failing to carry the new sensual quality.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source gives a Harman-style philosophical account of metaphor, not a general survey of linguistic, cognitive, or rhetorical theories. Its strongest claims should remain tied to aesthetic experience unless later sources connect them to broader metaphor theory.
