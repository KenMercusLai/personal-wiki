## Definition
[[ProductStickiness]] is the retention pressure that keeps users attached to a product through accumulated investment, habits, progress, identity, or switching friction.

## Current Synthesis
Product stickiness can come from accumulated user investment as well as from continuing utility or delight. Users may continue using a product not because it remains joyful or useful, but because leaving would make past investment feel wasted. Van Schneider uses games such as World of Warcraft and FarmVille as examples where time and progress can become retention pressure after entertainment value has faded. This differs from [[PlatformStickiness]], which is about a broader ecosystem becoming hard to leave because important apps, workflows, services, or data are better supported there.

Mind the Product adds a first-use and connected-product version of the concept. A sticky app should quickly show real user benefit, allow exploration without forced signup, avoid distracting detours, reduce early choices, and make the app experience match the physical product when hardware is involved. The practical product lesson is double-edged. Accumulated investment can make a product more durable, but it can also blur user judgment and keep people in loops they no longer value. Product stickiness therefore needs an ethical boundary: retention created by real continuing value is different from retention created mainly by [[SunkCostFallacy]].

## Key Claims
- Prior user investment can become a retention mechanism.
- Games are especially exposed to sunk-cost stickiness because progress, time, and identity can accumulate visibly.
- Sticky products may retain users even after enjoyment or value has faded.
- Sunk-cost stickiness can be useful to designers but ethically questionable when it exploits loss aversion rather than serving current user value.
- Product-level stickiness is narrower than platform-level stickiness because it concerns attachment to one product experience rather than an ecosystem.
- Healthy app stickiness starts with fast perceived benefit, low-friction exploration, and coherent experience design.

## Evidence
- Game retention: [[are-you-a-victim-of-the-sunk-cost-fallacy-desk-of-van-schneider-medium]] names World of Warcraft and FarmVille as examples where people may keep playing because of accumulated investment.
- Faded value: [[are-you-a-victim-of-the-sunk-cost-fallacy-desk-of-van-schneider-medium]] says some players no longer have much joy in the games but continue because quitting feels like a loss.
- Design use: [[are-you-a-victim-of-the-sunk-cost-fallacy-desk-of-van-schneider-medium]] explicitly says designers can use sunk-cost dynamics to make products more sticky.
- First-use value: [[5-product-design-tips-making-your-app-sticky-from-the-start-mind-the-product]] says users want real benefit fast and may delete apps after one use.
- No-strings exploration: [[5-product-design-tips-making-your-app-sticky-from-the-start-mind-the-product]] argues that forced signup can lose prospective users who are only testing the app before buying.
- Experience attachment: [[5-product-design-tips-making-your-app-sticky-from-the-start-mind-the-product]] says users attach to the experience and value a product brings, not to the object alone.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources give examples and practitioner heuristics rather than retention datasets, and they do not prove that sunk cost, onboarding, benefit messaging, or physical-digital coherence is the dominant reason people continue using any specific product. Product stickiness can also come from social connection, real utility, habit convenience, content depth, switching costs, legitimate long-term mastery, or connected-system convenience. The concept therefore needs source-specific interpretation: retention pressure from accumulated investment is different from retention earned by a valuable first experience.

## What Changed
- Added first-use app retention as a healthier stickiness path based on fast benefit, no-strings exploration, and coherent connected-product experience.
- Preserved the distinction between sunk-cost stickiness and value-earned retention.
