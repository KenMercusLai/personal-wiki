## Definition
[[VentureCapitalPortfolioSizing]] is the practice of choosing how many companies a VC fund should back so its exposure matches startup attrition, power-law return concentration, reserve policy, and the fund's ability to capture rare large winners.

## Current Synthesis
McClure frames VC portfolio size as a statistical exposure problem. In his model, most startups fail or return little, a modest share produce small exits, a few become $100M-plus "centaurs," and only about 1-2% become billion-dollar "unicorns." Because those rare outcomes dominate returns, a small portfolio can be less a demonstration of investor conviction than a high-variance bet that may simply miss every outlier. He therefore recommends much larger portfolios than many traditional VC funds use, especially at seed stage where attrition is higher.

Portfolio breadth addresses the chance that a fund never encounters or backs an outlier; [[VentureCapitalBlindSpots]] address the chance that an investor sees one inside its strike zone and rejects it because of founder pattern matching, price anchoring, personal preference, consensus, static market sizing, premature category closure, or missed behavioral change. Exposure and evaluation are separate failure points: more bets cannot repair systematically biased selection, while good judgment cannot remove small-sample risk.

## Key Claims
- VC portfolio construction should start from the frequency of large outcomes, not only from partner attention or board-seat capacity.
- Small portfolios can produce excellent returns when they happen to include a unicorn, but they can also miss all outlier winners.
- With unicorn rates around 1-2%, fewer than 50-100 investments gives a fund a weak statistical shot unless it has exceptional selection ability.
- Early-stage portfolios need more companies than later-stage portfolios because company-level attrition is higher before traction and financing are proven.
- Follow-on reserves, fees, deployment timing, and recycling matter, but they do not remove the basic rare-winner exposure problem.
- Power-law exposure does not solve omission errors caused by systematically misreading founders, customers, prices, or changing markets.

## Evidence
- Outcome distribution: [[99-vc-problems-but-a-batch-aint-one-why-portfolio-size-matters-for-returns]] says 500 Startups expected 50-80% of investments to return less than 1X, 15-25% to return 2-5X, 5-10% to become $100M-plus centaurs, and 1-2% to become unicorns.
- Size recommendation: [[99-vc-problems-but-a-batch-aint-one-why-portfolio-size-matters-for-returns]] recommends 50-100 companies for later-stage funds and at least 100-200, possibly 500, for early-stage funds.
- Concentration risk: [[99-vc-problems-but-a-batch-aint-one-why-portfolio-size-matters-for-returns]] argues that portfolios under 50-100 companies are gambling with LP money if unicorn hit rates are only 1-2%.
- Model qualification: [[99-vc-problems-but-a-batch-aint-one-why-portfolio-size-matters-for-returns]] explicitly excludes management fees, expenses, follow-on capital, deployment timing, recycling, and other reserve mechanics from its simple model.
- Omission mechanism: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] argues that rare winners can still be missed after entering the funnel because cognitive biases and static market assumptions obscure their potential.
- Historical concentration: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] cites third-party data claiming that 6% of deals generated 60% of US venture-fund returns from 1985 to 2014.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are practitioner arguments rather than complete fund simulations or prospective performance studies. McClure does not prove that every firm can maintain selection quality, founder support, pro-rata access, or governance quality across hundreds of investments and omits follow-on strategy and fund economics. The 645 Ventures essay uses selected successful anti-portfolio cases and third-party historical figures, so it establishes plausible omission mechanisms more strongly than their prevalence or predictive value. Portfolio size, ownership, support capacity, access, and decision quality must be modeled together.

## What Changed
- Distinguished small-sample exposure risk from biased evaluation after a deal enters the funnel.
- Added mistakes of omission as a second reason funds fail to capture rare outliers.
