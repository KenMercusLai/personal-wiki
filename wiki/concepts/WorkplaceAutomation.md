## Definition
[[WorkplaceAutomation]] is the redesign of work so demonstrated technologies perform selected activities or capabilities within jobs, while organizations reconfigure processes, roles, skills, controls, and responsibility around the resulting human-machine system.

## Current Synthesis
The McKinsey model makes the activity, not the occupation, the useful unit of analysis. It decomposes roughly 2,000 US work activities into 18 required capabilities and compares those requirements with technology demonstrated by 2015. On that basis, a large share of paid activity appeared technically automatable even though very few occupations could be automated end to end. The practical implication is distributed change: many roles may lose some tasks, gain exception and relationship work, and depend on redesigned workflows rather than simply disappear.

Cowen’s ATM example supplies a concrete but more skeptical version of that transition. Bank-teller roles reportedly moved away from cash handling and toward sales, interpersonal service, and customer relationships as ATMs lowered branch operating costs. This supports the partial-automation model while challenging the assumption that reassigned human work is necessarily more productive or socially valuable: relationship work can help customers, but it can also become costly competition for market share or pressure-driven selling.

Technical potential is not an adoption forecast. Economics, reliability, software and hardware requirements, workplace unpredictability, regulation, organizational change, and the speed of skill development all mediate whether and when a candidate activity is automated. Benefits can include output, quality, reliability, and augmentation of expertise, but they can coexist with displacement, inequality, privacy loss, scaled errors, and unclear accountability. A sound automation program therefore evaluates the whole process and its human consequences rather than maximizing the count of automated tasks.

## Key Claims
- Activity-level analysis reveals broad automation potential that occupation-level labels conceal.
- Partial automation is more common than complete occupational automation and therefore makes job and process redesign the dominant organizational problem.
- Automation can affect high-wage analytical and managerial work as well as routine low-wage work.
- Value can come from capacity, quality, reliability, and expert augmentation, not only lower labor cost.
- Demonstrated technical capability is only one condition for adoption; economics, integration, regulation, safety, and organizational readiness determine realized change.
- Human outcomes depend on how displaced tasks, retained judgment, new skills, transition costs, and accountability are allocated.
- Continued employment after automation does not prove that replacement tasks raise productivity, job quality, or social welfare.

## Evidence
Activity-versus-occupation distinction:
- [[four-fundamentals-of-workplace-automation-mckinsey]] reports that about 45% of paid US work activities could potentially be automated with technology demonstrated by 2015, while fewer than 5% of occupations could be automated entirely.
- [[four-fundamentals-of-workplace-automation-mckinsey]] estimates that about 60% of occupations contained at least 30% automatable activities.

Cross-wage reach:
- [[four-fundamentals-of-workplace-automation-mckinsey]] finds substantial automatable activity in financial management, medicine, and senior leadership, including more than 20% of CEO work time, while some lower-wage physical roles have little potential under the modeled technology.

Value beyond substitution:
- [[four-fundamentals-of-workplace-automation-mckinsey]] models process benefits at three to ten times cost in selected industries and describes mortgage, medical, legal, and sales examples where automation shifts capacity toward exceptions, advice, and client interaction.

Adoption and risk boundary:
- [[four-fundamentals-of-workplace-automation-mckinsey]] identifies technology maturity, organizational adaptation, industry capital intensity, regulation, privacy, safety, legal responsibility, displacement, and inequality as material constraints or costs.

Employment and value boundary:
- [[in-a-robot-economy-all-humans-will-be-marketers-bloomberg]] uses the ATM and bank-teller case to show an occupation surviving through branch expansion and a shift toward sales and customer relationships.
- [[in-a-robot-economy-all-humans-will-be-marketers-bloomberg]] argues that some replacement work informs and serves customers, while some mainly transfers demand among similar firms or creates harmful sales incentives.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The McKinsey figures are preliminary, model-based estimates for the 2015 US labor market, not measurements of actual adoption, productivity, job loss, or worker welfare. “Currently demonstrated” includes research demonstrations as well as commercial products and does not establish affordability, reliability at scale, integration feasibility, social acceptance, or regulatory approval. Capability thresholds and occupation data can hide variation among workplaces and workers, while wage totals are exposure measures rather than recoverable savings. The selected process examples do not prove the reported three-to-ten-times return generally, and shifting people toward creative, emotional, advisory, or relationship work is a design possibility rather than a guaranteed benefit.

Cowen’s 2017 essay adds a useful welfare qualification but remains an illustrative argument rather than a measured labor-flow or productivity study. Its ATM account lacks the underlying employment series and does not measure wages, hours, target pressure, surveillance, or job quality. Both sources predate later changes in AI capability and should not be treated as current forecasts of which persuasive, creative, or interpersonal tasks remain human.

## What Changed
- Established activity-level decomposition as the primary frame for evaluating workplace automation.
- Distinguished technical potential from adoption, economic return, and realized labor-market outcomes.
- Made role and process redesign, augmentation, transition costs, and accountability part of the automation system boundary.
- Added the distinction between preserving employment and reallocating labor toward work with demonstrable productive or social value.
