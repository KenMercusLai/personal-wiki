## Definition
[[EmployeeEquityRisk]] is the risk that startup employee equity will fail to become usable wealth because the company's outcome, liquidity path, vesting terms, preferences, dilution, or employment duration do not line up in the employee's favor.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames employee equity as a high-variance compensation instrument whose apparent value is usually clear only after the outcome is known. Employees are asked to discount salary today for possible upside later, but they often cannot sell, spend, or value that upside reliably. The article does not reject equity alignment outright; it argues that employees should treat equity as speculative and negotiate for cash when they do not have strong conviction in the company.

## Key Claims
- Employee equity outcomes are easy to judge in hindsight but hard to evaluate at the time of the offer or liquidity choice.
- Illiquidity makes startup equity meaningfully different from salary or other assets that can be exchanged for present value.
- Vesting cliffs and termination rights can leave employees with no shares despite an apparent award.
- Liquidation preferences can make common employee equity worth little or nothing in moderate exits.
- Founder equity and employee equity are asymmetric because founders control allocation, fundraising use, and some secondary-sale opportunities.
- Equity can still align employees with company goals, but that alignment does not erase the risk of low employee payout.

## Evidence
- Hindsight risk: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] describes an early [[TinySpeck]] employee who sold shares before the later [[Slack]] pivot and argues that this looked foolish only after Slack became valuable.
- Illiquidity: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] says employee equity usually cannot be spent, liquidated, or traded before a future event.
- Vesting exposure: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] notes that a typical one-year cliff allows the company to terminate an employee before any equity vests.
- Preference risk: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] highlights share preference as the rule that determines who gets paid first in a liquidation.
- Founder asymmetry: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] says founders can use equity to hire, raise capital, and sometimes sell portions privately while employees hold smaller, less liquid positions.
- Alignment qualification: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] acknowledges that ownership can align employees with company goals while still warning that cash may be better under uncertainty.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a polemical practitioner essay rather than a quantified compensation study. Its strongest warning applies to early-stage or illiquid startup equity; later-stage public-company RSUs, tender offers, extended exercise windows, grant size, strike price, tax treatment, and company-specific liquidity programs can materially change the risk profile.
