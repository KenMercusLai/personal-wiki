## Definition
[[EmployeeEquityRisk]] is the risk that startup employee equity will fail to become usable wealth because the company's outcome, liquidity path, vesting terms, preferences, dilution, or employment duration do not line up in the employee's favor.

## Current Synthesis
[[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] frames employee equity as a high-variance compensation instrument whose apparent value is usually clear only after the outcome is known. Employees are asked to discount salary today for possible upside later, but they often cannot sell, spend, or value that upside reliably. The article does not reject equity alignment outright; it argues that employees should treat equity as speculative and negotiate for cash when they do not have strong conviction in the company.

TechCrunch supplies the more conventional version of the same instrument from the company side. Equity compensation is presented as a deliberate recruiting tool, and the upside is greatest for early joiners because valuations generally rise as a startup grows. The shares stay paper money until a liquidity event, but the article conditionally relaxes the illiquidity claim: many startups let employees sell through a secondary offering, usually priced against the latest valuation. The two accounts agree on the tail risk - if the startup goes bust, the equity is worth nothing - and differ mainly in emphasis, with the investor-facing explainer describing a designed upside and the employee-facing essay warning that the design rarely pays out.

## Key Claims
- Employee equity outcomes are easy to judge in hindsight but hard to evaluate at the time of the offer or liquidity choice.
- Illiquidity makes startup equity meaningfully different from salary or other assets that can be exchanged for present value.
- Illiquidity is not absolute, because some startups permit employees to sell shares in a secondary offering priced off the latest valuation.
- Vesting cliffs and termination rights can leave employees with no shares despite an apparent award.
- Liquidation preferences can make common employee equity worth little or nothing in moderate exits.
- Founder equity and employee equity are asymmetric because founders control allocation, fundraising use, and some secondary-sale opportunities.
- Equity can still align employees with company goals, but that alignment does not erase the risk of low employee payout.

## Evidence
- Hindsight risk: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] describes an early [[TinySpeck]] employee who sold shares before the later [[Slack]] pivot and argues that this looked foolish only after Slack became valuable.
- Illiquidity: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] says employee equity usually cannot be spent, liquidated, or traded before a future event.
- Partial liquidity: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says many startups permit their employees to sell shares through a secondary offering and that those shares are usually sold based on the latest valuation.
- Early-join advantage: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says the upside potential is greatest when an employee joins in the startup's early days, because valuations generally rise over time and the value of the equity compensation grows with them.
- Paper value until an exit: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] describes employee shares as often paper money until a liquidity event such as an IPO or acquisition, and as worth nothing if the startup goes bust.
- Vesting exposure: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] notes that a typical one-year cliff allows the company to terminate an employee before any equity vests.
- Preference risk: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] highlights share preference as the rule that determines who gets paid first in a liquidation.
- Founder asymmetry: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] says founders can use equity to hire, raise capital, and sometimes sell portions privately while employees hold smaller, less liquid positions.
- Alignment qualification: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] acknowledges that ownership can align employees with company goals while still warning that cash may be better under uncertainty.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The while-west source is a polemical practitioner essay rather than a quantified compensation study. Its strongest warning applies to early-stage or illiquid startup equity; later-stage public-company RSUs, tender offers, extended exercise windows, grant size, strike price, tax treatment, and company-specific liquidity programs can materially change the risk profile. TechCrunch partially softens the illiquidity claim by describing employee secondary offerings, but it does not say how common or broadly available those programs are, how shares are priced in practice, or what restrictions apply, and it treats rising valuations as the general case, which flatters the early-joiner argument in flat or declining markets. Neither source quantifies expected employee payout.
