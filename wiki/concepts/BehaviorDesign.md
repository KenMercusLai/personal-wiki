## Definition
[[BehaviorDesign]] is the practice of deliberately shaping conditions so people are more likely to perform beneficial target behaviors.

## Current Synthesis
Within this wiki, behavior design begins with [[BJFogg]]'s model-centered account. The practice names a target behavior and designs around motivation, ability, and prompts. This makes it more concrete than generic persuasion because it gives the designer multiple diagnostic levers. If an action is not happening, the designer can ask whether users care enough, whether the action is simple enough, and whether the prompt arrives at the right time. Schulson's account adds a retention-oriented sequence from [[NirEyal]]: a trigger prompts action, an unpredictable reward sustains anticipation, and user investment raises the likelihood of another cycle.

The sources frame behavior design as an applied discipline rather than a purely academic theory, but they make its ethical ambiguity concrete. The same mechanisms can support medication adherence or extend an unwanted feed session. Existing desire does not settle the issue because design can amplify a ten-minute intention into thirty minutes of use, and attention-funded firms benefit from the extension. "For the better" therefore requires explicit user-goal, stopping, consent, power, and outcome boundaries rather than designer intent alone.

## Key Claims
- Behavior design treats behavior change as a designed outcome rather than only as persuasion.
- Target behaviors can be diagnosed through motivation, ability, and prompts.
- Simplicity is a central design lever because easier behaviors require less motivation.
- Professional behavior design aims to create beneficial change.
- Habit-forming products can combine triggers, easy actions, variable rewards, and accumulating investment into repeated cycles.
- Ethical evaluation must distinguish user-chosen benefit from designer-profitable amplification of an existing want.

## Evidence
- Applied system: [[behavior-model]] says the Fogg Behavior Model is part of a larger system that helps people design for behavior change.
- Model levers: [[behavior-model]] names core motivators, simplicity factors, and prompt types as subcomponents of the framework.
- Practical setting: [[behavior-model]] promotes professional training for people creating products that change behavior for the better.
- Simplicity lever: [[behavior-model]] explains ability through simplicity factors in the context of a prompt.
- Ethical claim: [[behavior-model]] explicitly frames the work as changing people's behavior for the better.
- Habit cycle: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] explains [[NirEyal]]'s trigger-action-variable-reward-investment model through social feeds.
- Beneficial application: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] reports medication reminders as a positive use of habit-forming design.
- Ethical boundary: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] contrasts persuasion with coercion but argues that designed interaction can amplify use beyond a person's initial intention.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Fogg's source comes from the model's author and includes training promotion, while Schulson's source is a popular essay built from analogy, interviews, and policy argument. Neither tests the models, establishes that every element is necessary, or supplies a general threshold between persuasion and coercion. "For the better" is underspecified; behavior design can support user goals, but it can also become manipulative when product goals replace user welfare. Variable reward is not proof of addiction, and useful, deliberate high-frequency behavior should not be classified as harmful from duration alone.
