## Definition
[[AggregationTheory]] is a platform-strategy frame in which companies gain power by controlling demand-side access to supply, typically through direct user relationships, low marginal user-serving costs, and demand-driven multi-sided networks.

## Current Synthesis
Dellanna adopts the Stratechery frame but argues that aggregator power should not be understood only as matching supply and demand. In markets where unfamiliar parties must transact, aggregators such as [[Airbnb]] and [[Uber]] become the trusted intermediary, making unfamiliar suppliers transactable while centralizing trust, attention, and profit. Thompson adds the advertising-super-aggregator case: because [[Facebook]] and [[Google]] control demand, they must keep users engaged even though advertisers pay for access to that attention. Their [[DataFactories|data factories]] deepen the advantage by combining user, supplier, advertiser, and third-party inputs into recommendations, distribution, targeting, and hidden processed profiles. His earlier Facebook essay adds the monopoly implication: zero price and near-zero marginal user cost can conceal power expressed through supplier revenue sharing, differentiated ad scarcity, and an incumbent's ability to appropriate entrant innovation through its network.

## Key Claims
- Aggregators control demand by becoming the preferred access point to supply.
- Matching supply and demand is only one aggregator advantage; reducing direct trust requirements is another.
- In trust-sensitive markets, supplier popularity can substitute for trust when no better mechanism exists.
- A trusted aggregator can flatten supplier profit distribution by making unfamiliar suppliers transactable.
- The same trust transfer can centralize profits and power at the aggregator layer.
- Aggregator disruption is hardest on demand ownership and more plausible around new trust-minimization mechanisms.
- In advertising aggregation, users are simultaneously the demand to retain and the attention advertisers pay to reach; data processing reinforces that position while monopoly costs may surface on other sides of the market.

## Evidence
- Demand-control frame: [[aggregators-and-trust-luca-dellanna]] summarizes Ben Thompson's aggregator traits as direct user relationships, near-zero marginal user-serving costs, and demand-driven networks.
- Trust proxy: [[aggregators-and-trust-luca-dellanna]] argues that customers without quick trust assessment use popularity as a supplier-trust proxy.
- Trust transfer: [[aggregators-and-trust-luca-dellanna]] says [[Airbnb]] lets guests and hosts rely on reviews, insurance, payment, reimbursement, and delisting rather than preexisting relationships.
- Profit redistribution: [[aggregators-and-trust-luca-dellanna]] argues that aggregators flatten supplier profits while centralizing profits at the aggregator layer.
- Disruption vector: [[aggregators-and-trust-luca-dellanna]] points to smart locks and smart contracts as possible ways to reduce the platform's trust-provision role.
- User-versus-product distinction: [[data-factories-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] argues that aggregators prioritize users because demand control is their power, while advertisers buy access to user attention.
- Data-processing extension: [[data-factories-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] shows content, ads, uploaded customer lists, third-party records, and external integrations feeding processed product and targeting outputs.
- Concentrated equilibrium: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] argues that reinforcing demand and supply participation make monopoly the expected equilibrium for an aggregator, all else equal.
- Side-specific costs: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] locates possible harm in publisher surplus, advertising prices, and innovation rather than relying only on the free user-facing market.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
All three sources are strategic essays rather than empirical measurements of profit distribution, take rates, user welfare, targeting lift, privacy harm, or competitive outcomes. Dellanna mostly develops marketplace examples and gives advertising aggregation less treatment; Thompson develops Facebook and generalizes portions of the model to Google without a complete technical audit. The monopoly essay does not establish a legal market, the counterfactual publisher share, the advertising price response, or how much innovation copying displaced. Demand ownership may still dominate trust or transparency reforms because users, suppliers, and advertisers go where the existing network is deepest.

## What Changed
- Added the monopoly implication of aggregation: low user prices can coexist with supplier, advertiser, and innovation costs.
- Added Facebook's publisher surplus, ad-scarcity, and network-leveraged copying as source-scoped mechanisms rather than proven general outcomes.
