## Definition
[[AggregationTheory]] is a platform-strategy frame in which companies gain power by controlling demand-side access to supply, typically through direct user relationships, low marginal user-serving costs, and demand-driven multi-sided networks.

## Current Synthesis
Dellanna adopts the Stratechery frame but argues that aggregator power should not be understood only as matching supply and demand. In markets where unfamiliar parties must transact, aggregators such as [[Airbnb]] and [[Uber]] become the trusted intermediary, making unfamiliar suppliers transactable while centralizing trust, attention, and profit. Thompson's advertising cases show how control of user demand, data processing, and a near-zero marginal user cost can produce power over publishers, advertisers, and product entrants even when the user price is zero.

The new agent thesis proposes a further layer. Web abundance made discovery scarce; agentic action may make doing things abundant, leaving volition or inspiration scarce. If users express goals to one context-rich agent, apps and services can be abstracted into suppliers selected and operated behind that interface. [[Muse]] and [[MicrosoftCopilot]] are presented as consumer and enterprise bids to combine platform and aggregator power. This is a forecast, not evidence that agents have already aggregated demand or displaced application interfaces.

## Key Claims
- Aggregators control demand by becoming the preferred access point to supply.
- Matching supply and demand is only one aggregator advantage; reducing direct trust requirements is another.
- In trust-sensitive markets, platform trust can make unfamiliar suppliers transactable and flatten supplier profit distribution.
- The same trust transfer can centralize profits and power at the aggregator layer.
- Aggregator disruption is hardest on demand ownership and more plausible around new trust-minimization mechanisms or a new primary interface.
- In advertising aggregation, data processing reinforces demand control while monopoly costs may surface on supplier, advertiser, and innovation sides.
- Agent aggregation could move the scarce control point from discovering supply to framing intent and executing across suppliers.

## Evidence
- Demand-control frame: [[aggregators-and-trust-luca-dellanna]] summarizes Ben Thompson's aggregator traits as direct user relationships, near-zero marginal user-serving costs, and demand-driven networks.
- Trust proxy: [[aggregators-and-trust-luca-dellanna]] argues that customers without quick trust assessment use popularity as a supplier-trust proxy.
- Trust transfer: [[aggregators-and-trust-luca-dellanna]] says [[Airbnb]] lets guests and hosts rely on reviews, insurance, payment, reimbursement, and delisting rather than preexisting relationships.
- Profit redistribution: [[aggregators-and-trust-luca-dellanna]] argues that aggregators flatten supplier profits while centralizing profits at the aggregator layer.
- Disruption vector: [[aggregators-and-trust-luca-dellanna]] points to smart locks and smart contracts as possible ways to reduce the platform's trust-provision role.
- User-versus-product distinction: [[data-factories-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] argues that aggregators prioritize users because demand control is their power, while advertisers buy access to user attention.
- Data-processing extension: [[data-factories-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] shows content, ads, uploaded customer lists, third-party records, and external integrations feeding processed product and targeting outputs.
- Concentrated equilibrium: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] argues that reinforcing demand and supply participation make monopoly the expected equilibrium for an aggregator, all else equal.
- Side-specific costs: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] locates possible harm in publisher surplus, advertising prices, and innovation rather than relying only on the free user-facing market.
- Scarcity shift: [[apps-agents-and-aggregation]] argues that web abundance made discovery scarce, while agentic execution makes inspiration or volition the prospective bottleneck.
- Agent interface: [[apps-agents-and-aggregation]] says a context-rich agent can turn apps and services into implementation suppliers behind one user relationship.
- Distribution cases: [[apps-agents-and-aggregation]] presents Meta's consumer reach and Microsoft's employee reach, identity, and work surfaces as advantages in the contest to become the primary agent.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
All four sources are strategic essays rather than empirical measurements of profit distribution, take rates, user welfare, targeting lift, privacy harm, agent adoption, or competitive outcomes. Dellanna mostly develops marketplace examples; Thompson develops Facebook and generalizes portions of the model to Google without a complete technical audit. The monopoly essay does not establish a legal market or measured counterfactual. The agent essay extrapolates from one personal workflow and product announcements: services retain access controls and users may prefer multiple agents, direct apps, fixed UI, or specialized providers. Concentrating context, credentials, intent, and execution can also increase privacy, security, governance, switching, and systemic-failure costs.
