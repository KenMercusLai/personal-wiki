## Definition
[[SelfEfficacy]] is a person's belief that they are capable of organizing and carrying out actions that produce intended results.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat self-efficacy as grounded in evidence of action rather than positive assertion alone. Zak Slayback connects clear goals with repeatable, manageable tasks whose successful completion supports progressively larger attempts. Lars漫步 adds a record-keeping mechanism: an achievement journal preserves small completed actions that memory or self-criticism might discount. Smith and Merwin add intervention evidence: exercise programs produce moderately large average improvements in self-efficacy, and mastery, planning, feedback, and self-monitoring may help participants maintain activity.

The combined loop is action, visible evidence, and a more credible next attempt. In [[ExerciseForMentalHealth]], this can become reciprocal: exercise mastery increases confidence, confidence supports [[ExerciseAdherence]], and continued participation creates further mastery opportunities. The intervention estimate is heterogeneous and does not show that confidence alone causes clinical improvement. A journal can preserve mastery evidence, but it cannot substitute for competence, feedback, opportunity, health, external support, or structural access.

## Key Claims
- Self-efficacy develops more credibly through successful execution than through aspiration alone.
- Smaller related tasks can condition a person for more demanding goals.
- Progressively increasing difficulty can build capability without requiring immediate success at the end goal.
- Recording small achievements may preserve mastery evidence that would otherwise be forgotten or dismissed.
- Exercise interventions can strengthen self-efficacy on average, while the mechanism and individual response remain variable.
- Self-efficacy may support maintenance by making later action feel achievable, but perceived capability still needs contact with accurate feedback and real constraints.

## Evidence
- Progressive load: [[zak-slayback-you-have-to-show-up-every-day]] compares efficacy development with increasing controlled weight rather than attempting an extreme lift immediately.
- Repeated mastery: [[zak-slayback-you-have-to-show-up-every-day]] says regularly executing habits and increasingly larger goals builds perceived capability.
- Achievement evidence: [[larsman-bu-cong-wei-shen-me-dao-xie-shen-me-dai-ni-chong-xin-ren-shi-ri-ji]] recommends recording up to five completed actions, including small acts of restraint, care, learning, and household maintenance.
- Review and motivation: [[larsman-bu-cong-wei-shen-me-dao-xie-shen-me-dai-ni-chong-xin-ren-shi-ri-ji]] argues that revisiting accumulated achievements can reveal progress and support later attempts, while supplying no measured outcome.
- Intervention estimate: [[nihms-1685283]] reports a meta-analytic average effect size of 0.59 for exercise interventions on self-efficacy across adolescent and adult cohorts.
- Maintenance loop: [[nihms-1685283]] connects exercise mastery and improved self-efficacy with a greater likelihood of sustained physical activity, while presenting the wider treatment mechanism as synergistic rather than settled.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The two practitioner essays do not distinguish perceived capability from measured competence or provide causal evidence for their mechanisms. The exercise review adds intervention evidence but reports a pooled average across cohorts and does not establish that self-efficacy mediates psychiatric improvement for every participant. Progressive practice, achievement logging, or exercise may fail when tasks are poorly chosen, feedback is misleading, symptoms or structural barriers dominate, records become performative, or workload ignores recovery, disability, health, safety, and resources. A completed task can support confidence without proving readiness for a different task, and high confidence can still be inaccurate. “Every day” is therefore best understood as continuity of engagement, not a universal requirement for literal daily performance.

## What Changed
- Added exercise-intervention evidence for average improvement in self-efficacy.
- Added a reciprocal mastery-and-maintenance loop while preserving uncertainty about mediation and individual response.
