## Definition
[[SelfEfficacy]] is a person's belief that they are capable of organizing and carrying out actions that produce intended results.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat self-efficacy as grounded in evidence of action rather than positive assertion alone. Zak Slayback connects clear goals with repeatable, manageable tasks whose successful completion supports progressively larger attempts. Lars漫步 adds a record-keeping mechanism: an achievement journal preserves small completed actions that memory or self-criticism might discount.

The combined loop is action, visible evidence, and a more credible next attempt. A journal can help retain mastery evidence, but it cannot substitute for competence, feedback, opportunity, health, or structural support, and neither source directly measures whether recording achievements causes confidence or performance to improve.

## Key Claims
- Self-efficacy develops more credibly through successful execution than through aspiration alone.
- Smaller related tasks can condition a person for more demanding goals.
- Progressively increasing difficulty can build capability without requiring immediate success at the end goal.
- Recording small achievements may preserve mastery evidence that would otherwise be forgotten or dismissed.
- Habit systems create repeated opportunities for action and review, but perceived capability still needs contact with accurate feedback and real constraints.

## Evidence
- Progressive load: [[zak-slayback-you-have-to-show-up-every-day]] compares efficacy development with increasing controlled weight rather than attempting an extreme lift immediately.
- Repeated mastery: [[zak-slayback-you-have-to-show-up-every-day]] says regularly executing habits and increasingly larger goals builds perceived capability.
- Achievement evidence: [[larsman-bu-cong-wei-shen-me-dao-xie-shen-me-dai-ni-chong-xin-ren-shi-ri-ji]] recommends recording up to five completed actions, including small acts of restraint, care, learning, and household maintenance.
- Review and motivation: [[larsman-bu-cong-wei-shen-me-dao-xie-shen-me-dai-ni-chong-xin-ren-shi-ri-ji]] argues that revisiting accumulated achievements can reveal progress and support later attempts, while supplying no measured outcome.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are practitioner essays and do not distinguish perceived capability from measured competence or provide causal evidence for their mechanisms. Progressive practice or achievement logging may fail when tasks are poorly chosen, feedback is misleading, structural barriers dominate, records become performative, or workload ignores recovery, disability, health, and available resources. A list of completed tasks can support memory without proving readiness for a different or harder task. “Every day” is therefore best understood as continuity of engagement, not a universal requirement for literal daily performance.

## What Changed
- Added achievement journaling as a way to preserve small mastery experiences for later review.
- Distinguished recording evidence of capability from proving competence or causing confidence.
