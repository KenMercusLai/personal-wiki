## Definition
[[TechnologySCurve]] is a model in which a product or architecture improves slowly while searching for fit, rapidly after gaining traction, and slowly again as it matures and approaches practical limits.

## Current Synthesis
The source uses the S-curve to distinguish current financial performance from future strategic position. A mature technology can be optimized, reliable, profitable, and still be near the flattening end of its improvement path. A replacement often begins below incumbent performance in a niche, looks unimportant by current-market measures, and then improves rapidly enough to start a new curve.

The durable strategic task is therefore not to extend one curve indefinitely but to finance and learn across successive curves. The inspected diagrams reinforce the sequence: early experimentation and low returns, rapid improvement and scaling, maturity and flattening, then overlap with a challenger whose acceleration begins before the incumbent's reported results visibly collapse.

## Key Claims
- Technology performance often develops through slow, rapid, and flattening phases rather than a straight line.
- Product-market fit and scaling occupy different parts of the curve and require different organizational behavior.
- Maximum efficiency and profitability can coincide with declining strategic optionality.
- New architectures often begin in small niches that look unattractive by incumbent standards.
- Revenue can peak after the underlying product trajectory has begun losing relevance.
- Long-lived firms need a repeatable way to move resources from a mature curve to uncertain successor curves.

## Evidence
- Curve shape: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] describes slow early improvement, rapid post-fit growth, and a mature plateau of incremental optimization.
- Successive architectures: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] argues that disruptors begin at the low end or in niches before improving on performance and cost.
- Lagging results: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] and its revenue chart show BlackBerry revenue rising through 2011 despite the source locating the strategic break at the 2007 iPhone launch.
- Visual model: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] includes diagrams of one product S-curve, successive incumbent and challenger curves, and revenue peaking after product relevance turns downward.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
An S-curve is a simplifying model, not a forecast by itself. The source does not define the performance metric, estimate inflection points, compare failed challengers, or show that every product follows one clean curve. Mature technologies can continue improving, combine with new architectures, or survive in valuable niches, while most attempted successor products never reach rapid growth.

- Created a reusable distinction between a product's financial peak and its underlying technology trajectory.
- Added successive-curve transition as the strategic problem linking mature products to new architectures.
