## Definition
[[PlatformPublisherRevenue]] describes the direct or indirect money professional publishers receive from large technology platforms that distribute, host, recommend, or monetize their content.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames publisher-platform economics as a broken but fixable relationship. Large platforms benefit from professional content while capturing much of the advertising value, so publishers face pressure to lower content costs and compete with cheaper, lower-quality material. BuzzFeed's proposed answer is to keep producing valuable content, push platforms to reward it, and make platform revenue one part of a broader business model rather than the only strategy.

## Key Claims
- Platforms and publishers have linked problems: platforms struggle with low-quality content, while publishers struggle to be paid fairly.
- Rewarding valuable content is presented as a shared-interest fix for platforms, publishers, and the public.
- Platform revenue is already material for BuzzFeed and expected to grow.
- Publishers still need negotiating leverage from quality content rather than dependence on a hoped-for platform windfall.
- Platform economics affect public culture because they influence whether quality journalism and entertainment remain broadly accessible.

## Evidence
- Fair-payment diagnosis: [[9-boxes]] says Google and Facebook take the majority of ad revenue while paying content creators too little for their user value.
- Quality-content fix: [[9-boxes]] argues platforms should reward valuable content because platforms need better content, publishers need sustainability, and the public needs quality news and entertainment.
- Revenue chart: [[9-boxes]] shows direct revenue from Facebook, Amazon, Netflix, and Google increasing from $7 million in 2014 to $14 million in 2015, $32 million in 2016, and $52 million in 2017.
- Strategic caution: [[9-boxes]] says BuzzFeed will not simply wait for a platform revenue windfall.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is written from BuzzFeed's publisher perspective and does not include the platforms' accounting, policy rationale, or independent market data. It also predates later platform, regulatory, subscription, short-video, and AI-era changes in publisher economics.
