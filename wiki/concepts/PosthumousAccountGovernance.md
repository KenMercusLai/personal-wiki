## Definition
[[PosthumousAccountGovernance]] is the allocation of preservation, deletion, access, management, and privacy rights for a person's online account after their death.

## Current Synthesis
The strongest default in the available source is continuity with choices made while alive: when intent is unknown, preserve the account's existing content and audience boundaries without allowing anyone to log in as the deceased. Advance directives can replace that default through deletion instructions or a nominated steward, but stewardship should be capability-limited rather than equivalent to inheriting the identity.

This is a multi-party governance problem, not a simple transfer of property. Relatives may disagree, grief can create urgent and understandable requests, and private communications include living participants who expected confidentiality. A defensible system therefore needs advance preference capture, bounded roles, authentication and memorial states, clear deletion procedures, protection for third-party communications, and lawful escalation for exceptional cases.

## Key Claims
- The deceased person's recorded wishes should generally outrank survivor preference where law permits.
- Unknown intent favors a conservative default that preserves prior content and audience boundaries while preventing new impersonating logins.
- Memorial stewardship should grant enumerated capabilities rather than full access to the deceased person's account and identity.
- Private messages create joint privacy interests because living correspondents did not necessarily consent to family disclosure.
- Digital reminders are not uniformly comforting; product behavior can either support memory or unexpectedly intensify grief.
- Ordinary inheritance concepts may not resolve access to hosted accounts, confidential communications, or platform-controlled identity.

## Evidence
- Conservative default and audience continuity: [[hard-questions-what-should-happen-to-peoples-online-identity-when-they-die-facebook-newsroom]] describes Facebook's memorial label, blocked logins, unchanged content, and unchanged visibility when user intent is unknown.
- Advance choice and bounded stewardship: [[hard-questions-what-should-happen-to-peoples-online-identity-when-they-die-facebook-newsroom]] distinguishes deletion instructions and limited legacy-contact powers from impersonation or private-message access.
- Multi-party privacy and grief: [[hard-questions-what-should-happen-to-peoples-online-identity-when-they-die-facebook-newsroom]] presents conflicting family requests, correspondents' confidentiality, painful automated reminders, and incomplete legal guidance.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is Facebook's own 2017 policy account, not a comparative study of platforms, jurisdictions, grieving families, or dispute outcomes. Preserving an account when intent is unknown is itself a substantive choice and may conflict with what the person wanted. Family testimony about deletion preferences can be sincere but difficult to verify, while strict privacy can deny survivors information they experience as important for understanding a death. The cited US statutes and practices are historical and jurisdiction-specific, so this page is not legal advice or a statement of current Facebook policy.

## What Changed
- Established posthumous accounts as a multi-party governance problem rather than ordinary property inheritance.
- Distinguished memorial stewardship from identity succession and access to private communications.
- Added grief-sensitive product behavior as part of account lifecycle design.
