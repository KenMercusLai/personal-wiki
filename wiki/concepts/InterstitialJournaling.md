## Definition
[[InterstitialJournaling]] is a work-transition practice in which a person records what they just did, what still occupies their attention, the literal first action of the next project, and how they intend to approach it.

## Current Synthesis
The method turns the boundary between projects into a short, repeatable ritual. A minimal entry includes a timestamp, full-sentence reflection on the completed work, a very small starting action for the upcoming work, and brief strategic consideration of scope, time, mood, or skill. This creates a written handoff from one cognitive context to another: unresolved thoughts are externalized, the next project's activation energy is reduced, and automatic avoidance has a chance to become a conscious problem that can be addressed.

The source also distinguishes the active daily work surface from a long-running backlog. Interstitial journaling can replace the checklist repeatedly consulted during the day without pretending that every someday task belongs in the journal. An extended version records work continuously, using the journal as a recovery point whenever distraction is noticed.

## Key Claims
- Project boundaries can serve as reliable cues for a journaling habit even when the projects themselves continually change.
- A useful transition entry closes the previous context before planning the next one.
- Naming a literal first action can reduce the ambiguity that makes project-sized to-dos difficult to start.
- Brief strategy reflection can adapt scope and method to available time rather than allowing default behavior to decide.
- Continuous work journaling can expose distraction, mood repair, and missing skill or strategy as concrete causes of delay.
- The method is a candidate replacement for a daily working list, not for a durable backlog of possible future work.

## Evidence
- Transition ritual: [[replace-your-to-do-list-with-interstitial-journaling-to-increase-productivity]] instructs readers to journal whenever they move from one project to another and compares the practice to an athlete's learned pivot cue.
- Cognitive closure: [[replace-your-to-do-list-with-interstitial-journaling-to-increase-productivity]] asks what part of the completed project remains mentally active so those thoughts can be written down before switching.
- Starting friction: [[replace-your-to-do-list-with-interstitial-journaling-to-increase-productivity]] contrasts project-sized tasks with literal first actions such as finding a phone number or opening an application.
- Strategy adjustment: [[replace-your-to-do-list-with-interstitial-journaling-to-increase-productivity]] describes choosing the easiest of five articles to edit after noticing that only one hour was available.
- Procrastination recovery: [[replace-your-to-do-list-with-interstitial-journaling-to-increase-productivity]] gives a 56-minute editing example in which the journal records three distractions and identifies skill uncertainty behind one of them.
- Working-list scope: [[replace-your-to-do-list-with-interstitial-journaling-to-increase-productivity]] says the journal can replace the list used to choose and update today's work while leaving long-term backlogs intact.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is one author's personal experience, conceptual reasoning, and informal feedback from a productivity-oriented coaching group. It does not compare the method against ordinary lists, time blocking, breaks, or other transition rituals, and the article's quantitative multitasking claim is not recoverable from the saved source. Frequent journaling also adds overhead and may interrupt rather than support work whose transitions are externally imposed, urgent, physical, collaborative, or safety-critical. The recommended action granularity is person-dependent: a literal micro-step can reduce ambiguity for one person while feeling too trivial to motivate another.

- Established interstitial journaling as a distinct transition ritual that combines cognitive closure, action decomposition, and strategy selection.
