A narrow market wedge is the discipline of entering a large market through a specific slice that a startup can understand, serve, and win. In the Drift interview summary, Pat Grady says founders should not target a big market from time zero. They should start with a narrow slice of that market and earn the right to evolve into more of it over time.

The concept reconciles ambition with sequence. A large market can matter because venture-scale companies often need room to compound, but trying to serve the entire market immediately creates vague positioning, excessive product scope, and weak proof. A narrower wedge makes the initial customer, problem, product promise, and feedback loop concrete.

Grady also frames this as an investor learning problem. Investors can underestimate what a market can become once a narrow entry point starts working. The wedge therefore should not be confused with a small ambition; it is a way to make the first market real enough that broader expansion becomes earned rather than assumed.

## Signals

- The initial customer group is specific enough for the product and message to be sharp.
- The wedge belongs to a larger market that could expand if the startup earns trust and learns quickly.
- Early proof comes from solving one urgent problem, not from claiming the whole market category.
- Expansion follows demonstrated pull from adjacent customers or workflows.
