## Definition
[[OsborneEffect]] is the product-transition failure mode where announcing a better future product or platform too early suppresses demand for the current product before the replacement can carry the business.

## Current Synthesis
The BlackBerry source uses Nokia as the smartphone-era example. Stephen Elop's memo recognized that devices had become an ecosystem war, but the public commitment to [[WindowsPhone]] made existing Nokia handsets look like dead ends before replacement devices were ready. The result, in Gassee's account, was not simply choosing the wrong technology; it was destroying near-term demand during the gap between old and new platforms.

## Key Claims
- Premature transition announcements can harm current-product demand even when the strategic diagnosis is right.
- The risk is highest when current customers, carriers, distributors, or sales channels believe the existing line has no future.
- Ecosystem transitions make the effect sharper because users and partners care about long-term platform viability.

## Evidence
- Nokia case: [[blackberry-meditation-at-the-grave-jean-louis-gassee-medium]] says carriers, distributors, and customers stopped buying no-future Nokia handsets after the Windows Phone transition became public.
- Ecosystem framing: [[blackberry-meditation-at-the-grave-jean-louis-gassee-medium]] quotes Elop's memo that device competition had become a war of ecosystems.
- Outcome: [[blackberry-meditation-at-the-grave-jean-louis-gassee-medium]] links the revenue collapse to Microsoft's later purchase of Nokia's devices business and subsequent write-off.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source applies the Osborne Effect label to Nokia but does not prove it was the only cause of Nokia's decline. Platform choice, app availability, Symbian fragmentation, pricing, carrier support, regional competition, and execution quality may also have contributed.

## What Changed
- Created the Osborne Effect concept from the Nokia transition case.
