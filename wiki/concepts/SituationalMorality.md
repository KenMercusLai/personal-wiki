## Definition
[[SituationalMorality]] is the view that human moral behavior is strongly shaped by scarcity, roles, power relations, rules, and community norms rather than flowing only from fixed inner goodness or evil.

## Current Synthesis
The source favors a Gaozi-like view of human nature as water whose direction depends on terrain. It uses famine, concentration-camp prisoner functionaries, the Stanford prison experiment, and rescuers during World War II to argue that environments can either deform ordinary people or make care more reachable. The practical conclusion is not fatalism but field selection: avoid environments that trigger cruelty, and seek communities that normalize decency.

## Key Claims
- Human nature may be directionless like water until terrain gives it a path.
- Extreme scarcity can make ordinary moral categories inadequate.
- Roles and power imbalances can rapidly normalize cruelty or submission.
- Communities that model dignity and compassion can make courageous care more likely.
- Choosing one's information, friends, neighborhood, and culture helps choose the moral field that will shape future reactions.

## Evidence
- Water metaphor: [[chang]] invokes Gaozi's comparison of human nature to water that flows according to where the channel is opened.
- Scarcity pressure: [[chang]] uses famine examples to argue that survival environments can distort behavior beyond ordinary moral judgment.
- Role pressure: [[chang]] cites the Stanford prison experiment as an example of situational power turning ordinary students toward abuse or breakdown.
- Compassion field: [[chang]] contrasts this with people who sheltered Jews during World War II after growing up in families or communities that emphasized dignity and justice.
- Daily moral atmosphere: [[chang]] argues that friends, feeds, communities, and cultural products quietly define whether honesty, kindness, complaint, anxiety, or indifference feels normal.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is careful to avoid saying environment removes all responsibility, but its examples risk overgeneralizing from extreme or contested cases. The Stanford prison experiment in particular has a debated research history, so the concept should be treated as a moral-psychological lens rather than a settled empirical proof.
