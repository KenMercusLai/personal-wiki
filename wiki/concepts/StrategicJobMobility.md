## Definition
[[StrategicJobMobility]] is the deliberate use of employer changes to seek faster learning, greater responsibility, better fit, or improved market position rather than treating either long tenure or frequent movement as an end in itself.

## Current Synthesis
The sources support a conditional rather than calendrical case for changing employers. [[VivianGiang]] presents the broad mechanism through [[PattyMcCord]]'s recommendation that young workers consider moving every three to four years: a newcomer faces a steep learning curve, must understand a different organization, build credibility, contribute quickly, and adapt beyond an established comfort zone. Mobility may also expose workers to current labor-market demand rather than relying on internal raises, but the article does not establish that cadence as universally optimal.

[[RyanHoover]] supplies the complementary stay-or-leave case. He chose to leave [[PlayHaven]] after its successful turnaround even though he was well paid, liked his colleagues, and still believed in the company's vision. Growth had narrowed his generalist role, a new specialist assumed challenges he enjoyed, and his interest in gaming did not return after a vacation. His decision separates employer health from individual fit: a company can be improving while the learning, scope, or domain connection that justified staying weakens.

A move therefore has strategic value when the new path plausibly creates learning, responsibility, fit, or compensation gains that exceed switching costs and the value still available in the current role. Relevant signals include sustained domain interest, the challenge and breadth of actual work, future option value, and whether a break restores engagement. None is decisive alone: loss of passion may reflect burnout or conditions that can change, and building for oneself may aid empathy without guaranteeing market demand or execution.

## Key Claims
- Entering a new company can accelerate learning by forcing rapid adaptation to unfamiliar systems, people, and expectations.
- Short expected tenure can create urgency to contribute and establish credibility, but does not itself prove high performance or loyalty.
- Employer changes may improve advancement or compensation when internal progression lags the external market.
- Job-hopping stigma is context-dependent and can weaken as labor-market expectations shift.
- A healthy, growing employer can still become a poor individual fit when role scope, learning rate, or domain interest changes.
- Mobility should be judged by learning, role fit, and option value, not followed as a fixed timer or reserved for company failure.

## Evidence
- Learning mechanism: [[vivian-giang-you-should-plan-on-switching-jobs-every-three-years-for-the-rest-of-your-life]] attributes faster skill building to the learning curve created by changing companies.
- Suggested cadence: [[vivian-giang-you-should-plan-on-switching-jobs-every-three-years-for-the-rest-of-your-life]] reports McCord's recommendation that young people consider moving every three to four years.
- Reputation and contribution: [[vivian-giang-you-should-plan-on-switching-jobs-every-three-years-for-the-rest-of-your-life]] argues that job hoppers may learn quickly and try to make a strong impression during limited tenure.
- Compensation possibility: [[vivian-giang-you-should-plan-on-switching-jobs-every-three-years-for-the-rest-of-your-life]] cites a large long-tenure pay penalty from secondary reporting, while providing no method sufficient to infer causation.
- Successful-company departure: [[knowing-when-its-time-to-move-on-ryan-hoover-medium]] says Hoover left despite good pay, close colleagues, reported company growth, and continued belief in PlayHaven's vision.
- Role and domain fit: [[knowing-when-its-time-to-move-on-ryan-hoover-medium]] connects the move to specialization of a formerly generalist role, fading interest in gaming, and a vacation that did not restore that interest.
- Opportunity criteria: [[knowing-when-its-time-to-move-on-ryan-hoover-medium]] evaluates future work through genuine desire, passion for the product and market, challenge, and expected learning.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence consists of a brief 2016 advice article and one 2013 first-person departure essay, not longitudinal or experimental research. Workers who change jobs and workers who stay may differ in occupation, ambition, bargaining power, geography, performance, or opportunity, so a pay association does not prove a mobility effect. Hoover's later outcomes are not examined, and his introspection cannot show that fading interest was permanent or that passion, generalist scope, and founder-user alignment predict sustainable success. Switching also carries search costs, probation risk, lost benefits, visa or caregiving constraints, weaker institutional knowledge, and the possibility of leaving before a valuable project or relationship compounds. Some roles reward longer horizons, while repeated short stays may still concern employers in particular labor markets.

## What Changed
- Added successful-company departure as a distinct case where individual learning and fit can decline while the employer grows.
- Added role specialization, persistent domain interest, and recovery after a break as qualified decision signals.
- Preserved the distinction between strategic mobility and calendar-driven job hopping.
