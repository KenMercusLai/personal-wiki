## Definition
[[StrategicJobMobility]] is the deliberate use of employer changes to seek faster learning, greater responsibility, better fit, or improved market position rather than treating either long tenure or frequent movement as an end in itself.

## Current Synthesis
[[VivianGiang]] presents a strong version of the strategy through [[PattyMcCord]]'s recommendation that young workers consider changing companies every three to four years. The proposed mechanism is repeated exposure to steep learning curves: a newcomer must understand a different organization, build credibility, contribute quickly, and adapt outside an established comfort zone. Mobility may also expose workers to current labor-market demand rather than relying on internal raises.

The useful synthesis is conditional rather than calendrical. A move has strategic value when the new role creates meaningful learning, responsibility, fit, or compensation gains that exceed switching costs and the value still available in the current role. The source does not establish three years as an optimal universal threshold.

## Key Claims
- Entering a new company can accelerate learning by forcing rapid adaptation to unfamiliar systems, people, and expectations.
- Short expected tenure can create urgency to contribute and establish credibility, but does not itself prove high performance or loyalty.
- Employer changes may improve advancement or compensation when internal progression lags the external market.
- Job-hopping stigma is context-dependent and can weaken as labor-market expectations shift.
- Mobility should be judged by learning and option value, not followed as a fixed timer.

## Evidence
- Learning mechanism: [[vivian-giang-you-should-plan-on-switching-jobs-every-three-years-for-the-rest-of-your-life]] attributes faster skill building to the learning curve created by changing companies.
- Suggested cadence: [[vivian-giang-you-should-plan-on-switching-jobs-every-three-years-for-the-rest-of-your-life]] reports McCord's recommendation that young people consider moving every three to four years.
- Reputation and contribution: [[vivian-giang-you-should-plan-on-switching-jobs-every-three-years-for-the-rest-of-your-life]] argues that job hoppers may learn quickly and try to make a strong impression during limited tenure.
- Compensation possibility: [[vivian-giang-you-should-plan-on-switching-jobs-every-three-years-for-the-rest-of-your-life]] cites a large long-tenure pay penalty from secondary reporting, while providing no method sufficient to infer causation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a brief 2016 advice article built from expert opinion and secondary links, not longitudinal or experimental evidence. Workers who change jobs and workers who stay may differ in occupation, ambition, bargaining power, geography, performance, or opportunity, so a pay association does not prove a mobility effect. Switching also carries search costs, probation risk, lost benefits, visa or caregiving constraints, weaker institutional knowledge, and the possibility of leaving before a valuable project or relationship compounds. Some roles reward longer horizons, while repeated short stays may still concern employers in particular labor markets.
