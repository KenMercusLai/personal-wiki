## Definition
[[AggregatorMonopolyPower]] is the ability of a demand-controlling, multi-sided platform to capture surplus or weaken competition across suppliers, advertisers, and innovators even when its user-facing service is free and adding another user costs almost nothing.

## Current Synthesis
Conventional monopoly analysis looks for higher prices, restricted output, and consumer deadweight loss. Thompson's Facebook case argues that those signals can be weak on the user side of a free digital service while power appears elsewhere. A dominant aggregator can offer users abundant access, give publishers less revenue than a competitive distributor would, constrain advertising inventory where its ads are differentiated, copy an entrant's product into a stronger network, and use consumer telemetry to observe emerging rivals before public signals. The useful test is therefore side-specific: identify whose participation creates value, who controls access to demand and information, how surplus is divided, whether participants have practical substitutes, and whether the incumbent can appropriate innovation without bearing the entrant's discovery risk.

## Key Claims
- A zero monetary price does not rule out monopoly power in a multi-sided platform.
- Near-zero marginal user-serving cost can eliminate the usual user-market quantity restriction while leaving supplier, advertiser, or innovation harms.
- Demand control weakens supplier bargaining power when suppliers cannot afford to leave even after monetization disappoints.
- Restricting differentiated advertising inventory can increase profit rather than revenue, but price response is necessary evidence.
- Copying a challenger's product into an incumbent network can shift competition from innovation quality to installed-network strength.
- Monopoly-funded research does not guarantee shipped innovation, and eventual displacement does not erase rents or foreclosed experimentation during dominance.
- Privileged visibility into rival usage can shorten an incumbent's response time and strengthen copying or acquisition options.

## Evidence
- Multi-sided power: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] separates Facebook's users, content providers, and advertisers rather than treating social networking as a single market.
- Publisher surplus: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] argues that Instant Articles improved consumption while Facebook lacked competitive pressure to share more monetization with publishers.
- Conditional ad scarcity: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] says an ad-load cap would support a market-power interpretation if differentiated demand caused prices to rise.
- Innovation appropriation: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] uses Facebook's adoption of Stories and Snap's camera-first vision to argue that network leverage can capture more return than originating the innovation.
- Dynamic qualification: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] contrasts the incentive to create a new monopoly with the conduct of an established one and disputes that eventual incumbent replacement resolves interim costs.
- Information advantage: [[facebook-knew-about-snaps-struggles-months-before-the-public-engadget]] reports that Onavo telemetry let Facebook see Snapchat's slowdown and WhatsApp's strength before those signals were public.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources supply economic and journalistic interpretations, not a complete antitrust analysis. They do not rigorously define relevant markets, estimate publisher counterfactual revenue, show the price response to the planned ad-load cap, measure innovation that did not occur, or separate copying from ordinary product competition and consumer benefit. The Onavo report adds no raw telemetry, representativeness analysis, or proof that the intelligence caused Facebook's decisions. Network effects can improve matching and user experience, copied features can broaden access, and disclosed market research can be legitimate. The claims are historical to Facebook's 2017 product and market position.

## What Changed
- Created a side-specific account of platform monopoly costs that do not require a user-facing price increase.
- Connected publisher surplus, differentiated ad scarcity, network-leveraged copying, and innovation incentives in one framework.
- Preserved the unresolved empirical tests and the distinction between new-monopoly incentives and established-monopoly conduct.
- Added early rival visibility as a mechanism that may reinforce network-leveraged response capacity.
