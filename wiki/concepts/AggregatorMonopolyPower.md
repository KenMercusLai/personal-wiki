## Definition
[[AggregatorMonopolyPower]] is the ability of a demand-controlling, multi-sided platform to capture surplus or weaken competition across suppliers, advertisers, and innovators even when its user-facing service is free and adding another user costs almost nothing.

## Current Synthesis
Conventional monopoly analysis looks for higher prices, restricted output, and consumer deadweight loss. The Facebook case argues that those signals can be weak on the user side of a free digital service while power appears elsewhere. A dominant aggregator can give suppliers less revenue than a competitive distributor would, constrain differentiated advertising inventory, copy an entrant into a stronger network, or use consumer telemetry to observe rivals early.

The Grubhub case adds control of attribution and branded demand. Restaurant-linked domains, generic microsites, forwarding numbers, marketplace rank, and commission rules can make a platform appear to originate a transaction even when a customer intended to reach a known supplier directly. The side-specific test should therefore ask not only who controls aggregate demand, but who owns the supplier's identity surface, classifies the source of a customer, audits errors, and captures the resulting fee. This mechanism can coexist with real matching or delivery value, and harm depends on contracts, substitutes, direct-order capability, and whether the platform truly generated incremental demand.

## Key Claims
- A zero monetary price does not rule out monopoly power in a multi-sided platform.
- Near-zero marginal user-serving cost can eliminate the usual user-market quantity restriction while leaving supplier, advertiser, or innovation harms.
- Demand control weakens supplier bargaining power when suppliers cannot afford to leave, and control of branded discovery or attribution can convert intended direct demand into commission-bearing platform demand.
- Restricting differentiated advertising inventory can increase profit rather than revenue, but price response is necessary evidence.
- Copying a challenger's product into an incumbent network can shift competition from innovation quality to installed-network strength.
- Monopoly-funded research does not guarantee shipped innovation, and eventual displacement does not erase rents or foreclosed experimentation during dominance.
- Privileged visibility into rival usage can shorten an incumbent's response time and strengthen copying or acquisition options.

## Evidence
- Multi-sided power: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] separates Facebook's users, content providers, and advertisers rather than treating social networking as a single market.
- Publisher surplus: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] argues that Instant Articles improved consumption while Facebook lacked competitive pressure to share more monetization with publishers.
- Conditional ad scarcity: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] says an ad-load cap would support a market-power interpretation if differentiated demand caused prices to rise.
- Innovation appropriation: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] uses Facebook's adoption of Stories and Snap's camera-first vision to argue that network leverage can capture more return than originating the innovation.
- Dynamic qualification: [[facebook-and-the-cost-of-monopoly-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] contrasts the incentive to create a new monopoly with the conduct of an established one and disputes that eventual incumbent replacement resolves interim costs.
- Information advantage: [[facebook-knew-about-snaps-struggles-months-before-the-public-engadget]] reports that Onavo telemetry let Facebook see Snapchat's slowdown and WhatsApp's strength before those signals were public.
- Supplier identity and attribution: [[grubhub-is-buying-up-thousands-of-restaurant-web-addresses-that-means-mom-and-pop-cant-own-their-slice-of-the-internet-new-food-economy]] reports restaurant-linked domains, microsites, forwarding numbers, ranked visibility, and disputed phone-order classifications that could place Grubhub between a restaurant and customers already seeking it.
- Merchant economics and countercase: [[grubhub-is-buying-up-thousands-of-restaurant-web-addresses-that-means-mom-and-pop-cant-own-their-slice-of-the-internet-new-food-economy]] reports an $8,000 invoice, a restaurant's claim that direct lunch orders retained about 30% more revenue, Grubhub's contractual defense, and another owner's view that added web presence could be useful.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources supply economic and journalistic interpretations, not complete antitrust analyses. They do not rigorously define relevant markets, estimate publisher counterfactual revenue, show the price response to the planned ad-load cap, measure innovation that did not occur, or separate copying from ordinary product competition and consumer benefit. The Onavo report adds no raw telemetry, representativeness analysis, or proof that the intelligence caused Facebook's decisions. The Grubhub article supplies selected merchant cases rather than a representative welfare study; owners and Grubhub dispute authorization, some domains may have expired, and merchants that need delivery may value the platform more than those with established direct ordering. Network effects and aggregation can improve matching, convenience, and supplier reach. The Facebook mechanisms are historical to 2017, and the Grubhub evidence to 2019.

## What Changed
- Created a side-specific account of platform monopoly costs that do not require a user-facing price increase.
- Connected publisher surplus, differentiated ad scarcity, network-leveraged copying, and innovation incentives in one framework.
- Preserved the unresolved empirical tests and the distinction between new-monopoly incentives and established-monopoly conduct.
- Added early rival visibility as a mechanism that may reinforce network-leveraged response capacity.
- Added supplier-identity and attribution control as mechanisms that can convert direct demand into platform-fee demand, while preserving contractual disagreement and possible merchant value.
