## Definition
[[StartupJobDiligence]] is the practice of evaluating and continuing to verify an early-stage company's viability, commercial logic, employment operations, organizational conditions, and fit with a worker's priorities rather than treating the decision as a cultural preference or equity lottery ticket.

## Current Synthesis
The sources combine company, business, employment, organizational, and personal-fit diligence. Candidates should test runway, burn, investor provenance, governance, founder and early-employee quality, shipped products, sales, customer evidence, hiring intent, internal transparency, payroll reliability, and their own belief in the product. For ML/AI companies, technical sophistication is not enough: candidates should ask why the problem matters, how prior solutions compare, what founders learned from users, how the current product makes money and grows, how the market estimate is derived, and why the business remains defensible after an algorithm can be copied.

The decision still depends on fit. Candidates should weigh autonomy, accelerated responsibility, founder networks, and learning against weaker management, uncertain progression, lower stability, work-life costs, and equity that may never create wealth. Behavioral evidence is available during recruiting: guaranteed-riches claims, missed commitments followed by blame, contempt for coworkers, product complacency, competitor hatred, rushed relocation, intrusive pre-start work, self-promotion in place of product answers, and unclear reporting lines show how leaders handle uncertainty, boundaries, mistakes, and differentiation. [[PaulBuchheit]] adds a direct learning-investment test: ask whether the people, pace, and above-level responsibility are likely to improve the candidate faster than an established employer would.

Diligence must continue after joining because a polished interview cannot prove that funding, payroll, contracts, or governance work as represented. A late or irregular paycheck, absent pay stub, missing contractual payment, founder borrowing from employees, daily but shifting payment explanations, or evidence of falsified records should trigger verification and reduced exposure rather than another round of optimism. The first-person scam account adds a defensive layer: preserve contracts, payment promises, work records, and contemporaneous communications, protect personal runway, and research authoritative remedies. These actions do not establish fraud or guarantee recovery, but they make a worker less dependent on management's narrative when ordinary startup risk may have crossed into employment misconduct.

Koutnik adds a reusable evidence-gathering method that applies beyond startups. Broad questions invite safe abstractions, so candidates should ask for recent examples and probe how the role actually spends time, what the company expects the hire to change, how engineering and deployment work, how disagreement is resolved, how major announcements travel, how performance gaps become visible, and how employees develop. Answers are signals rather than proof: interviewers can select favorable stories, teams vary, and candidates do not always have enough leverage to ask sensitive questions without cost.

## Key Claims
- Startup jobs are multi-year career commitments whose company risk, business logic, employment operations, and personal fit should be investigated before joining and monitored afterward.
- Runway, burn, investor quality, founders, board structure, early employees, shipped products, sales, defined milestones, and reliable payroll provide stronger evidence than fundraising headlines, prestige claims, awards, press, or vanity traffic.
- A credible technology startup should explain a necessary customer problem, material improvement over prior practice, deep user learning, present revenue, a viable acquisition path, derived market size, and durable advantage.
- Candidates should not treat equity as dependable wealth; upside must be weighed against failure, liquidity uncertainty, forgone stability, and work-life opportunity cost.
- Hiring intent, reporting lines, employee access to business information, respect for boundaries, and observed treatment of mistakes, dissent, and colleagues reveal whether organizational conditions support long-term execution.
- Broken compensation commitments, irregular payroll, founder borrowing from workers, changing payment stories, or falsified records are escalation signals, not normal startup ambiguity.
- Specific questions, recent examples, and follow-up probes make role scope, operating practice, decision authority, communication, feedback, growth, and promised learning returns more inspectable than broad culture assurances; documentary evidence and personal runway remain necessary when those returns fail to appear.

## Evidence
- Commitment and assessability: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] says an early-stage role can consume prime career years and argues that candidates can improve their odds through diligence.
- Financial survival and governance: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] recommends examining runway, investors, founders, board members, early employees, revenue, paid customers, and defined active use; [[avoiding-zombie-startups]] adds backer incentives, track record, product launch, building, and selling.
- Product and market logic: [[business-questions-engineers-should-ask-when-interviewing-at-ml-ai-companies]] tests need, the pre-AI alternative, order-of-magnitude improvement, user-learning depth, current monetization, acquisition economics, market derivation, and defensibility beyond an algorithm.
- Organizational conditions: [[avoiding-zombie-startups]] recommends checking whether the broader team has meaningful equity, whether a hire delegates understood work or seeks a rescuer, and whether current employees can see the state of the business.
- Management and stability fit: [[why-you-should-and-shouldnt-join-a-startup-atrium]] contrasts weak startup structure and uncertain five-year survival with established-company onboarding, mentorship, goal setting, and career paths.
- Equity qualification: [[why-you-should-and-shouldnt-join-a-startup-atrium]] says becoming rich by joining a startup is statistically improbable and should not be the main reason to accept the chaos.
- Recruiting claims: [[evaluating-delusional-startups]] treats confident promises that a new hire's stock will create wealth as a warning rather than decision-grade compensation evidence.
- Cultural observation: [[evaluating-delusional-startups]] uses public blame, missed commitments, complaints about leadership, contempt for coworkers, product complacency, and unqualified hatred of competitors as interview-stage signals.
- Responsibility and learning: [[why-you-should-and-shouldnt-join-a-startup-atrium]] uses [[JustinTV]], Socialcam, Triplebyte, Shogun, Exec, and Cruise to show how early responsibility and even failed ventures can build technical, operating, network, and negotiation capability.
- Learning-investment test: [[gmail-creator-and-yc-partner-paul-buchheit-on-joining-google-how-to-become-a-great-engineer-and-happiness-triplebyte-blog]] says startup candidates should optimize for learning and accept the trade only when capable people and above-level responsibility create faster development.
- Interview and product signals: [[gmail-creator-and-yc-partner-paul-buchheit-on-joining-google-how-to-become-a-great-engineer-and-happiness-triplebyte-blog]] recommends hard questions, direct and insightful founder answers, a usable product, technically meaningful interviews, and observation of workplace energy while warning against hype without a product.
- Recruiting and operating warnings: [[i-got-scammed-by-a-silicon-valley-startup-startup-grind-medium]] reports rushed travel and relocation, pre-start boundary tests, founder biography-selling, unclear role authority, weak differentiation, unsupported budget claims, and investor dependence hidden behind claims of committed capital.
- Payroll escalation: [[i-got-scammed-by-a-silicon-valley-startup-startup-grind-medium]] describes cashier's checks, missing pay stubs and bonuses, prolonged unpaid wages, founder borrowing from employees, shifting transfer stories, and alleged forged confirmations as evidence that normal financing risk had become an employment-protection problem.
- Evidence preservation: [[i-got-scammed-by-a-silicon-valley-startup-startup-grind-medium]] says the author retained contracts, emails, payment promises, task-board records, work communications, and transfer documents before pursuing wage and retaliation complaints.
- Interview evidence method: [[questions-to-ask-your-interviewer]] recommends replacing broad trust or culture prompts with specific questions and recent examples that are harder to answer through generic reassurance alone.
- Role and change expectations: [[questions-to-ask-your-interviewer]] asks what the employer hopes the candidate will change and how often employees leave their primary task, exposing hidden scope and unsupported culture-repair expectations.
- Operating and cultural practice: [[questions-to-ask-your-interviewer]] tests development, deployment, communication, disagreement, success criteria, performance feedback, and professional development through concrete operational questions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
All eight sources are practitioner advice or first-person narrative rather than predictive studies, and none assigns validated weights to the signals or shows that strong interview answers predict outcomes. Holman's examples are a small set of anonymized interviews and reported friend experiences; Buchheit's account is retrospective and draws heavily on an exceptional early Google experience. The scam account is retrospective, anonymizes the company and people, and includes legal claims tied to the author's understanding of California practice in 2016; it supports concrete warning and documentation categories but not their prevalence, predictive value, or current legal interpretation. Its seven evidentiary screenshots survive only as 60-pixel-wide thumbnails, so their fine details cannot be independently read from the local source. Candid criticism may reveal dysfunction, but isolated disagreement or product dissatisfaction is not proof of a toxic culture. Workplace energy, technically interesting questions, and founder directness are informative but subjective and can be staged during recruiting. The ten-times-better-or-cheaper bar is a useful challenge but not a universal cutoff; augmentation, access, consistency, risk reduction, regulation, and high-stakes human service can make smaller measured improvements valuable. Paid acquisition is not automatically superior to word of mouth, and network effects are not the only form of defensibility.

Weak-looking answers can also reflect a very early stage, a capital-light model, patient research, regulation, or a founder still learning to communicate. Pastor's suspicion of public capital, inexperienced investors, awards, and publicity identifies weak proxies but does not show that those features cause stagnation. Kan's examples are memorable outliers selected from his network, and his claim that startup management generally performs poorly is not a comparative survey. The framework best fits prospective core employees in venture-style startups and applies less directly to later-stage firms, deliberately small businesses, nonprofits, studios, or independent products.

Koutnik's questions likewise have no validated predictive weights or outcome study behind them. A concrete anecdote can still be selected or rehearsed, one interviewer's experience may not represent another team, and a candidate's safety in asking about firings, management, or disagreement varies with power and context. Asynchronous communication, formal review, automated deployment, and rollback are useful signals rather than universal requirements in one prescribed form.

## What Changed
- Extended diligence from the offer decision into continued verification after joining.
- Added payroll reliability, founder borrowing, shifting payment stories, and alleged falsified records as escalation signals.
- Added boundary-testing, role-authority ambiguity, rushed relocation, and biography-led pitching as recruiting evidence.
- Added documentary evidence and personal runway as protections when management claims become unreliable.
- Added specific questions, recent examples, and follow-up probes as an interview-stage evidence method.
