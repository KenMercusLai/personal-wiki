## Definition
[[StartupJobDiligence]] is the practice of evaluating an early-stage startup before joining it by testing company viability, governance, team quality, milestone quality, and personal conviction rather than treating the job as a purely cultural or lottery-ticket choice.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that startup candidates should behave more like informed investors than passive recipients of employer branding. The proposed diligence is compact but broad: ask how much cash remains, how fast it is being spent, who invested, why those investors were chosen, who the founders and board members are, whether early employees look exceptional, what business milestones have been reached, and whether the idea is persuasive enough to deserve years of work. This shifts startup career choice from "join a team you like and hope" toward an evidence-based assessment of risk, upside, and fit.

## Key Claims
- Startup jobs should be evaluated as multi-year career commitments with assessable company risk.
- Runway questions need cash balance, burn rate, and projected months remaining, not fundraising headline alone.
- Investor, founder, and board quality are signals about judgment, governance, and future company support.
- Early employee quality reveals whether urgency has already lowered the hiring bar.
- Business milestones should privilege revenue, paid customers, and clearly defined active use over press or vanity traffic.
- Personal belief in the idea matters because early teams need conviction and because founder persuasiveness predicts customer-facing difficulty.

## Evidence
- Career-risk frame: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] says joining an early-stage startup can consume several prime career years, so candidates owe themselves diligence before committing.
- Assessability premise: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] argues startups are a game of skill, not pure chance, and cites venture-fund return dispersion as evidence that startup potential can be differentiated.
- Runway diligence: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] contrasts a startup burning $50,000 per month with one burning $300,000 per month to show that equal bank balances can mean roughly 40 months versus under 7 months of runway.
- Funding threshold evidence: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] includes a TechCrunch chart indicating materially higher Series A odds once seed funding reaches roughly $1.5M, while warning that money raised is not the same as success.
- Governance and people signals: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] recommends asking about investor choice, founder qualifications, board structure, and early employee backgrounds.
- Milestone quality: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] treats recurring revenue, paid customers, and defined active users as stronger evidence than TechCrunch coverage or website visits.
- Vision conviction: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] says candidates should pass if the idea still does not make sense after founders explain the broader vision.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is practical career advice, not a predictive model, and it does not quantify how much weight to assign each diligence signal. Strong answers do not guarantee startup success, while weak-looking answers may reflect an unusually early company, a capital-light strategy, a technical founder still learning to communicate, or a market that requires non-obvious evidence. The advice is most applicable to prospective core early employees considering venture-style startups, less so to lifestyle businesses, later-stage companies, nonprofits, studios, or independent products where funding, boards, and equity upside play different roles.
