## Definition
[[StartupJobDiligence]] is the practice of evaluating both an early-stage company's prospects and a candidate's fit for startup conditions before accepting a role, rather than treating the decision as a cultural preference or equity lottery ticket.

## Current Synthesis
The sources combine company diligence, organizational diligence, and personal-fit diligence. Candidates should test runway, burn, investor provenance, governance, founder and early-employee quality, shipped products, customer evidence, team incentives, hiring intent, internal transparency, and their own belief in the product. They should also decide whether autonomy, accelerated responsibility, founder networks, and learning justify weaker management, uncertain progression, lower stability, work-life costs, and equity that may never create wealth. The result is not a universal case for or against startups. It is a decision about whether a particular company has credible momentum and whether its risk-and-learning profile matches the candidate's current priorities.

## Key Claims
- Startup jobs are multi-year career commitments whose company risk and personal fit can be investigated before joining.
- Runway, burn, investor quality, founders, board structure, early employees, shipped products, sales, and business milestones provide stronger evidence than fundraising headlines, awards, press, or vanity traffic.
- Candidates should not treat equity as dependable wealth; upside must be weighed against failure, liquidity uncertainty, forgone stability, and work-life opportunity cost.
- Team-wide equity incentives, hiring intent, and employee access to business information reveal whether organizational conditions support long-term execution.
- Early startups often provide less onboarding, management training, mentorship, career structure, and directional clarity, which makes self-direction and active feedback-seeking important fit criteria.
- Startups can compensate for uncertainty by giving inexperienced employees unusually consequential work, fast feedback, and access to founder networks that may expand future career options.

## Evidence
- Commitment and assessability: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] says an early-stage role can consume prime career years and argues that candidates can improve their odds through diligence.
- Financial survival: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] shows that the same cash balance can imply radically different survival windows when burn differs.
- Governance, capital, and milestones: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] recommends examining investors, founders, board members, early employees, revenue, paid customers, and defined active use; [[avoiding-zombie-startups]] adds backer incentives, track record, product launch, building, and selling.
- Organizational conditions: [[avoiding-zombie-startups]] recommends checking whether the broader team has meaningful equity, whether a hire delegates work the founders understand or seeks a rescuer, and whether current employees can see the state of the business.
- Management and stability fit: [[why-you-should-and-shouldnt-join-a-startup-atrium]] contrasts weak startup structure and uncertain five-year survival with established-company onboarding, mentorship, goal setting, and career paths.
- Equity qualification: [[why-you-should-and-shouldnt-join-a-startup-atrium]] says becoming rich by joining a startup is statistically improbable and should not be the main reason to accept the chaos.
- Responsibility and learning: [[why-you-should-and-shouldnt-join-a-startup-atrium]] uses [[JustinTV]], Socialcam, Triplebyte, Shogun, Exec, and Cruise to show how early responsibility and even failed ventures can build technical, operating, network, and negotiation capability.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
All three sources are practitioner advice rather than predictive studies, and none assigns validated weights to the signals. Strong diligence answers do not guarantee success; weak-looking answers can reflect a very early stage, a capital-light model, patient research, regulation, or a founder still learning to communicate. Pastor's suspicion of public capital, inexperienced investors, awards, and publicity can identify weak proxies but does not show that those features cause stagnation. Kan's examples are memorable outliers selected from his network, and his claim that startup management generally performs poorly is not a comparative survey. The framework best fits prospective core employees in venture-style startups and applies less directly to later-stage firms, deliberately small businesses, nonprofits, studios, or independent products.

## What Changed
- Added investor provenance, product and sales evidence, team-wide equity incentives, hiring intent, and internal transparency as company-level diligence signals.
- Distinguished a healthy small company from a venture-style company whose prestige signals conceal weak momentum.
- Added employee interviews as a way to test founder transparency and reduce the risk of staying after the company's prospects deteriorate.
