## Definition
[[StartupJobDiligence]] is the practice of evaluating both an early-stage company's prospects and a candidate's fit for startup conditions before accepting a role, rather than treating the decision as a cultural preference or equity lottery ticket.

## Current Synthesis
The sources combine company diligence with personal-fit diligence. Candidates should test runway, burn, investors, governance, founder quality, early employees, milestones, and their own belief in the product; they should also decide whether autonomy, accelerated responsibility, founder networks, and learning justify weaker management, uncertain progression, lower stability, work-life costs, and equity that may never create wealth. The result is not a universal case for or against startups. It is a decision about whether a particular company is credible and whether its risk-and-learning profile matches the candidate's current priorities.

## Key Claims
- Startup jobs are multi-year career commitments whose company risk and personal fit can be investigated before joining.
- Runway, burn, investors, founders, board structure, early employee quality, and business milestones provide stronger company evidence than fundraising headlines, press, or vanity traffic.
- Candidates should not treat equity as dependable wealth; upside must be weighed against failure, liquidity uncertainty, forgone stability, and work-life opportunity cost.
- Early startups often provide less onboarding, management training, mentorship, career structure, and directional clarity, which makes self-direction and active feedback-seeking important fit criteria.
- Startups can compensate for uncertainty by giving inexperienced employees unusually consequential work, fast feedback, and access to founder networks that may expand future career options.
- Learning rate is a strong reason to join only when it matters more to the candidate than predictability, structured development, and stability.

## Evidence
- Commitment and assessability: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] says an early-stage role can consume prime career years and argues that candidates can improve their odds through diligence.
- Financial survival: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] shows that the same cash balance can imply radically different survival windows when burn differs.
- Governance and milestones: [[7-questions-to-ask-founders-before-joining-their-startup]] recommends examining investors, founders, board members, early employees, revenue, paid customers, and defined active use.
- Management and stability fit: [[why-you-should-and-shouldnt-join-a-startup-atrium]] contrasts weak startup structure and uncertain five-year survival with established-company onboarding, mentorship, goal setting, and career paths.
- Equity qualification: [[why-you-should-and-shouldnt-join-a-startup-atrium]] says becoming rich by joining a startup is statistically improbable and should not be the main reason to accept the chaos.
- Responsibility and learning: [[why-you-should-and-shouldnt-join-a-startup-atrium]] uses [[JustinTV]], Socialcam, Triplebyte, Shogun, Exec, and Cruise to show how early responsibility and even failed ventures can build technical, operating, network, and negotiation capability.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are practitioner advice rather than predictive studies, and neither assigns validated weights to the signals. Strong diligence answers do not guarantee success; weak-looking answers can reflect a very early stage, a capital-light model, or a founder still learning to communicate. Kan's examples are memorable outliers selected from his network, and his claim that startup management generally performs poorly is not a comparative survey. The framework best fits prospective core employees in venture-style startups and applies less directly to later-stage firms, lifestyle businesses, nonprofits, studios, or independent products.

## What Changed
- Expanded diligence from company viability to candidate fit across management structure, stability, equity risk, autonomy, networks, and learning rate.
- Added high-responsibility learning and founder-network access as real but non-guaranteed startup career benefits.
- Added an explicit warning that improbable employee wealth is a weak reason to accept startup risk.
