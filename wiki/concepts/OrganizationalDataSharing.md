## Definition
[[OrganizationalDataSharing]] is the practice of making observations and datasets from different teams or systems available together so a company can form better hypotheses, analyses, and decisions.

## Current Synthesis
The sources frame organizational data sharing as the business version of innovation at intersections. Each team holds partial knowledge: customer success hears pain points, sales hears objections, marketing creates proof assets, finance understands cash implications, and product or analytics teams may see behavioral data. When those fragments remain isolated, hypotheses are weaker; when they are connected, a company can reason across the whole customer journey.

The article also treats tooling as part of the problem. As company data splinters across databases and SaaS tools, each dataset becomes like a specialist with narrow expertise. The cited combination of [[TwilioSegment]] Sources and [[Looker]] Blocks is valuable because it can join funnel evidence across marketing campaign, landing page, sales contract, customer success interaction, lifecycle marketing, and upsell stages.

## Key Claims
- Different teams hold different customer and business observations.
- Sharing observations can improve the hypotheses teams choose to test.
- Data fragmentation increases as organizations adopt more databases and data sources.
- Cross-system analytics can expose end-to-end funnels that single tools cannot show alone.
- Data sharing is valuable only when it supports decisions across business functions.

- Hypothesis support: [[all-new-ideas-are-combinations-of-old-ideas]] says businesses innovate at intersections by sharing observations and data to support hypotheses.
- Data fragmentation: [[all-new-ideas-are-combinations-of-old-ideas]] says organizational data is splintering as new databases and data sources appear.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source emphasizes the upside of shared data but does not address governance, privacy, data quality, metric ownership, semantic consistency, access control, or the risk that more data can create false confidence. The tooling example is from 2016 and should be treated as a period-specific startup data-stack illustration rather than a current vendor recommendation.
