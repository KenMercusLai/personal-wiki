## Definition
[[MimeticDesire]] is desire borrowed by imitating what others appear to want, rather than an aim discovered through self-honesty and direct contact with one's own values.

## Current Synthesis
The source uses mimetic desire as a warning inside its broader AI action argument. It says people can mistake imitation, hesitation, or half-commitment for genuine motivation, especially when founder stories and success formulas are everywhere. In the article's view, the antidote is not more motivational content but a sharper internal question: what does this person actually want badly enough to act on?

## Key Claims
- Borrowed desire can masquerade as ambition.
- Success stories and methods can intensify imitation when they are consumed passively.
- Self-honesty is needed before action becomes meaningful rather than performative.
- The desire worth acting on should create concrete urgency and willingness to pay costs.

## Evidence
- False motivation: [[blog-maiyang-naval-insights-taking-action-with-ai]] warns against hesitation and desire produced by imitating others.
- Passive consumption: [[blog-maiyang-naval-insights-taking-action-with-ai]] frames success-method consumption as pleasurable but non-creative.
- Self-questioning: [[blog-maiyang-naval-insights-taking-action-with-ai]] uses Bezos's pre-Amazon decision as an example of asking what he truly wanted at that moment.
- Urgency test: [[blog-maiyang-naval-insights-taking-action-with-ai]] asks whether the desire keeps a person awake.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source uses mimetic desire as a concise motivational category and does not develop a full theory of imitation. Imitation can also be useful in learning, apprenticeship, taste formation, and market discovery. The warning is strongest when borrowed desire substitutes for self-owned action.
