## Definition
[[ManagingUp]] is the employee practice of making needed context, feedback, priorities, follow-up, and relationship repairs explicit instead of waiting for a manager or organization to supply them automatically.

## Current Synthesis
[[CassieMarketos]] frames managing up as a learned response to an environment where she lacked clear goals and feedback but was initially too inexperienced to identify or request them. Her later practice replaces anxious interpretation with direct inquiry: remind a busy reviewer instead of assuming silence means rejection, address repeated tension candidly, state the need for frequent and honest feedback, and respond constructively when difficult feedback arrives. The aim is not to make the employee responsible for every management failure, but to create information and action where avoidable ambiguity would otherwise persist.

This practice depends on power and reciprocity. An employee can clarify needs and invite candor, while managers still own goal clarity, safe feedback, fair evaluation, and response to structural problems. Marketos's warning that young women may internalize workplace dysfunction also makes self-separation part of the practice: not every missed reply, sharp interaction, or organizational failure is evidence of personal inadequacy.

## Key Claims
- Employees can reduce avoidable ambiguity by asking directly for goals, feedback, decisions, and follow-up.
- Silence or a tense interaction should be checked against workload and context before being treated as a judgment of personal worth.
- Kind, specific, direct conversation can interrupt passive-aggressive conflict and surface changes both parties can make.
- Requesting candid feedback works best when the recipient can process criticism without treating it automatically as personal rejection.
- Managing up supplements but does not erase managerial responsibility or unequal workplace power.
- Separating self-worth from organizational dysfunction can make workplace problem-solving more direct and less fear-driven.

## Evidence
- Missing conditions: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] says unclear goals and absent feedback hindered Marketos while she lacked the experience to ask for what she needed.
- Follow-up: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] contrasts assuming an unanswered draft was bad with recognizing that busy colleagues may need a reminder.
- Conflict repair: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] recommends addressing sustained interpersonal tension directly and asking how the working relationship can improve.
- Feedback contract: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] says she now tells managers she needs consistent, highly candid feedback and reinforces that behavior by receiving it constructively.
- Structural boundary: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] argues that workplace failures, especially for young women, should not automatically be internalized as personal defects.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is one person's retrospective practice rather than measured comparative research. Directness may be punished in unsafe, discriminatory, retaliatory, or highly unequal workplaces, and workers differ in culture, communication style, disability, security, and access to alternatives. A manager cannot shift responsibility for vague goals, missing feedback, harassment, or systemic dysfunction onto employees by telling them to manage up. Difficult feedback also deserves evaluation; candor is not the same as accuracy, fairness, or usefulness.

## What Changed
- Created the concept from Marketos's account of turning missing feedback, anxious inference, and passive conflict into explicit requests and conversations.
- Preserved power, safety, discrimination, and managerial-accountability limits.
