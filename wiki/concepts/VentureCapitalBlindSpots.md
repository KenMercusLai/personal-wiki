## Definition
[[VentureCapitalBlindSpots]] are recurring cognitive and market-model errors that cause investors to overlook exceptional companies within areas they could reasonably have evaluated.

## Current Synthesis
The 645 Ventures framework starts from venture capital's asymmetric payoff structure: when a small number of companies produce most returns, passing on a rare breakout can be more damaging than backing several failures. Its seven blind spots span three layers. People errors arise from founder pattern matching, [[Homophily]], homogeneous partnerships, and network-shaped deal flow. Evidence errors arise when investors substitute personal taste, consensus, secondhand framing, or entry price for customer behavior, direct founder learning, and long-run outcome analysis. Market-model errors arise when they freeze today's category boundaries, assume competition has ended, or let earlier failures hide improving technology economics and changing preferences. [[JohnGreathouse]]'s Uber retrospective supplies a concrete interaction among these layers: a service-versus-software thesis filter, a few-city market assumption, concern about marketplace liquidity and regulation, and a later high-price judgment prevented direct investigation of a repeatedly referred company.

The proposed countermeasures are complementary rather than mechanical: develop a prepared market thesis while remaining open about founder form and business-model evolution; broaden the perspectives and networks involved in evaluation; observe real target customers and traction; distinguish taking a meeting from making an investment; ask how large the company could become if the thesis works; examine adjacent expansion paths; and revisit categories when adoption curves or enabling conditions change. These practices reduce avoidable blindness but do not turn contrarian investing into a formula or erase loss discipline.

## Key Claims
- Power-law returns make missed exceptional companies unusually costly mistakes of omission, while portfolio losses still justify a selective investment bar.
- Founder evaluation is vulnerable to pattern matching, similarity bias, and homogeneous networks or decision groups.
- Investor preferences and consensus are weak substitutes for direct evidence from the product's actual customers.
- Entry valuation should be judged jointly with maximum plausible outcome and success probability.
- Today's measured market can understate latent demand or a niche product's credible expansion path.
- Early incumbency and prior category failures do not prove that innovation, economics, or behavior has stopped changing.
- Reducing blind spots requires prepared theses, diverse perspectives, independent judgment, direct diligence, and observable customer evidence.

## Evidence
- Return asymmetry: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] cites historical fund data in which a small minority of deals generated most returns and uses that pattern to prioritize omission risk.
- Founder and customer judgment: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] uses Stitch Fix and Rent the Runway to show how male, affluent, or socially similar evaluators can mistake unfamiliarity for weak demand.
- Price and consensus: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] uses Facebook and Salesforce to argue that price anchoring and herd judgment can conceal large product-market upside.
- Market shape: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] distinguishes sleeper markets with underestimated demand from Trojan-horse markets that begin in a small niche and expand.
- Category change: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] uses Google and Tesla to illustrate differentiated entry into a supposedly won market and renewed opportunity after technical and behavioral change.
- Direct-diligence failure: [[confessions-of-a-vc-crap-why-i-passed-on-ubers-seed-round-john-greathouse]] says Greathouse never heard Uber's seed pitch despite repeated trusted referrals, relying instead on secondhand framing and a firm-thesis mismatch.
- Market-boundary error: [[confessions-of-a-vc-crap-why-i-passed-on-ubers-seed-round-john-greathouse]] says Uber's premium town-car wedge made the company look geographically and categorically narrow, obscuring a possible expansion into the taxi market.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are practitioner retrospectives built mainly around famous anti-portfolio cases selected after success. Those examples make the failure modes memorable but invite survivorship and hindsight bias: many unconventional founders, expensive rounds, contrarian products, small markets, crowded categories, and behavioral-change theses still fail. Greathouse explicitly preserves this boundary by arguing that capital-losing investments hurt fund outcomes more acutely than one missed winner. The sources do not provide a prospective classification rule, base-rate comparison, controlled performance evidence, or a method for balancing omission risk against ownership, dilution, portfolio capacity, meeting time, and false-positive costs. Their company metrics and market conditions are historical snapshots.

## What Changed
- Established a unified model linking VC omission risk to founder, evidence, and market-model errors.
- Distinguished sleeper-market underestimation from Trojan-horse expansion.
- Made direct customer observation, diverse judgment, and changing adoption curves explicit countermeasures.
- Added direct founder diligence and trusted repeated referrals as information-quality checks.
- Added the Uber case's thesis-fit, local-market, liquidity, regulation, and valuation interaction.
