## Definition
[[MensMarathonPerformance]] is the observed distribution and progression of elite men's marathon finishing times across athletes, countries, race venues, dates, ages, and official record-eligibility rules.

## Current Synthesis
The September 2025 source snapshot shows both broad improvement and strong concentration. Its 1,903 sub-2:08 results extend from sparse observations in the 1980s to a much denser field by the 2020s, yet Kenya and Ethiopia account for 74.8% of those performances and 89.2% of the 231 results at or below 2:05. A small set of fast courses also concentrates the strictest performances: Berlin, Valencia, London, Dubai, Rotterdam, Amsterdam, Chicago, and Tokyo account for 81% of the at-or-below-2:05 results.

Official records form a narrower sequence than raw fastest times because performance and eligibility are distinct. The record fell 13 times from 1985 through 2023, ending at [[KelvinKiptum]]'s 2:00:35, while Geoffrey Mutai's 2:03:02 at Boston did not qualify because the course is downhill and point-to-point. Repeatability also matters alongside a single peak: [[EliudKipchoge]] leads the source with 18 sub-2:08 and 14 at-or-below-2:05 performances.

Age is descriptive rather than determinative in this evidence. Eighty percent of listed sub-2:08 results occurred from ages 23 through 33 and the count peaks at 26, but the input includes only already-elite performances and does not separate age from cohort, course, conditions, training, selection, or technology.

## Key Claims
- Elite men's marathon results became denser and faster from 1985 through 2025 in the compiled snapshot.
- Kenyan and Ethiopian athletes dominate both analyzed thresholds, with greater concentration at 2:05 or faster.
- A small group of race cities accounts for most of the at-or-below-2:05 results.
- Official record progression depends on course eligibility, so the fastest observed performance is not automatically a world record.
- Repeat sub-threshold performances reveal sustained excellence that a single record alone does not capture.
- Most listed sub-2:08 results occur from ages 23 through 33, but this selected dataset cannot establish a causal “best age.”

## Evidence
- Time and nationality concentration: [[marathon-world-record-evolution-2025]] plots 1,903 sub-2:08 results and reports Kenya at 44.8% and Ethiopia at 30.0%.
- Stricter threshold and venues: [[marathon-world-record-evolution-2025]] counts 231 results at or below 2:05, attributes 89.2% to Kenya and Ethiopia, and reports eight cities accounting for 81%.
- Official progression: [[marathon-world-record-evolution-2025]] charts 13 record improvements from Carlos Lopes's 2:07:12 to Kiptum's 2:00:35.
- Eligibility boundary: [[marathon-world-record-evolution-2025]] distinguishes Mutai's non-qualifying Boston 2:03:02 from the official record sequence.
- Athlete depth: [[marathon-world-record-evolution-2025]] counts Kipchoge as the most frequent athlete below both analyzed thresholds.
- Age distribution: [[marathon-world-record-evolution-2025]] reports that 80% of sub-2:08 results occurred from ages 23 through 33 and shows a count peak at age 26.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The synthesis rests on one author's calculations from Peter Larsson's results table and is not independently reproduced here. The source covers men's performances only, uses threshold-selected observations rather than all marathon starts, and does not state a statistical model or uncertainty. Country, city, and age patterns are associations: they do not isolate athlete opportunity, field selection, prize incentives, pacing, weather, course geometry, altitude, footwear, training, anti-doping enforcement, or changing participation. Counts can also change as results are corrected or new races are run.

## What Changed
- Created a threshold-based view of elite men's marathon depth, concentration, and record progression.
- Distinguished official record eligibility from raw finishing time.
- Framed the age distribution as descriptive rather than causal.
