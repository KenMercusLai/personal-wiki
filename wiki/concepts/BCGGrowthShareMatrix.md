## Definition
[[BCGGrowthShareMatrix]] is a portfolio model that classifies businesses by market growth and relative market share into stars, question marks, cash cows, and dogs, with implied differences in cash generation and investment need.

## Current Synthesis
The source emphasizes the matrix's capital-allocation logic. A mature market leader should generate more cash than it consumes, but harvesting that cash is not the final objective. Cash from today's leader should fund R&D and question marks: uncertain, cash-consuming products with the potential to become future stars and eventually new cash cows.

This interpretation turns the matrix into a succession mechanism across [[TechnologySCurve|technology S-curves]]. It also exposes a tension the original stable-market framing can understate: when product cycles shorten and software creation and distribution become cheaper, a cash cow may lose relevance before portfolio labels or reported financials register the transition.

## Key Claims
- Relative market share and market growth proxy for a business's cash generation and consumption.
- Cash cows should be harvested, but their cash should finance future options rather than only current earnings.
- Question marks are costly and uncertain yet are the portfolio's route to future stars.
- Portfolio renewal requires several bets because most new products never achieve sustained growth.
- Faster technology cycles make static portfolio classifications less reliable.

## Evidence
- Matrix structure: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] describes four quadrants organized by relative market share and market growth.
- Reinvestment rule: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] argues that cash generated by mature leaders should be routed to R&D and question marks.
- Successor logic: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] presents question marks as the only internal route to future stars and cash cows.
- Visual evidence: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] includes a four-quadrant matrix with stars, question marks, cash cows, and dogs.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not measure market growth, relative share, capital requirements, or returns from each quadrant, and it acknowledges that the model assumes more stable markets than many technology firms now face. Labels can conceal synergies, strategic complements, nonlinear market changes, and businesses that remain valuable without high growth or dominant share. Funding question marks increases option value but does not identify which experiments deserve continued investment.

## What Changed
- Created the matrix as a dynamic reinvestment model rather than only a four-box classification.
- Qualified static portfolio labels for shorter, steeper technology cycles.
