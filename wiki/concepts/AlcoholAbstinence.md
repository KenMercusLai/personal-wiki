## Definition
[[AlcoholAbstinence]] is the deliberate choice to avoid alcohol, framed in this source as a way to protect cognitive clarity, bodily control, and long-term agency.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents abstinence as an extension of attention and self-control rather than only a health preference. The author's position is that alcohol is a sedating substance that can impair cognition and self-command, so refusing it is consistent with protecting the brain as a scarce tool for success, happiness, and chosen action.

## Key Claims
- Alcohol avoidance is framed as rational when the brain is treated as a central life and work instrument.
- The source links alcohol to impaired cognition, memory, movement, sensation, and central nervous system activity.
- The author treats loss of subjective control as unacceptable unless medically or practically necessary.
- Social pressure to drink can conflict with personal agency, even when refusal disrupts ordinary banquet or drinking culture.

## Evidence
- Brain-as-tool rationale: [[zhi-you-zi-lv-cai-neng-kan-dao-zhen-zheng-de-zi-you-durov-podcast-sui-bi]] quotes Durov asking why one would damage the brain for short-term pleasure.
- Pharmacological framing: [[zhi-you-zi-lv-cai-neng-kan-dao-zhen-zheng-de-zi-you-durov-podcast-sui-bi]] summarizes alcohol as enhancing GABA inhibition and suppressing central nervous system activity.
- No-safe-dose concern: [[zhi-you-zi-lv-cai-neng-kan-dao-zhen-zheng-de-zi-you-durov-podcast-sui-bi]] cites material suggesting even commonly considered safe drinking levels may be associated with hippocampal and cognitive risk.
- Agency boundary: [[zhi-you-zi-lv-cai-neng-kan-dao-zhen-zheng-de-zi-you-durov-podcast-sui-bi]] records the author's long-standing refusal to let nonessential substances reduce conscious control.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This page records the source's argument; it does not independently review medical evidence on alcohol risk. The source's stance is strongly abstentionist and may not address social, cultural, or clinical contexts where alcohol use is evaluated differently.
