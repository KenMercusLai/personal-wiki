## Definition
[[ProductRetrospectives]] are team reflection sessions or post-mortems that convert recent product work into product, process, and collaboration learning.

## Current Synthesis
The sources present retrospectives as a recurring product-team practice for naming what went well, what should improve, and what lessons should travel into the next project. The rule-of-threes source prefers start, stop, and continue because that frame focuses the team on learning rather than incident detail. A neutral facilitator can help keep the session balanced, while individual sticky-note writing followed by grouping reveals common patterns and differences in how team members experienced the work. Building Products broadens the cadence and scope: teams should run post-mortems after every project, regardless of success or failure, and separate product lessons from team lessons before sharing relevant learning with the broader company. Ideally, the practice happens close enough to current evidence that process improvement stays specific rather than becoming folklore.

## Key Claims
- Product teams need structured opportunities to discuss both successes and process problems.
- A neutral facilitator can help retrospectives stay balanced.
- Start-stop-continue focuses reflection on actionable learning.
- Individual note-writing followed by grouping can reveal shared patterns and different interpretations.
- Retrospectives should recur every few weeks rather than only after major failure.
- Post-mortems should happen after successful projects as well as failed ones.
- Teams should distinguish product lessons from team-process lessons and share useful learning beyond the immediate group.

## Evidence
- Purpose: [[7-ways-to-use-the-rule-of-threes-to-build-great-products]] says retrospectives air what went well and what could improve during the quarter.
- Facilitation: [[7-ways-to-use-the-rule-of-threes-to-build-great-products]] recommends someone not directly involved with the team as a neutral facilitator.
- Framework: [[7-ways-to-use-the-rule-of-threes-to-build-great-products]] recommends start, stop, and continue because it keeps attention on learning.
- Pattern discovery: [[7-ways-to-use-the-rule-of-threes-to-build-great-products]] describes writing one thought per sticky note, grouping notes on a whiteboard, and comparing how people internalize the situation.
- Success and failure scope: [[building-products-the-year-of-the-looking-glass-medium]] recommends team post-mortems after every project, regardless of outcome.
- Learning categories: [[building-products-the-year-of-the-looking-glass-medium]] asks what product lessons, team lessons, and future changes emerged from the project.
- Organizational sharing: [[building-products-the-year-of-the-looking-glass-medium]] recommends sharing project learning with the whole company.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not deeply discuss psychological safety, facilitation skill, power dynamics, remote-team adaptation, or follow-through mechanisms. Without those, a retrospective can produce notes without changing behavior. Company-wide sharing can also strip context or create performative lessons unless teams preserve nuance about the project, evidence, and constraints.

## What Changed
- Added Building Products' post-mortem norm: reflect after both successful and failed projects, separate product and team lessons, and share durable learning.
