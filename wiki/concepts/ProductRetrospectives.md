## Definition
[[ProductRetrospectives]] are team reflection sessions or post-mortems that convert recent product work into product, process, and collaboration learning.

## Current Synthesis
The sources present retrospectives as a recurring product-team practice for converting recent experience into a small set of product, process, and collaboration changes. Start-stop-continue makes the decision structure explicit: add a useful behavior, remove a wasteful one, or keep reinforcing an improvement until it becomes habitual. Teams can first gather observations individually or through open call-outs, round-robin turns, or category-focused prompts, then group patterns and vote once idea generation slows. The Mountain Goat Software source recommends selecting no more than three priorities, pruning achieved or obsolete continue items, and carrying the prior session's full list forward only as optional context. Building Products broadens the scope beyond sprints: post-mortems should follow successful and failed projects, separate product lessons from team lessons, and share durable learning with the wider organization. The method is strongest when facilitation protects participation and psychological safety, action owners and review points create follow-through, and direct behavior framing does not suppress emotional or interpersonal evidence.

## Key Claims
- Product teams need structured reflection after routine iterations and after successful or failed projects.
- Start-stop-continue converts experience into behaviors to add, remove, or reinforce until habitual.
- Facilitation methods should vary while protecting balanced participation and surfacing different interpretations.
- Pattern grouping and voting can turn a broad observation set into a prioritized action set.
- Selecting no more than three changes protects focus, while continue items should leave the list once achieved or obsolete.
- Previous ideas can seed the next retrospective without dictating its agenda.
- Teams should separate product lessons from team-process lessons and share durable learning beyond the immediate group.

## Evidence
- Reflection scope: [[7-ways-to-use-the-rule-of-threes-to-build-great-products]] describes recurring reflection on what went well and what should improve; [[building-products-the-year-of-the-looking-glass-medium]] recommends post-mortems after every project regardless of outcome.
- Action framework: [[7-ways-to-use-the-rule-of-threes-to-build-great-products]] recommends start-stop-continue to focus on learning; [[a-simple-way-to-run-a-sprint-retrospective]] defines starts as additions, stops as waste removal, and continues as temporary reinforcement until a behavior becomes habitual.
- Participation and pattern discovery: [[7-ways-to-use-the-rule-of-threes-to-build-great-products]] combines neutral facilitation with individual notes and grouping; [[a-simple-way-to-run-a-sprint-retrospective]] varies open call-outs, round-robin turns, and category-focused prompts.
- Prioritization and maintenance: [[a-simple-way-to-run-a-sprint-retrospective]] recommends voting when ideation slows, choosing no more than three priorities, reviewing continue items for removal, and displaying the previous list as optional context at the next session.
- Learning categories and distribution: [[building-products-the-year-of-the-looking-glass-medium]] separates product lessons, team lessons, and future changes, then recommends sharing useful learning with the company.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
These are practitioner prescriptions rather than comparative evidence that start-stop-continue, neutral facilitation, voting, or a three-item cap improves team outcomes. Open call-outs and majority voting can privilege confident or senior voices, equate popularity with importance, and miss low-frequency but high-severity concerns. A deliberately action-oriented session may also suppress emotion, conflict, burnout, or power dynamics that are themselves material evidence; Mountain Goat Software acknowledges that attending to feelings first may sometimes be necessary. The sources do not specify remote or asynchronous adaptation, accessibility, anonymous input, action ownership, deadlines, outcome measures, or escalation paths. Without those, a retrospective can produce a prioritized list without behavior change. Carrying old ideas forward may help continuity but can also anchor discussion, while company-wide sharing can strip context or make lessons performative.

## What Changed
- Refined start-stop-continue into a lifecycle: propose changes, prioritize no more than three, reinforce selected behavior, and retire achieved or obsolete continue items.
- Added facilitation variants and the use of the previous session's full list as optional context.
- Qualified action orientation and voting with psychological-safety, participation-bias, severity, and follow-through risks.
