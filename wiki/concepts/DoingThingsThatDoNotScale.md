## Definition
[[DoingThingsThatDoNotScale]] is the startup practice of using manual, high-touch, or inefficient work to learn what users need and create intense early love before automating or expanding.

## Current Synthesis
The sources present unscalable work as a stage-specific path to learning, not automatically a path to scale. [[BrianChesky]] describes Airbnb founders visiting hosts, living with them, writing reviews, and helping with photos because making 100 people love the product was more valuable than chasing weak mass adoption. Nextdoor's manual verification, door-to-door work, and direct calls helped test and seed neighborhood engagement before automation. [[ClassPass]] manually made reservations to learn what motivated customers and which cancellation behavior should shape policy. [[ZeroCater]] used an inbox and spreadsheet to fulfill office meals until a 500-column schedule and roughly 20 weekly billing hours made the automation need concrete. FundersClub states the transition rule directly: give the first customer cohort personalized attention, then automate stable recurring portions without removing human listeners and escalation. Maderight supplies the failure-side qualification: manual service work can preserve learning and revenue but become a competing business that splits attention and delays a product-market-fit decision.

## Key Claims
- A small number of users who love a product can be more valuable than a large number who only mildly like it.
- Manual work with the first customer cohort exposes product, trust, and engagement details whose learning value can exceed short-term automation efficiency.
- High-touch workflows can later become technology-supported processes.
- Unscalable work is most useful before teams scale neighborhoods, markets, or headcount broadly.
- Manual operational work can be worth deeper investment when it exposes root problems that cheap tests cannot reach.
- Manual backend work can test the customer transaction before robust automation, while observed bottlenecks can identify what software should replace first.
- Manual service work becomes risky when it preserves revenue while pulling resources away from the product path.

## Evidence
- Airbnb host work: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] describes founders meeting hosts, living with them, writing reviews, and helping take photos.
- Customer-love threshold: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] cites the contrast between 100 loving customers and one million indifferent users.
- Nextdoor manual seeding: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] describes asking for licenses, HOA rosters, door-to-door outreach, phone calls to small neighborhoods, and babysitter-demand walks.
- Automation transition: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] says Nextdoor later systematized manual processes into technology.
- Root-problem access: [[4-lessons-from-a-failed-startup-from-and-for-first-time-founders]] says [[Maderight]] later believed operating a micro-factory deserved a deeper look because it could have put the team closer to apparel lead-time and quality problems.
- Service trap: [[4-lessons-from-a-failed-startup-from-and-for-first-time-founders]] says Maderight's high-touch service work produced customers but later competed with the technology product for time and resources.
- Transaction learning: [[classpass-founder-on-how-marketplace-startups-can-achieve-product-market-fit-first-round-review]] says Kadakia manually made reservations instead of building the perfect booking backend.
- Policy insight: [[classpass-founder-on-how-marketplace-startups-can-achieve-product-market-fit-first-round-review]] says manual reservation handling revealed that many cancellations happened within 15 minutes of booking.
- First-customer boundary: [[founders-guide-to-automation-fundersclub]] recommends personalized attention for early clients, then selective automation for stable acquisition, education, sales, billing, and delivery work.
- ZeroCater validation: [[from-selling-scoops-of-ice-cream-to-founding-zerocater-techcrunch]] says Sabeti served early customers through an inbox and spreadsheet before writing custom software.
- Automation trigger: [[from-selling-scoops-of-ice-cream-to-founding-zerocater-techcrunch]] reports that scheduling expanded to 500 spreadsheet columns and billing consumed about 20 hours per week before Sabeti recruited a technical cofounder.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Manual work can reveal demand, but it can also hide unit economics, compliance, safety, operational costs, supplier impacts, or strategy conflicts that emerge only at scale. ZeroCater's founder-reported customer and meal totals do not establish those economics or show which parts of the eventual software solved the observed bottlenecks. Conversely, FundersClub's automation examples are selected anecdotes and do not prove that a process is stable merely because it repeats. Together, the sources imply that unscalable work should be treated as a discovery method and bridge, with automation following evidence of stable patterns rather than a schedule or ideology.

## What Changed
- Added an explicit first-customer boundary and a monitored transition from manual learning to automation.
- Added ZeroCater's inbox-and-spreadsheet service and quantified scheduling and billing bottlenecks as an automation trigger.
