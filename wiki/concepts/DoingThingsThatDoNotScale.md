## Definition
[[DoingThingsThatDoNotScale]] is the startup practice of using manual, high-touch, or inefficient work to learn what users need and create intense early love before automating or expanding.

## Current Synthesis
The sources present unscalable work as a path to learning, but not automatically as a path to scale. [[BrianChesky]] describes Airbnb founders visiting hosts, living with them, writing early reviews, and helping with photos because making 100 people love the product was more valuable than chasing weak mass adoption. The Nextdoor example extends the point: manual verification, door-to-door work, and direct calls helped test and seed neighborhood engagement before automation. Maderight adds the failure-side qualification: manual service work can keep founders close to customers, but if it becomes a parallel business with different demands, it can split attention and delay a hard product-market-fit decision.

## Key Claims
- A small number of users who love a product can be more valuable than a large number who only mildly like it.
- Manual customer work exposes product, trust, and engagement details that broad metrics can hide.
- High-touch workflows can later become technology-supported processes.
- Unscalable work is most useful before teams scale neighborhoods, markets, or headcount broadly.
- Manual operational work can be worth deeper investment when it exposes root problems that cheap tests cannot reach.
- Manual service work becomes risky when it preserves revenue while pulling resources away from the product path.

## Evidence
- Airbnb host work: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] describes founders meeting hosts, living with them, writing reviews, and helping take photos.
- Customer-love threshold: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] cites the contrast between 100 loving customers and one million indifferent users.
- Nextdoor manual seeding: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] describes asking for licenses, HOA rosters, door-to-door outreach, phone calls to small neighborhoods, and babysitter-demand walks.
- Automation transition: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] says Nextdoor later systematized manual processes into technology.
- Root-problem access: [[4-lessons-from-a-failed-startup-from-and-for-first-time-founders]] says [[Maderight]] later believed operating a micro-factory deserved a deeper look because it could have put the team closer to apparel lead-time and quality problems.
- Service trap: [[4-lessons-from-a-failed-startup-from-and-for-first-time-founders]] says Maderight's high-touch service work produced customers but later competed with the technology product for time and resources.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Manual work can reveal demand, but it can also hide unit-economics, compliance, safety, operational costs, or strategy conflicts that emerge only at scale. The successful scaling source and Maderight failure source together imply that unscalable work should be treated as a discovery method and bridge, not proof that every high-touch behavior can become a scalable product.
