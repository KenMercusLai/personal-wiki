## Definition
[[Usability]] is the quality of an interface that determines how easily and pleasantly intended users can accomplish their goals, together with the methods used to improve that quality during design.

## Current Synthesis
Nielsen divides usability into learnability, efficiency, memorability, errors and recovery, and satisfaction. These dimensions prevent “easy to use” from collapsing into one impression: a design can be quick for experts but difficult to learn, memorable but error-prone, or successful but unpleasant. Usability also does not substitute for utility. A product is useful only when it offers capabilities people need and makes those capabilities realistically accessible. The article treats usability as an economic and design constraint: poor web interfaces encourage immediate abandonment, while poor internal systems consume paid employee time.

## Key Claims
- Usability has at least five distinct dimensions: learnability, efficiency, memorability, errors and recovery, and satisfaction.
- Usefulness requires both usability and utility; ease cannot rescue irrelevant functionality, and valuable functionality cannot help when people cannot operate it.
- Usability should be evaluated through user behavior on realistic tasks, not inferred only from designer intent or user opinion.
- Early, repeated testing makes structural problems cheaper to correct than testing only after implementation.
- The consequences of poor usability vary by context, including abandonment and lost conversion on the web and lower productivity in internal systems.

## Evidence
- Quality dimensions: [[usability-101-introduction-to-usability]] defines learnability, efficiency, memorability, errors, and satisfaction as complementary components.
- Usefulness model: [[usability-101-introduction-to-usability]] distinguishes needed functionality from the ease and pleasure of accessing it.
- Behavioral evaluation: [[usability-101-introduction-to-usability]] recommends observing representative users on representative tasks without coaching them.
- Lifecycle timing: [[usability-101-introduction-to-usability]] places research before redesign, across prototypes and iterations, and after implementation.
- Organizational stakes: [[usability-101-introduction-to-usability]] connects web difficulty to leaving and intranet difficulty to wasted employee time.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The five-part model is a practical decomposition, not an exhaustive account of product quality: accessibility, utility, trust, safety, desirability, and context can independently determine whether an experience works. The source's return-on-investment figures and five-user recommendation are broad heuristics from Nielsen's practice rather than universal guarantees; sample needs depend on user diversity, task diversity, risk, and the research question.

## What Changed
- Created a canonical usability concept that separates usability from utility and organizes its five quality dimensions.
- Added early iterative behavioral testing as the main improvement mechanism while qualifying fixed sample-size rules.
