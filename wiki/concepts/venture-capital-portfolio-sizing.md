Venture capital portfolio sizing treats fund construction as a probability problem rather than only a conviction problem. The 500 Startups source argues that if unicorn-scale outcomes appear in only about 1-2% of investments, a fund with fewer than 50 companies can easily miss that class of outcome entirely.

The concept challenges concentrated VC portfolios. Concentration can produce high returns when a portfolio contains a major winner, but the same construction creates a high chance of having no large outcome when the base rate is low. In the source's framing, sub-50-company portfolios can become a form of gambling with LP capital unless the investor has a reliably higher unicorn hit rate than the market.

The sizing recommendation depends on stage. The source suggests roughly 50-100 companies for later-stage funds and at least 100-200 companies for early-stage funds. Early-stage portfolios need more breadth because startup attrition is higher before product-market fit, follow-on financing, and exit paths are visible.

The article also points to board-seat norms as one possible reason portfolios remain small. If investors believe they must personally serve on many boards, they may cap portfolio count below the level implied by outcome probabilities. Separating governance rights from direct board service can make a larger portfolio more operationally plausible.

## Sizing Questions

- What is the fund's assumed unicorn or large-outcome hit rate?
- How many investments are needed for a credible chance at more than one large outcome?
- Does the stage of investment create enough attrition risk to require a larger portfolio?
- Is the fund limiting company count because of investment math, or because of board-service habits?
- Would a concentrated portfolio still work if it produced zero unicorns?
