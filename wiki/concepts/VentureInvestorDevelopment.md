## Definition
[[VentureInvestorDevelopment]] is the selection and formation of venture investors through attributes, domain capability, mentorship, networks, repeated investment work, and experience across company and market cycles.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents investor development as both a selection problem and a long-duration learning problem. Candidate qualities include curiosity, relevant expertise, passion, focus, relationship building, persuasion, emotional intelligence, and conviction, with communication practices from journalism offering a concrete model for inquiry and synthesis. Yet these traits are not a complete recipe: investment judgment is also shaped by mentorship, repeated portfolio problems, failure, and changing market conditions.

A scalable program could combine software, education, institutional hiring, brand, fellowships, syndicates, scouts, and peer networks. Its hardest task would be measuring intangibles without confusing existing access with future ability. The source's diversity goal therefore creates an important design test: a system should broaden opportunity across gender, race, and geography, but reliance on prestigious experience, network strength, or incumbent validation may reproduce the barriers it intends to lower.

## Key Claims
- Strong investor selection requires several cognitive, interpersonal, commercial, and domain capabilities rather than a single background credential.
- Research and communication practices associated with journalism can support investor sourcing, inquiry, verification, and concise explanation.
- Apprenticeship, mentorship, repeated company problems, and exposure to market cycles are formative inputs that short assessments cannot easily replace.
- Technology, education, brand, and network mechanisms can be combined into new investor-development models, but the source does not establish which combination works.
- Pipeline expansion is a substantive success criterion because capital, relationships, experience, and geography make venture entry unusually difficult.

## Evidence
- Multidimensional qualities: [[decoding-the-qualities-of-a-great-vc-by]] gives eight core attributes and records six additional community suggestions.
- Transferable inquiry skills: [[decoding-the-qualities-of-a-great-vc-by]] uses journalists' sourcing, questioning, validation, and communication practices as a model.
- Experiential formation: [[decoding-the-qualities-of-a-great-vc-by]] invokes mentorship, prior operating or writing experience, the dot-com cycle, and portfolio-company problems as influences on investor judgment.
- Program design space: [[decoding-the-qualities-of-a-great-vc-by]] points to franchising, classes, fellowships, syndicates, scouts, internal training, self-funded learning, and a possible “YC for investors.”
- Access objective: [[decoding-the-qualities-of-a-great-vc-by]] identifies gender, race, geography, capital, networks, and experience as dimensions of the investor-pipeline problem.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The framework comes from one 2017 investor essay and a social-media discussion, not a prospective study of investor performance. Its qualities are broad, overlap conceptually, lack operational definitions and weights, and may be judged differently across cultures or investment strategies. The author explicitly does not endorse every crowdsourced addition. No evidence shows that a course, ranking system, fellowship, scout program, or other “YC for investors” predicts returns, improves founder outcomes, or produces more durable diversity. Selection based on network strength, conventional experience, or approval by established investors may encode incumbency and homophily rather than latent skill.
