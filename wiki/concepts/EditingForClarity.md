## Definition
[[EditingForClarity]] is the deliberate revision of purpose, language, context, tone, and visual structure so readers can recover the intended meaning with less ambiguity and effort.

## Current Synthesis
[[EvaParish]] treats editing as alignment before correction. A writer first records the main point, audience, and intended tone in a private preamble, then tests the draft against that brief. Revision can repeat the central purpose at useful boundaries, name the noun behind an ambiguous “this” or “that,” remove words that do not change the meaning, turn indirect instructions into imperatives, split overloaded sentences, and keep tone consistent.

Clarity also acts as a test of understanding. Replacing passive constructions with named actors can reveal that the writer does not know which person, service, or component performs an action. Replacing adverbs, jargon, cliches, and unexplained acronyms with concrete verbs, definitions, or examples makes assumptions visible. Headings, short paragraphs, lists, tables, bold emphasis, and whitespace then expose the information hierarchy for readers who scan. These are diagnostic heuristics, not universal bans: the governing standard is whether a deliberate choice serves the message and audience.

## Key Claims
- Editing begins with an explicit statement of purpose, audience, and intended effect.
- Useful repetition provides orientation and closure, while precise nouns prevent reference ambiguity.
- Concision removes interpretive work when it eliminates hedges, indirect instruction, and redundant construction rather than necessary context.
- Naming actors and choosing specific verbs can reveal gaps in both prose and the writer's underlying model.
- Reader knowledge should be treated as a design constraint through definitions, expanded acronyms, context, and optional links.
- Tone and visual hierarchy are part of meaning because readers infer stance and often scan before reading closely.
- Editing rules are best used as prompts for judgment rather than as unconditional prohibitions.

## Evidence
- Editorial brief: [[eva-parish-what-i-think-about-when-i-edit]] recommends a private preamble naming the point, audience, narrator, tone, or mood, then comparing the draft with that intent.
- Precision and compression: [[eva-parish-what-i-think-about-when-i-edit]] demonstrates replacing ambiguous pronouns, “you should” phrasing, stacked clauses, excess words, and long sentences with more direct constructions.
- Understanding through agency: [[eva-parish-what-i-think-about-when-i-edit]] argues that active voice in technical prose can expose uncertainty about which component triggers an alert or starts a job.
- Audience context: [[eva-parish-what-i-think-about-when-i-edit]] expands an unexplained TTFB reference into a definition, a short functional explanation, and an optional link.
- Scan design: [[eva-parish-what-i-think-about-when-i-edit]] recommends short paragraphs, useful headings, lists, tables, bold emphasis, and whitespace for screen readers who skim.
- Judgment boundary: [[eva-parish-what-i-think-about-when-i-edit]] closes by subordinating its individual recommendations to conscious choices suited to the intended message.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is one practitioner's cross-genre craft essay, not a comparative study of comprehension, task success, accessibility, or reader preference. Active voice is not always superior when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or intentionally backgrounded; repetition can become clutter; adverbs can be exact; and jargon can be efficient within a genuinely shared specialist context. Imperatives, brevity, bold emphasis, and informal explanation also need to fit genre, culture, accessibility needs, risk, and audience expectations.
