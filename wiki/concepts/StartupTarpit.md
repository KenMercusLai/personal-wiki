## Definition
A [[StartupTarpit]] is a state of commercial inertia in which a startup has a functioning product but has spent so much runway pursuing product additions that it lacks the distribution capacity, revenue evidence, or financing leverage needed to escape stagnation.

## Current Synthesis
The source describes a reinforcing allocation failure. Slow growth is diagnosed as missing product capability, so the team funds more engineering; the added features produce little measurable growth, which consumes runway without creating the metrics needed for another round. By the time the team tests marketing, it may have a stable product but insufficient cash to learn quickly or scale a working channel.

Vonjour's reported recovery path was to stop feature development, redirect revenue toward paid search, and use the resulting traffic to test the actual funnel. This revealed that signup friction—not product breadth—was a major constraint and reportedly reduced CAC after small changes. The broader lesson is not that marketing always dominates product work, but that product, acquisition, conversion, retention, and economics should be tested as competing bottlenecks before scarce capital is concentrated in one of them.

## Key Claims
- A good-enough product can remain commercially inert when founders assume every growth problem is a feature problem.
- Feature spending becomes a trap when it consumes runway without improving demand, conversion, retention, revenue, or fundraising evidence.
- Early acquisition experiments can test whether distribution is the binding constraint before the team commits more capital to development.
- More traffic can improve product learning by exposing conversion bottlenecks that a small organic sample hides.
- A stable, lean product base can preserve the option to redirect resources toward distribution and measured funnel improvement.
- Escaping the trap requires viable acquisition and retention economics, not merely a larger signup count.

## Evidence
- Misallocated runway: [[its-not-a-feature-problem-avoiding-startup-tarpits-by]] says Vonjour expanded its feature list while revenue failed to change meaningfully and seed funding ran out.
- Distribution test: [[its-not-a-feature-problem-avoiding-startup-tarpits-by]] reports a sizable signup increase after the company began paid advertising.
- Bottleneck discovery: [[its-not-a-feature-problem-avoiding-startup-tarpits-by]] says paid traffic exposed a difficult signup flow and that small changes reduced reported CAC from about $130 to $70.
- Recovery mechanism: [[its-not-a-feature-problem-avoiding-startup-tarpits-by]] credits product stability and reduced engineering spend with allowing revenue to be redirected toward marketing.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is coined and supported here by one retrospective rather than comparative evidence. Growth stagnation can reflect weak value, poor retention, pricing, market timing, reliability, sales motion, regulation, or economics rather than insufficient distribution. Paid acquisition can also accelerate losses when attribution, conversion quality, gross margin, churn, or lifetime value is weak. Vonjour's reported figures omit campaign period, sample size, gross margin, churn, cohort retention, customer count, and independent verification, while its scale projection assumes acquisition cost and conversion remain stable as spend rises.

## What Changed
- Created the concept as a resource-allocation feedback loop rather than a synonym for slow growth.
- Identified acquisition experiments and funnel measurement as tests for a misdiagnosed feature problem.
- Preserved product quality, retention, and unit economics as necessary boundaries on a marketing-led recovery.
