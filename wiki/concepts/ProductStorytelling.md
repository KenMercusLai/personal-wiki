## Definition
[[ProductStorytelling]] is the practice of explaining product direction through a coherent narrative and supporting model so teams and stakeholders understand why the work matters.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats product storytelling as the communication step after divergent product thinking has converged on a goal. The recommended story covers past, present, and future: what has happened, what the current state is, and what the product will make possible. Data should support the point without overwhelming the listener, and quantitative models should show the expected impact or ROI. The goal is not decorative narrative; it is to make strategic direction digestible enough for teams and stakeholders to choose where time should go.

## Key Claims
- Product direction often becomes clear only after divergent thinking converges on one goal.
- Teams and stakeholders need a digestible story, not only raw analysis.
- A useful product story covers past context, present status, and future vision.
- Data should support the story without making it overly complicated.
- Quantitative models can clarify ROI and guide strategic decisions about time allocation.

## Evidence
- Story arc: [[7-ways-to-use-the-rule-of-threes-to-build-great-products]] says a good story covers the past, touches the status quo, and builds a future vision with the product in the world.
- Data restraint: [[7-ways-to-use-the-rule-of-threes-to-build-great-products]] says the story should include key supporting data but avoid unnecessary complication.
- ROI model: [[7-ways-to-use-the-rule-of-threes-to-build-great-products]] recommends models that show quantifiable impact and help stakeholders decide how much time to spend on product work.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Storytelling can distort product judgment if narrative coherence substitutes for weak evidence. The source's model advice helps counter that risk, but the model is only useful when assumptions and metric links are explicit enough to be challenged.
