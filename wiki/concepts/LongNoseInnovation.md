## Definition
[[LongNoseInnovation]] is [[BillBuxton]]'s frame for the long, low-visibility gestation period in which technologies are invented, copied, adapted, refined, and commercialized before they appear as sudden breakthroughs.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that the public story of innovation is usually too event-centered. Multi-touch on the iPhone or the mainstream computer mouse can feel like sudden invention, but Buxton treats those moments as late visible points in a much longer development curve.

The practical lesson is organizational: people who want future impact should learn to identify already-existing but underdeveloped ideas, then support the refinement work that turns them into reliable, usable, market-shaping products. Sinofsky's CES 2018 report supplies the near-term product-cycle view of the same process. Voice control, Qi charging, OLED, wireless audio, connected appliances, and specialized sensing did not need to replace everything at once; several years of component, standards, integration, and usability work made formerly awkward ideas increasingly ordinary. In this view, shortening a technology's long nose through better recognition, integration, design, and commercialization can be as valuable as originating the first prototype.

## Key Claims
- Breakthrough products often stand on decades of low-visibility prior work.
- The difference between invention and innovation lies in refinement, augmentation, adoption, and practical usability.
- A 20- to 30-year path from first concept to major industry impact is common in technology history.
- Technologies likely to matter in the next decade are often already old enough to be discoverable.
- Organizations that chase invention alone may neglect the higher-leverage work of recognizing and refining neglected ideas.
- Year-to-year improvements in standards and everyday products are visible slices of the same long maturation process.

## Evidence
- Hidden gestation: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] describes the Long Nose as the long low-amplitude history behind a visible "wow" moment.
- Mouse history: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] traces the mouse from William English and Doug Engelbart's mid-1960s work through NRC Canada, Xerox PARC, PERQ-1, Xerox Star, Macintosh, and Windows 95.
- Technology timescale: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] cites Butler Lampson's National Research Council work to argue that 20 years to a billion-dollar industry is typical and 30 years is not unusual.
- Ten-year implication: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] argues that technologies likely to matter within the next decade are already at least 10 years old.
- Refinement value: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] says innovation depends on prospecting, mining, refining, and goldsmithing, not only original creation.
- Show-floor accumulation: [[ces-2018-real-advances-real-progress-real-questions]] argues that CES should be judged by whether everyday life improves over the next product cycles, not whether every exhibit is wholly new.
- Standards critical mass: [[ces-2018-real-advances-real-progress-real-questions]] uses Qi charging after iPhone adoption as an example of an industry effort needing several turns of the product-cycle crank before broadly useful products appear.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The Buxton source is a reconstructed archive note rather than a full primary copy of the original BusinessWeek article, while the CES source is one observer's show-floor report. Their examples are strong as technology-history and product-cycle illustrations, but they do not prove that every domain follows a 20- to 30-year maturation path or that every incremental change compounds into adoption. The framing may also understate cases where regulation, distribution, business-model timing, or complementary infrastructure matters as much as technical refinement.
