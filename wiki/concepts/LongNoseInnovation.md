## Definition
[[LongNoseInnovation]] is [[BillBuxton]]'s frame for the long, low-visibility gestation period in which technologies are invented, copied, adapted, refined, and commercialized before they appear as sudden breakthroughs.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that the public story of innovation is usually too event-centered. Multi-touch on the iPhone or the mainstream computer mouse can feel like sudden invention, but Buxton treats those moments as late visible points in a much longer development curve.

The practical lesson is organizational: people who want future impact should learn to identify already-existing but underdeveloped ideas, then support the refinement work that turns them into reliable, usable, market-shaping products. Sinofsky's CES 2018 report supplies the near-term product-cycle view of the same process. Voice control, Qi charging, OLED, wireless audio, connected appliances, and specialized sensing did not need to replace everything at once; several years of component, standards, integration, and usability work made formerly awkward ideas increasingly ordinary.

O'Reilly broadens the frame from the history of a product to transitions across markets and institutions. Algorithmic coordination, mobile payments, drone logistics, precision agriculture, genomics, neural interfaces, and embedded learning can each look abrupt when adoption becomes visible, even though capabilities, infrastructure, and practices accumulated beforehand. This broader use makes the Long Nose a useful attention heuristic, not a timing forecast: observers should look for interacting changes nearing consequence without assuming that every promising precursor will cross a threshold.

## Key Claims
- Breakthrough products often stand on decades of low-visibility prior work.
- The difference between invention and innovation lies in refinement, augmentation, adoption, and practical usability.
- A 20- to 30-year path from first concept to major industry impact is common in technology history.
- Technologies likely to matter in the next decade are often already old enough to be discoverable.
- Organizations that chase invention alone may neglect the higher-leverage work of recognizing and refining neglected ideas.
- Year-to-year improvements in standards and everyday products are visible slices of the same long maturation process.
- Apparently abrupt market or institutional change can reflect accumulated capabilities, infrastructure, and practice rather than a single product invention.

## Evidence
- Hidden gestation: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] describes the Long Nose as the long low-amplitude history behind a visible "wow" moment.
- Mouse history: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] traces the mouse from William English and Doug Engelbart's mid-1960s work through NRC Canada, Xerox PARC, PERQ-1, Xerox Star, Macintosh, and Windows 95.
- Technology timescale: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] cites Butler Lampson's National Research Council work to argue that 20 years to a billion-dollar industry is typical and 30 years is not unusual.
- Ten-year implication: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] argues that technologies likely to matter within the next decade are already at least 10 years old.
- Refinement value: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] says innovation depends on prospecting, mining, refining, and goldsmithing, not only original creation.
- Show-floor accumulation: [[ces-2018-real-advances-real-progress-real-questions]] argues that CES should be judged by whether everyday life improves over the next product cycles, not whether every exhibit is wholly new.
- Standards critical mass: [[ces-2018-real-advances-real-progress-real-questions]] uses Qi charging after iPhone adoption as an example of an industry effort needing several turns of the product-cycle crank before broadly useful products appear.
- Cross-domain accumulation: [[gradually-then-suddenly-oreilly]] applies the gradual-then-sudden pattern to algorithmic firms, infrastructure leapfrogging, agriculture, genomics, neural interfaces, learning, climate, and government capacity.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The Buxton source is a reconstructed archive note rather than a full primary copy of the original BusinessWeek article, the CES source is one observer's show-floor report, and O'Reilly's article is a broad 2019 forecast whose saved Africa and economics passages are incomplete. Their examples are useful technology-history and product-cycle illustrations, but they do not prove that every domain follows a 20- to 30-year maturation path, identify when a threshold will arrive, or show that every incremental change compounds into adoption. Regulation, distribution, institutions, business-model timing, and complementary infrastructure can matter as much as technical refinement; retrospective narratives also risk selecting visible successes while ignoring long-maturing ideas that failed.

## What Changed
- Broadened the model from product gestation to market and institutional transitions built from interacting capabilities, infrastructure, and practice.
- Added a forecasting boundary: gradual accumulation helps direct attention but does not identify which precursor will cross a threshold or when.
