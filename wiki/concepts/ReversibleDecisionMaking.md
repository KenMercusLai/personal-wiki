## Definition
[[ReversibleDecisionMaking]] is the practice of varying decision speed, information demands, and review burden according to how completely and cheaply a choice can be undone.

## Current Synthesis
The Farnam Street article presents Bezos's two-way-door and one-way-door distinction as a practical response to uncertainty. A genuinely reversible choice can be made by a small, high-judgment group before information is complete because action creates evidence and a poor result can be corrected. An irreversible or nearly irreversible choice deserves more deliberate analysis, consultation, and understanding because learning after commitment may arrive too late.

The value lies in proportional process, not indiscriminate speed. The article's reported 70% threshold is a heuristic for correctable choices, not a universal probability rule. Reversibility must include real rollback cost, time, affected people, data loss, safety, trust, and downstream commitments; otherwise a nominally reversible decision can conceal one-way consequences.

## Key Claims
- Decision process should scale with consequence and actual reversibility rather than use one heavyweight standard for every choice.
- Reversible decisions can often proceed with incomplete information because action produces feedback and correction remains available.
- Irreversible or nearly irreversible decisions require slower analysis, broader consultation, and clearer understanding.
- Fast reversible cycles can improve organizational learning and reduce the opportunity cost of waiting for near-perfect certainty.
- Treating small failures as information can support course correction and weaken sunk-cost persistence.
- Organizations lose invention when they misclassify most choices as one-way doors, but reckless classification in the other direction can externalize lasting harm.

## Evidence
- Process distinction: [[go-fast-and-break-things-the-difference-between-reversible-and-irreversible-decisions]] reproduces Bezos's Type 1/Type 2 account of slow one-way-door decisions and fast two-way-door decisions.
- Information threshold: [[go-fast-and-break-things-the-difference-between-reversible-and-irreversible-decisions]] attributes to Bezos the advice to act around 70% certainty and course-correct rather than wait for 90%.
- Learning loop: [[go-fast-and-break-things-the-difference-between-reversible-and-irreversible-decisions]] argues that decisions generate data and that faster feedback cycles improve later judgment.
- Organizational cost: [[go-fast-and-break-things-the-difference-between-reversible-and-irreversible-decisions]] says large organizations often impose Type 1 procedure on Type 2 choices, producing slowness, risk aversion, and less experimentation.
- Visual model: [[go-fast-and-break-things-the-difference-between-reversible-and-irreversible-decisions]] includes an inspected diagram contrasting a circular two-way path with a single forward arrow.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The article is a practitioner synthesis built around Bezos's account, not comparative evidence that a 70% threshold improves outcomes. It does not provide a reliable classification test, quantify rollback cost, or address decisions whose effects are reversible for the decision-maker but not for customers, employees, counterparties, or the public.

More information can be distracting, but the article's second-hand medical example is not a basis for clinical decisions. Safety-critical, legal, security, privacy, persistent-data, reputational, and high-blast-radius choices may warrant additional evidence even when a technical rollback exists.

## What Changed
- Created the concept and separated proportional decision process from a generic bias toward speed.
- Made actual rollback cost and third-party consequences explicit limits on the two-way-door classification.
