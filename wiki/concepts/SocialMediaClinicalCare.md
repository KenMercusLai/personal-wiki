## Definition
[[SocialMediaClinicalCare]] is the use of online platforms by healthcare professionals to support patients through community spaces, coping resources, reliable information, or clinically relevant communication while maintaining professional boundaries.

## Current Synthesis
The source uses cancer care as a constructive counterpoint to patient-targeted searching. Social platforms can meet patients where they already spend time, help patients and families connect with peers, and distribute clinician-vetted information. But the benefit depends on boundary discipline: online clinical support should not collapse personal and professional identities, expose private patient information, or make patients feel secretly watched.

## Key Claims
- Social platforms can support patient community, emotional needs, and psychosocial care.
- Clinician-vetted communities can help patients share coping strategies and reliable medical information.
- Cancer-care settings illustrate constructive use because mental health clinicians often support medically ill patients beyond narrow diagnosis.
- Professional boundaries and privacy remain necessary even when the intent is supportive.
- Constructive online engagement should be distinguished from unilateral [[PatientTargetedOnlineSearch]].

- Reliable information: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] says clinicians can vet medical information in online communities.
- Patient familiarity: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] quotes [[LiliyaGershengoren]] supporting social media for emotional and psychosocial needs because many patients already know those platforms.
- Boundary need: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] says clinicians should maintain professional boundaries and privacy online.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The article presents social-media clinical care through guidelines and supportive expert opinion rather than measured outcomes. It does not evaluate moderation workload, misinformation risk, patient confidentiality failures, accessibility, platform governance, or how clinicians should separate personal profiles from professional communities.
