## Definition
[[CorporateRiskAversion]] is an organization's preference for predictable, financially legible outcomes that leads it to avoid, narrow, redirect, or exit uncertain investments even when it has ample capital and valuable strategic assets.

## Current Synthesis
The Disney games case shows risk aversion as an interaction among uncertainty, capability, governance, and opportunity cost rather than simple unwillingness to spend. Disney could forecast films, television, and parks from established experience, while internally published games required long development, repeated failure, and uncertain returns. Former employees say that gap favored spreadsheet-led decisions, shifting mandates, repeated cancellations, and overlapping approvals.

Licensing was the resulting risk-transfer mechanism: Disney retained intellectual-property leverage while external companies carried more development and publishing exposure. Disney Infinity makes the judgment difficult rather than one-sided. Ending a billion-dollar franchise before expected decline supports the article's risk-aversion thesis, but high costs and weakening toys-to-life growth also give management a rational capital-allocation argument.

Norton's essay generalizes the hierarchy and portfolio mechanism. It argues that management systems reduce tolerance for failure as approval layers increase, and contrasts a nearly certain modest project with a small chance of much greater upside. Kodak supplies the incumbent version: fear of harming profitable film reportedly blocked an internally invented digital successor. This strengthens the concept's link to [[LossAversion]] while also sharpening its boundary—arithmetic expected value does not make a concentrated existential bet prudent when the organization cannot survive failure.

## Key Claims
- Large cash reserves do not imply willingness to fund outcomes that are difficult to forecast or compare with established businesses.
- Weak domain knowledge increases apparent uncertainty and makes financial proxies more influential in product decisions.
- Frequent mandate changes and project cancellations can prevent an acquired team from accumulating the evidence needed to justify continued investment.
- Layered approval across corporate and franchise owners can impose delays that are especially costly in fast product cycles.
- Licensing can reduce operational downside while sacrificing internal capability, product control, and some potential margin.
- Exiting before projected decline may be prudent allocation, excessive downside sensitivity, or both; outcome labels alone cannot decide which.
- Hierarchy can amplify downside sensitivity when each approval layer is rewarded for avoiding visible failure rather than preserving portfolio upside.

## Evidence
- Forecastability gap: [[inside-disneys-messy-video-game-business-business-insider]] says Disney understood likely returns in film and television better than in games.
- Product-versus-spreadsheet judgment: [[inside-disneys-messy-video-game-business-business-insider]] reports Alex Seropian's contrast between Microsoft and Disney decision making.
- Organizational churn: [[inside-disneys-messy-video-game-business-business-insider]] says Wideload's mandate changed repeatedly and projects were prototyped and killed.
- Approval burden: [[inside-disneys-messy-video-game-business-business-insider]] describes separate Disney and Marvel oversight on a mobile game.
- Risk transfer: [[inside-disneys-messy-video-game-business-business-insider]] quotes Bob Iger saying licensing managed risk better than publishing.
- Ambiguous exit: [[inside-disneys-messy-video-game-business-business-insider]] pairs Disney Infinity's scale with expected toys-to-life decline and high development cost.
- Hierarchy and project selection: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] argues that tolerance for risk and failure falls as management hierarchy grows and uses an expected-value thought experiment to contrast probable modest returns with unlikely large upside.
- Incumbent exposure: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] says fear of damage to Kodak's film business obstructed its internally developed digital camera.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence consists of a 2016 journalistic retrospective centered on former Disney employees and a 2015 keynote-derived practitioner essay. Disney declined to comment, and neither source provides the internal hurdle rates, forecasts, project budgets, complete portfolios, counterfactual releases, or later returns needed for causal comparison. Canceled projects are not automatically good projects, internal capability is not valuable at any price, and a firm can rationally choose established higher-return businesses. Norton's expected-value illustration also omits variance, correlation, liquidity, timing, and ruin. Conversely, project-by-project financial discipline can become self-defeating when a hit-driven portfolio needs stable mandates and enough survivable attempts for learning and upside to emerge.

## What Changed
- Added hierarchy, expected-value framing, and incumbent-profit defense as general mechanisms beyond Disney's game portfolio.
- Added organizational survival as a boundary on treating high arithmetic expectation as a sufficient decision rule.
