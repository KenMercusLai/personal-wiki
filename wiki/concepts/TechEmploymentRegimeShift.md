## Definition
[[TechEmploymentRegimeShift]] is the proposed transition from a cheap-capital technology labor market characterized by rapid hiring, broad experimentation, strong engineer bargaining power, and generous benefits toward one emphasizing profitability, concentrated executive priorities, workforce reduction, and direct business contribution.

## Current Synthesis
Goedecke's account joins financing conditions to the employment bargain. When capital was cheap and investors rewarded users, growth, and valuation, technology companies could fund many initiatives at once and compete aggressively for engineers. Higher rates, public-market pressure, and the end of the temporary COVID demand boom then made focus and profit more valuable, encouraging layoffs and reducing support for work such as open-source libraries, developer experience, and speculative product expansion.

The regime framing separates corporate alignment from personal identification. An engineer may still value technically worthy or socially useful work that leadership does not fund, but should not confuse past organizational tolerance with permanent shared purpose. In the source's descriptive model, rewards increasingly follow visible progress against executive plans. That can clarify priorities while weakening autonomy, security, and the space for long-horizon work.

This remains a practitioner thesis rather than a demonstrated macroeconomic decomposition. Interest rates are plausible but not isolated from pandemic over-hiring, company maturation, market imitation, heterogeneous business conditions, or later AI adoption. The source's March 2025 judgment that AI had not caused the shift is time-bounded and does not establish future labor effects.

## Key Claims
- Capital cost can change the employment bargain by altering how much unprofitable growth, staffing, experimentation, and employee retention a company is willing to fund.
- The end of a temporary demand boom can trigger layoffs without being the deeper cause of a longer change in investment incentives.
- Tighter resource allocation concentrates engineering work around fewer executive priorities and raises the career cost of pursuing unfunded interests.
- Engineer and employer interests may overlap without being identical; perks, consultation, and technical latitude do not prove shared institutional purpose.
- A clearer demand to ship business value can improve priority legibility while reducing job security, bargaining power, and support for indirect or long-horizon work.
- Claims about AI displacement must be dated and evidenced separately from layoffs attributed to capital conditions or pandemic hiring.

## Evidence
- Financing mechanism: [[the-good-times-in-tech-are-over]] contrasts near-zero-rate investment and valuation-led growth in the 2010s with a roughly 5% rate environment that rewarded profitability and restraint.
- Demand correction: [[the-good-times-in-tech-are-over]] identifies COVID-era engagement and hiring as a temporary boom whose reversal contributed to the first large layoff rounds.
- Priority contraction: [[the-good-times-in-tech-are-over]] reports a shift from simultaneous product, platform, open-source, and developer-experience initiatives toward a few leadership-selected bets.
- Employment consequences: [[the-good-times-in-tech-are-over]] argues that work outside those bets is more likely to be judged ineffective and expose vulnerable engineers to layoffs.
- AI boundary: [[the-good-times-in-tech-are-over]] calls contemporary AI attribution unsupported while explicitly allowing future software-job displacement.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sole source is a March 2025 practitioner essay, not a labor-economics study. It supplies no time series, cross-company sample, rate-sensitivity estimate, layoff-cause coding, employee survey, or counterfactual. "Tech" covers firms with different capital structures, profitability, maturity, geography, and talent needs, while software engineers vary in seniority, specialty, immigration status, and bargaining power. Interest rates, pandemic demand, prior over-hiring, public-market repricing, management fashion, antitrust conditions, and AI investment may interact. Executive priorities can also be wrong or short-term; ethical, legal, safety, reliability, and professional obligations bound any instruction to maximize company value. The framework is therefore a hypothesis for interpreting changing incentives, not a universal career rule or proof of a single cause.

## What Changed
- Established a source-scoped model linking capital cost to engineering employment, initiative breadth, and bargaining power.
- Separated the longer financing-regime thesis from the shorter COVID hiring correction.
- Made the March 2025 rejection of AI-caused layoffs explicit and time-bounded.
