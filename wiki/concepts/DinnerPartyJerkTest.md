## Definition
[[DinnerPartyJerkTest]] is [[AndrewChen]]'s founder-pitch diagnostic: if describing the team, achievements, evidence, and future upside would not feel uncomfortably self-promotional at a normal dinner party, the startup pitch is probably too muted.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames the test as a way to separate social modesty from startup communication. In ordinary friendly settings, people avoid bragging because it violates conformity norms and can make differences among peers feel too visible. In fundraising, recruiting, partnership, and media contexts, Chen argues that this restraint hides the exact signals stakeholders need: why this team is unusually suited to the market, what earned secret led to the idea, what early proof exists, why the market can become much larger, and why the work matters if it succeeds. The test therefore functions as a prompt to enter pitch mode deliberately, not as permission to deceive.

## Key Claims
- Early founders often underpitch because dinner-party norms reward modesty while startup stakeholders need evidence of ambition and exceptional fit.
- The test asks whether the pitch includes achievements, credentials, earned secrets, traction, small proof points, and a future-oriented market narrative.
- Pre-seed and seed pitches especially need team and product-belief signals because later-stage revenue or unit-economics evidence may not yet exist.
- The same future story helps investors assess ambition, employees see adventure and meaning, journalists notice the company, and founders preserve motivation.
- Pitch optimism must coexist with sober operating awareness of missing fit, weak economics, marketing problems, and team gaps.

## Evidence
- Diagnostic framing: [[andrew-chen-the-dinner-party-jerk-test]] says a proper pitch may make a kind founder blush because it includes claims that would sound socially excessive at dinner.
- Stage logic: [[andrew-chen-the-dinner-party-jerk-test]] describes pre-seed as a team bet, seed as product, Series A as traction, Series B as revenue, and Series C as unit economics.
- Earned-secret evidence: [[andrew-chen-the-dinner-party-jerk-test]] recommends that less-credentialed first-time founders explain the non-obvious lived insight, market observation, metrics, or professional experience behind the idea.
- Future narrative: [[andrew-chen-the-dinner-party-jerk-test]] tells founders to emphasize what the company could become, upside, customer love, grit, wedge strategy, and why the world is moving toward the opportunity.
- Operating boundary: [[andrew-chen-the-dinner-party-jerk-test]] closes by warning founders not to ignore unresolved product-market fit, unit economics, marketing, or team problems.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The test is advice from a venture-backed startup context, not a universal communication rule. Conservative investors, non-venture businesses, regulated industries, or cultures that strongly penalize self-promotion may require a different balance of ambition and proof. The source also assumes the founder is genuinely aiming for a large outcome; using the test to inflate a smaller or lifestyle-oriented company would misrepresent the founder's real intent.
