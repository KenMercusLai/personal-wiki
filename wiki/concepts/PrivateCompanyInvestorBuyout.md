## Definition
[[PrivateCompanyInvestorBuyout]] is a negotiated repurchase of outside investors' shares by a private company or its founders, using operating cash, debt, or another financing source to provide liquidity and change ownership or control without selling the company or going public.

## Current Synthesis
The Inc. cases present an investor buyout as a possible reset when a viable company no longer fits venture-scale growth and exit expectations. [[Wistia]] used $17.3 million of debt, offered investors a reported 10x return, and regained full control; [[Buffer]] used cash flow to buy roughly two-thirds of its Series A investors' holdings for about $3.3 million. The mechanism is not a simple reversal of fundraising: founders must establish a price, finance the purchase, preserve operating resilience, negotiate with investors who may sell only part of their stakes, and state what future liquidity investors should expect.

## Key Claims
- A buyout can realign ownership when founders prefer a profitable independent company to venture-scale growth, acquisition, or IPO.
- Operating cash flow avoids new external financing but competes with reserves, hiring, product investment, and later investor liquidity.
- Debt can fund a larger repurchase and restore control sooner, while adding fixed repayment risk to the operating company.
- Original ownership percentages, preference terms, liquidity clauses, prior disclosures, and investor willingness shape feasibility and price.
- Partial repurchases can reduce misalignment without giving every shareholder immediate liquidity or eliminating all outside ownership.
- A moderate or even strong company-level return may still be unattractive to a venture fund whose portfolio depends on rare outliers.

## Evidence
- Debt-financed control reset: [[heres-how-and-why-entrepreneurs-are-getting-venture-investors-out-of-their-companies-inc-com]] reports that Wistia borrowed $17.3 million, offered a 10x return, and regained full control through share repurchases.
- Cash-flow-funded partial exit: [[heres-how-and-why-entrepreneurs-are-getting-venture-investors-out-of-their-companies-inc-com]] reports that Buffer spent about $3.3 million to buy roughly two-thirds of its Series A investors' shares.
- Negotiation inputs: [[heres-how-and-why-entrepreneurs-are-getting-venture-investors-out-of-their-companies-inc-com]] says Buffer's 6.2% Series A ownership, potential profitability, early disclosure about exit goals, and contractual liquidity option gave it a basis for negotiation.
- Investor choice: [[heres-how-and-why-entrepreneurs-are-getting-venture-investors-out-of-their-companies-inc-com]] says Wistia let investors sell as much or as little as they wished while warning that another liquidity event was not planned.
- Reserve constraint: [[heres-how-and-why-entrepreneurs-are-getting-venture-investors-out-of-their-companies-inc-com]] says Buffer deferred seed-investor liquidity while rebuilding cash reserves.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source selects companies that found a workable path and gives no base rate for attempted or failed buyouts. Reported prices and returns are not independently verified, and the article omits loan rates, repayment schedules, tax treatment, preference rights, board approvals, transaction costs, investor accounts, and later company performance. A buyout can concentrate founder control but also weaken liquidity, increase leverage, or transfer risk from investors to employees and the operating company. It is therefore a contingent recapitalization option, not a general escape hatch from venture financing.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to distinguish private-company investor liquidity and control resets from public-market capital-return buybacks.
