## Definition
[[StartupEquityTransparency]] is the norm that startup candidates and employees should clearly understand equity terms, exercise windows, vesting consequences, liquidation preferences, liquidity limits, and realistic payout risks before accepting compensation.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat transparency as both an employee-protection norm and a market-design condition. [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] argues that candidates need to know whether leaving before an IPO can make vested options expensive or impossible to keep, because hidden exercise-window penalties prevent real competition on compensation quality. [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] widens the disclosure burden: employees should understand that equity may be illiquid, forfeitable before vesting, subordinated by preferences, and structurally different from founder equity.

## Key Claims
- Exercise-window rules must be visible before candidates choose a startup job.
- Transparent equity terms enable real competition among compensation strategies.
- Transparency must include liquidity risk and not just the nominal percentage or grant count.
- Vesting cliffs, termination rights, and exercise windows can make promised equity much less protective than it sounds.
- Liquidation preferences and payout order should be understood before employees count equity as probable wealth.
- Disclosure matters even when a company chooses terms that are less favorable to departing employees.
- Founder and employee equity should not be described as if they carry the same control, liquidity, or bargaining power.

## Evidence
- Pre-join disclosure: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] says companies can choose short windows if employees are fully informed before joining.
- Market competition: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] says transparency is necessary to enable true competition between compensation strategies.
- Liquidity risk: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] argues that employee equity has little present value when it cannot be spent, liquidated, or traded.
- Vesting and exercise exposure: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] says short windows can erase practical value after departure, while [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] highlights the one-year cliff before any equity vests.
- Payout order: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] points to share preference as a critical but often under-explained liquidation term.
- Harsher terms: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] says even all-or-nothing pre-IPO equity strategies could be acceptable if candidates know what they are getting into.
- Founder asymmetry: [[4-hard-truths-about-equity-while-west]] says founders can allocate equity, trade it for capital, and sometimes sell privately while ordinary employees hold small illiquid positions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not specify what legally adequate disclosure should look like or how candidates should compare option grants across strike price, valuation, tax treatment, exercise cost, dilution, preferences, and liquidity probability. Transparency improves market choice only if candidates can understand and negotiate the disclosed terms, and even full disclosure cannot make speculative startup equity equivalent to cash.

## What Changed
- Added employee-equity risk as a broader disclosure requirement beyond exercise-window policy.
- Reframed transparency to include liquidity, vesting, preferences, and founder-employee asymmetry.
