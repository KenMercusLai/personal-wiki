## Definition
[[StartupEquityTransparency]] is the norm that startup candidates and employees should clearly understand equity terms, exercise windows, vesting consequences, and liquidity risks before accepting compensation.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats transparency as both an employee-protection norm and a market-design condition. If candidates do not understand that leaving before an IPO may make vested options expensive or impossible to keep, then employers are not truly competing on compensation quality. Clear disclosure lets different compensation strategies compete openly: one startup might offer harsher equity mechanics with more nominal upside, while another might protect vested equity through an extended exercise window.

## Key Claims
- Exercise-window rules must be visible before candidates choose a startup job.
- Transparent equity terms enable real competition among compensation strategies.
- The survival of 90-day exercise windows may partly reflect employee ignorance of their consequences.
- Disclosure matters even when a company chooses terms that are less favorable to departing employees.
- Transparency should cover scenarios where employees leave involuntarily before an IPO.

## Evidence
- Pre-join disclosure: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] says companies can choose short windows if employees are fully informed before joining.
- Market competition: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] says transparency is necessary to enable true competition between compensation strategies.
- Employee ignorance: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] suggests 90-day exercise periods survived because many employees were unaware of the consequences.
- Harsher terms: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] says even all-or-nothing pre-IPO equity strategies could be acceptable if candidates know what they are getting into.
- Involuntary departure: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] asks whether employees would accept the risk of losing equity after family-care departures, performance exits, or role eliminations if they understood it clearly.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not specify what legally adequate disclosure should look like or how candidates should compare option grants across strike price, valuation, tax treatment, exercise cost, dilution, and liquidity probability. Transparency improves market choice only if candidates can understand and negotiate the disclosed terms.
