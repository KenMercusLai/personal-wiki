## Definition
[[ProductLifecycleTrust]] is the confidence that a product, service, feature, API, or supporting ecosystem will remain available and usable long enough to justify the money, data, learning, coordination, development, or hardware commitment required to adopt it.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats lifecycle trust as a portfolio-level asset for platform companies. Adoption often creates dependency: consumers store data and recruit contacts, enterprises change workflows, developers build against APIs, and manufacturers design products around remote services. A shutdown therefore imposes more than migration inconvenience; it changes how prospective adopters value the next offer from the same company.

Repeated closures can transfer risk across otherwise unrelated teams. The article's [[GoogleStadia]] example shows a new product being asked to answer for Google+, Inbox, Hangouts, Reader, Fiber, Allo, and other earlier decisions. Investment size, executive assurances, and cross-company effort can support credibility, but they do not independently prove durable commitment when similarly supported products have later closed. Lifecycle trust therefore depends on demonstrated stewardship, legible support horizons, migration and export paths, proportionate change, and credible recourse—not launch scale alone.

## Key Claims
- Product adoption can create switching costs in data, hardware, social coordination, workflows, development, and partner roadmaps.
- A dense shutdown cadence can make rational portfolio management look like organizational instability when users cannot distinguish which commitments are durable.
- Reputation spillover makes unrelated new products bear the trust cost of earlier closures by the same company.
- Consumers, enterprises, developers, and hardware partners face different dependency costs but share a need for credible support horizons.
- Migration plans reduce harm but do not make feature removal, repricing, service exit, and full closure equivalent or costless.
- Large investment and cross-company scope are incomplete commitment signals without governance, support, portability, and exit evidence.

## Evidence
- Shutdown cadence: [[googles-constant-product-shutdowns-are-damaging-its-brand-ars-technica]] counts an official Google product, feature, or service ending roughly every nine days during the first 91 days of 2019.
- Consumer dependency: [[googles-constant-product-shutdowns-are-damaging-its-brand-ars-technica]] connects closure risk to stored photos, videos, email, posts, purchased connected hardware, and the coordination cost of moving friends and family to a service.
- Enterprise dependency: [[googles-constant-product-shutdowns-are-damaging-its-brand-ars-technica]] argues that enterprise buyers prefer established, predictable partners and notes that named core services can still change or close.
- Developer dependency: [[googles-constant-product-shutdowns-are-damaging-its-brand-ars-technica]] cites months of learning and implementation, Google Maps API repricing, Google Play enforcement, and weak access to human support as investment risks.
- Partner dependency: [[googles-constant-product-shutdowns-are-damaging-its-brand-ars-technica]] contrasts multi-year vehicle and device cycles with Android Things dropping a major supported use case nine months after launch.
- Portfolio spillover: Stadia coverage quoted in [[googles-constant-product-shutdowns-are-damaging-its-brand-ars-technica]] asks whether Google would sustain the new platform for two, three, or five years because of unrelated earlier shutdowns.
- Assurance boundary: [[googles-constant-product-shutdowns-are-damaging-its-brand-ars-technica]] reports Phil Harrison citing Stadia's significant cross-company investment while noting that Google+ once had similarly broad backing.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The founding source is an opinionated April 2019 article, not a measured model of brand equity, adoption, churn, partner investment, or shutdown necessity. It groups feature removals, repricing, geographic withdrawal, product migration, and complete service closure even though their costs and rationales differ. Continuing a weak, unsafe, obsolete, or strategically incompatible product can waste resources or expose users to greater harm, so lifecycle trust cannot mean permanent support for everything. Clear horizons, data export, compatibility, migration help, refunds, open protocols, and advance notice may preserve trust even when closure is warranted, but the source does not compare these remedies. Its Stadia evidence records launch-time skepticism only and does not establish later outcomes.

## What Changed
- Established a portfolio-level trust mechanism linking repeated shutdowns to the adoption of unrelated future products.
- Distinguished commitment costs across consumers, enterprises, developers, and hardware partners.
- Identified investment size and cross-company scope as insufficient lifecycle guarantees on their own.
