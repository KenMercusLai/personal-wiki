## Definition
[[AugmentedIntelligence]] is the design of data- and algorithm-driven systems to extend human capability through continuing collaboration among people, data, and algorithms rather than treating automation primarily as a separate replacement for people.

## Current Synthesis
The IDEO-Datascope framing joins a normative goal with an iterative operating model. The goal is enhancement: technology should help people perceive, decide, create, or act more capably. The operating model observes human interaction, feeds what is learned back into data and algorithm choices, and repeats. This makes data collection, objectives, interfaces, and feedback channels part of one designed system. The McKinsey workplace analysis adds an organizational frame: partial automation can increase expert capacity when routine or analytical activities move to machines and people shift toward exceptions, advice, client interaction, creativity, or emotional understanding.

O'Reilly extends augmentation beyond workplace task allocation into the architecture of organizations and interfaces. Platform and logistics businesses are already hybrids in which algorithms guide people while people supply judgment and action. Neural interfaces suggest that a system might add controllable capabilities rather than merely automate an existing movement, while performance-adjacent learning moves instruction into the moment of work and uses prior coursework to build enough structural literacy for on-demand guidance.

The term does not guarantee that people retain meaningful authority or that the resulting system is fair. A loop can still privilege easily measured behavior, exclude affected groups, automate an unjust objective, intensify coordination and surveillance, or present recommendations that are difficult to question. Augmentation therefore requires explicit decisions about whose capability is extended, who allocates opportunity, who bears errors, where override and appeal exist, and how evidence changes the system.

## Key Claims
- Augmentation treats AI as a means of extending human capability rather than an autonomous substitute by default.
- Human interaction is an ongoing source of system learning, not merely an initial requirements input.
- Data selection, collection, objectives, algorithm construction, and interface behavior are connected design choices.
- Effective augmentation requires designers and data scientists to share responsibility for technical behavior and human outcomes.
- Human participation alone does not prevent bias; representation, authority, feedback quality, and correction mechanisms determine whether the loop helps.
- Augmentation depends on job and process redesign; automating isolated activities does not by itself create higher-value human work.
- Augmentation can operate through organizational coordination, neural interfaces, and guidance embedded directly in a task, not only through decision-support software.

Capability extension:

Iterative collaboration:
- [[exclusive-ideos-plan-to-stage-an-ai-revolution]] quotes [[DeanMalmgren]] describing a constant loop in which human interaction informs data and algorithm design.

Cross-disciplinary design:
- [[exclusive-ideos-plan-to-stage-an-ai-revolution]] presents [[IDEO]] and [[Datascope]] as combining designers' attention to human needs with data scientists' ability to build adaptive systems.

Work redesign:
- [[four-fundamentals-of-workplace-automation-mckinsey]] describes mortgage, medical, legal, and sales cases where automating selected activities could increase capacity and shift people toward exceptions, advice, review, and customer interaction.

Expanded interfaces and coordination:
- [[gradually-then-suddenly-oreilly]] describes algorithmically coordinated companies as human-machine hybrids, a CTRL-labs demonstration in which learned nerve signals control virtual action, and ride-hailing guidance as performance-adjacent learning in the flow of work.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is grounded here in a 2017 acquisition article, a preliminary 2015 labor-market model, and a broad 2019 forecast, not comparative evidence that augmented systems outperform automation or unaided human judgment. The McKinsey and O'Reilly examples describe design possibilities and demonstrations rather than measured transitions into more meaningful work or durable new capability. “Human in the loop” can be rhetorical if people cannot understand, contest, override, or reshape consequential outputs. Human feedback can itself be biased or unrepresentative, and capability gains for one group can transfer displacement, cost, surveillance, coordination pressure, or risk to another.

## What Changed
- Expanded augmentation from decision support and work redesign to organizational coordination, neural interfaces, and task-adjacent guidance.
- Added allocation power, surveillance, and coordination pressure to the risks that a human-machine partnership can conceal.
