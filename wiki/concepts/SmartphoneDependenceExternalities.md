## Definition
[[SmartphoneDependenceExternalities]] are the costs imposed when organizations move ordinary service interactions onto QR codes, apps, mobile web pages, or other workflows that presume every customer carries a compatible connected smartphone.

## Current Synthesis
The source exposes this dependence through deliberate non-use. On the first day of a dumbphone trial, the author could no longer scan parking codes, use app-like restaurant ordering, or look up IKEA warehouse locations while in the store. These are small examples, but they reveal a design transfer: the organization can reduce signs, staff interaction, kiosks, or printed guidance by making the customer's device perform discovery and transaction work.

The resulting friction is not evidence that every mobile workflow is harmful. Smartphone interfaces can improve speed, translation, accessibility, personalization, and real-time updating. The externality appears when mobile access becomes the only practical path and the service provides no legible alternative for people without a suitable device, data plan, battery, account, technical confidence, or willingness to remain constantly connected.

## Key Claims
- QR-, app-, and mobile-web-first service design transfers interface, connectivity, and device requirements to customers.
- Deliberate smartphone non-use reveals dependencies that remain invisible to routinely connected users.
- A mobile workflow becomes exclusionary when no practical staffed, physical, or device-independent alternative exists.
- Smartphone convenience and organizational efficiency must be weighed against accessibility, resilience, privacy, and user choice.

## Evidence
Observed service friction:
- [[im-switching-to-a-dumbphone-this-summer]] anticipates difficulty with QR-based parking and restaurants without waitstaff, then reports being unable to look up IKEA warehouse locations in store.

Device boundary:
- [[im-switching-to-a-dumbphone-this-summer]] retains calls, SMS, offline maps, and hotspot access on a feature phone, showing that the gap concerns smartphone-specific interfaces rather than total absence of communication or connectivity.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is a single author's early anecdote and does not establish how often alternatives were actually unavailable, whether staff could help, or how the relevant services were designed. The source does not measure affected populations, transaction failure, accessibility outcomes, privacy cost, staffing savings, or customer preference. Smartphone workflows can themselves be important accessibility aids and can reduce queues or provide current information; the stronger design test is whether users have a workable alternative, not whether a mobile option exists.

## What Changed
- Created the concept from concrete parking, restaurant, and retail-navigation failures encountered during deliberate smartphone non-use.
- Distinguished optional mobile convenience from service dependence without a practical alternative.
