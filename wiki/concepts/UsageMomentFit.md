## Definition
[[UsageMomentFit]] is the alignment between a product and a recurring state, routine, or transition that makes the product salient and useful at a specific moment of choice.

## Current Synthesis
This lens adds time and context to product-market reasoning. A product can solve a real problem yet remain marginal if users do not encounter a recognizable situation in which opening it feels natural. Product planning should therefore name the state, its frequency, the trigger that makes the need salient, the time and attention available, and the alternatives already occupying that moment.

The strongest cases attach to behavior that already exists. Exercise can cue tracking, movement can cue a location-based game, and a need to travel can cue ride-hailing. By contrast, “idling” is broad and frequent but crowded: smartphones let social, media, game, and utility products compete for the same unpredictable spare moments. A narrow initial use case can still reveal a large opportunity when it is a beachhead into a widespread state, but state fit does not by itself prove retention, defensibility, welfare, or sustainable economics.

## Key Claims
- Product opportunity depends on when use occurs as well as who the user is and what problem is solved.
- Existing routines can supply reliable internal or situational triggers without requiring constant notification or promotion.
- State frequency, duration, urgency, and incumbent competition shape the realistic attention available to a product.
- Broad but weakly defined states such as idling can be larger and harder to win than narrower, strongly cued states such as exercise or transportation.
- A narrow entry product can address a much larger market when its initial pain point sits inside a widespread recurring state.
- Routine fit is a discovery hypothesis to test, not evidence that a product owns a state or will build a durable business.

## Evidence
- State taxonomy: [[question-exactly-when-is-someone-going-to-use-your-app-service]] lists daily states including awakening, orienting, eating, transiting, idling, exercising, observing, working, procuring, reflecting, returning, and sleeping; its retained diagram adds examples for several states.
- Existing triggers: [[question-exactly-when-is-someone-going-to-use-your-app-service]] uses exercise apps, [[PokemonGo]], and [[Uber]] to argue that established activity can bring a relevant product to mind.
- Competitive intensity: [[question-exactly-when-is-someone-going-to-use-your-app-service]] describes mobile idling as an unpredictable, heavily contested state dominated by incumbent social and media products.
- Market expansion: [[question-exactly-when-is-someone-going-to-use-your-app-service]] argues that Uber's premium San Francisco pain point understated the breadth of recurring point-to-point transportation.
- Interruption bid: [[question-exactly-when-is-someone-going-to-use-your-app-service]] presents [[ESPN]]'s live alerts as a bid to make sports updates the notification worth receiving.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept currently rests on one short 2016 practitioner essay and selected success stories. It does not compare products with and without state fit, measure state frequency or attention, distinguish causal routine fit from brand, distribution, subsidy, network, content, or execution effects, or examine failed products attached to the same states. People can use multiple products within one state, change routines, ignore prompts, or adopt products for episodic high-value events. Designing for a moment can also become manipulative when a company tries to manufacture compulsive idling or interruption rather than serve an existing user goal.

## What Changed
- Established temporal and routine alignment as a distinct product-discovery lens.
- Added state frequency, trigger strength, duration, and incumbent competition as evaluation dimensions.
- Distinguished strongly cued existing activities from the broad but crowded mobile-idling state.
- Added the boundary that routine fit does not prove causality, retention, defensibility, welfare, or sustainable economics.
