## Definition
[[PlatformNeutrality]] is the strategic choice to avoid competing in an adjacent layer when doing so would reduce the willingness of other companies in that layer to distribute, integrate, or support the core product.

## Current Synthesis
The Netflix case shows neutrality as an ecosystem tradeoff rather than a general preference against hardware. [[ProjectGriffin]] could have given [[Netflix]] direct control of a television experience, but [[ReedHastings]] concluded that a Netflix-branded player would turn prospective partners such as device and television makers into competitors. Spinning the team out as [[Roku]] sacrificed first-party control while preserving the hardware effort and making Netflix easier to place across many competing devices. [[NeilHunt]] adds participant corroboration: concurrent work with Xbox demonstrated the opportunity, and separating the player business made subsequent work with PlayStation, LG, Samsung, and other manufacturers easier.

Neutrality therefore matters when the core service gains more from broad complementary distribution than from owning one endpoint. The choice is not costless: the company gives up hardware margin, differentiation, data, bargaining power, and control, and it may become dependent on partners. The Griffin account supports the partner-conflict mechanism but does not prove that neutrality is always superior to vertical integration.

## Key Claims
- Entering an adjacent product layer can reduce cooperation from firms that become direct competitors.
- Neutrality can expand addressable distribution when a service benefits from appearing across many rival devices or platforms.
- Organizational separation can preserve an adjacent product effort while reducing strategic conflict inside the core company.
- Sunk development cost should not determine whether a project remains strategically compatible with the desired ecosystem position.
- Neutrality trades away first-party control and economics, so its value depends on service leverage, partner incentives, and the availability of credible integrations.

## Evidence
- Partner conflict: [[inside-netflixs-project-griffin-the-forgotten-history-of-roku-under]] says Hastings feared that owning a player would prevent conversations with Apple and create competition with Sony, LG, and Samsung.
- Broad distribution objective: [[inside-netflixs-project-griffin-the-forgotten-history-of-roku-under]] attributes Netflix's later presence across computers, phones, televisions, set-top boxes, and consoles partly to remaining hardware-agnostic.
- Separation mechanism: [[inside-netflixs-project-griffin-the-forgotten-history-of-roku-under]] reports that the near-launch Griffin team was spun out as Roku rather than simply dissolved.
- Decision under momentum: [[inside-netflixs-project-griffin-the-forgotten-history-of-roku-under]] documents validation, manufacturing, pricing, marketing, and advertising work already underway when Netflix reversed course.
- Cross-device corroboration: [[neil-hunt-on-netflix-and-the-story-of-netflix-streaming-internet-history-podcast]] connects the Roku separation with Netflix's desire to work across Xbox, PlayStation, LG, Samsung, and other devices.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence comes from two retrospectives about one successful company and benefits from hindsight after Netflix and Roku both became recognizable businesses. Hunt's account corroborates the mechanism but not through contemporaneous partner evidence. The sources do not quantify partner willingness, compare the economics of neutral distribution with a vertically integrated Netflix device, or show that Apple, Sony, LG, Samsung, or other firms would actually have refused integration. Vertical integration can instead improve quality, differentiation, security, data, bargaining power, or pace when partner incentives are weak. Platform neutrality is therefore conditional, not a rule against owning complementary products.
