## Definition
[[MarketplaceColdStart]] is the coordination problem in which a marketplace cannot satisfy demand without enough relevant supply, while suppliers will not participate without enough demand.

## Current Synthesis
The Uber retrospective shows why the problem can be both a valid investment risk and an incomplete reason to reject a marketplace. Sparse riders make drivers wait without fares, while sparse drivers make riders abandon the service; expanding across jurisdictions multiplies that local density problem. Uber's response, as described by Greathouse, was to constrain the initial market: launch in dense, technology-friendly San Francisco, begin with underused town cars, target affluent smartphone owners who tolerated premium pricing, and establish one usable local exchange before broadening toward taxis and other cities. A narrow wedge can therefore create liquidity, but it can also cause observers to mistake the entry mechanism for the eventual market boundary.

## Key Claims
- Two-sided marketplaces must create sufficient supply and demand in the same place, time, and use case.
- Geographic businesses face repeated local cold starts rather than one global launch problem.
- Dense geography and an acute incumbent-service failure can reduce the participation threshold.
- Constraining initial supply and customer segments can make early matching tractable.
- A premium or narrow beachhead can conceal a much larger adjacent market from investors and competitors.
- Liquidity success does not by itself prove favorable unit economics, regulatory durability, or defensibility.

## Evidence
- Coordination risk: [[confessions-of-a-vc-crap-why-i-passed-on-ubers-seed-round-john-greathouse]] describes the feared loop in which too few drivers repel riders and too few riders leave drivers idle.
- Geographic concentration: [[confessions-of-a-vc-crap-why-i-passed-on-ubers-seed-round-john-greathouse]] says Uber began in San Francisco, whose dense center, technology adoption, and poor taxi service made initial matching more plausible.
- Segment constraint: [[confessions-of-a-vc-crap-why-i-passed-on-ubers-seed-round-john-greathouse]] says the company initially matched underused limo-style town cars with affluent, smartphone-equipped, price-insensitive riders.
- Expansion ambiguity: [[confessions-of-a-vc-crap-why-i-passed-on-ubers-seed-round-john-greathouse]] says this town-car positioning led Greathouse to miss the possibility that Uber could later cannibalize the broader taxi market.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a retrospective account centered on a famous winner and does not provide cohort, wait-time, utilization, subsidy, acquisition-cost, retention, or city-launch data. A narrow beachhead may remain narrow, and dense early liquidity can depend on unsustainable incentives. Regulation, trust, safety, service quality, multi-homing, and [[SubsidizedUnitEconomics]] can determine whether a marketplace remains viable after the cold start is solved.

## What Changed
- Established the concept from Uber's driver-rider coordination risk and concentrated entry strategy.
- Distinguished an initial marketplace wedge from the eventual addressable market.
