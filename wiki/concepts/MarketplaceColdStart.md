## Definition
[[MarketplaceColdStart]] is the coordination problem in which a marketplace cannot satisfy demand without enough relevant supply, while suppliers will not participate without enough demand.

## Current Synthesis
The Uber retrospective shows why the problem can be both a valid investment risk and an incomplete reason to reject a marketplace. Sparse riders make drivers wait without fares, while sparse drivers make riders abandon the service; expanding across jurisdictions multiplies that local density problem. Uber's response, as described by Greathouse, was to constrain the initial market: launch in dense, technology-friendly San Francisco, begin with underused town cars, target affluent smartphone owners who tolerated premium pricing, and establish one usable local exchange before broadening toward taxis and other cities. A narrow wedge can therefore create liquidity, but it can also cause observers to mistake the entry mechanism for the eventual market boundary.

Chen's operator account clarifies what happens after the first local threshold. Each city or sub-city area remains its own rider-driver market, and denser participation is proposed to improve pickup time, coverage, and driver utilization. [[MarketplaceColdStart]] therefore describes reaching minimum viability; [[MarketplaceLiquidity]] describes the continuing match-quality and utilization loop. Expansion does not inherit liquidity automatically from global brand or aggregate users, although product, capital, reputation, and operating knowledge may make repeated launches easier.

## Key Claims
- Two-sided marketplaces must create sufficient supply and demand in the same place, time, and use case.
- Geographic businesses face repeated city- and sub-city cold starts rather than one global launch problem, even when the platform already has aggregate scale.
- Dense geography and an acute incumbent-service failure can reduce the participation threshold.
- Constraining initial supply and customer segments can make early matching tractable.
- A premium or narrow beachhead can conceal a much larger adjacent market from investors and competitors.
- Liquidity success does not by itself prove favorable unit economics, regulatory durability, or defensibility.

## Evidence
- Coordination risk: [[confessions-of-a-vc-crap-why-i-passed-on-ubers-seed-round-john-greathouse]] describes the feared loop in which too few drivers repel riders and too few riders leave drivers idle.
- Geographic concentration: [[confessions-of-a-vc-crap-why-i-passed-on-ubers-seed-round-john-greathouse]] says Uber began in San Francisco, whose dense center, technology adoption, and poor taxi service made initial matching more plausible.
- Segment constraint: [[confessions-of-a-vc-crap-why-i-passed-on-ubers-seed-round-john-greathouse]] says the company initially matched underused limo-style town cars with affluent, smartphone-equipped, price-insensitive riders.
- Expansion ambiguity: [[confessions-of-a-vc-crap-why-i-passed-on-ubers-seed-round-john-greathouse]] says this town-car positioning led Greathouse to miss the possibility that Uber could later cannibalize the broader taxi market.
- Repeated local markets: [[ubers-virtuous-cycle-geographic-density-hyperlocal-marketplaces-and-why-drivers-are-key-at-andrewchen]] describes Uber as hundreds of hyperlocal two-sided markets rather than one globally liquid exchange.
- Post-threshold reinforcement: [[ubers-virtuous-cycle-geographic-density-hyperlocal-marketplaces-and-why-drivers-are-key-at-andrewchen]] says greater local participation can shorten pickups, expand coverage, and increase paid driver utilization.
- Supply fragility: [[ubers-virtuous-cycle-geographic-density-hyperlocal-marketplaces-and-why-drivers-are-key-at-andrewchen]] reports that only about 40% of drivers remained active one year after their first trip, making supply renewal part of maintaining local viability.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are retrospective or first-party accounts centered on a famous winner and do not provide city-launch cohorts, wait-time series, coverage measures, utilization distributions, acquisition costs, causal pricing evidence, or independently verified retention and earnings data. A narrow beachhead may remain narrow, and dense early liquidity can depend on unsustainable incentives. Brand, product reuse, capital, and operating knowledge may also reduce the difficulty of later launches, so “repeated cold start” does not mean each geography begins under identical conditions. Regulation, trust, safety, service quality, multi-homing, driver churn, and [[SubsidizedUnitEconomics]] can determine whether a marketplace remains viable after the cold start is solved.

## What Changed
- Established the concept from Uber's driver-rider coordination risk and concentrated entry strategy.
- Distinguished an initial marketplace wedge from the eventual addressable market.
- Separated minimum viable participation from the post-threshold [[MarketplaceLiquidity]] flywheel.
- Added city- and sub-city locality plus driver churn as reasons cold-start work can recur during expansion.
