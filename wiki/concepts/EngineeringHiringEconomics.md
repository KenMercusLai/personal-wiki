## Definition
[[EngineeringHiringEconomics]] is the analysis of the money and productive time required to source, evaluate, onboard, and replace software engineers, together with the savings that may come from referrals and effective retention.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats hiring cost as more than a recruiter invoice. Its model adds candidate sourcing, internal recruiting coordination, engineering interview time, ramp-up before full productivity, and signing, relocation, or similar package costs. In the worked example, those terms total roughly $50,000 for an engineer earning $125,000, with recruiting and six weeks of ramp-up dominating the arithmetic. The retained infographic makes the intended magnitude concrete by comparing the estimate to a $50,675 Ford F-150.

This decomposition supports decisions better than the headline alone. Recruiter percentage fees make higher salaries increase acquisition cost; referrals can remove much of that term; interview loops consume existing engineering capacity; and departures may add knowledge-transfer and replacement costs. The same model can test retention proposals by comparing their cost with the expected value of avoided departures.

The defensible conclusion is local calculation, not a universal $50,000 constant. Each organization needs its own salary basis, sourcing mix, interview hours, fully loaded labor rates, ramp definition, attrition baseline, causal evidence for retention interventions, and uncertainty range. Quality and morale claims also need separate evidence: cost arithmetic cannot prove that a candidate is stronger or that a workplace improvement will prevent departures.

## Key Claims
- Hiring cost should include internal labor and delayed productivity, not only cash paid to recruiters or candidates.
- Recruiting fees and ramp-up can dominate the total, making sourcing route and time-to-productivity important economic variables.
- Referrals may lower acquisition cost by bypassing external recruiting, but they do not remove the need for job-relevant evaluation or access controls.
- Replacement economics strengthen the case for retention investment when an intervention credibly reduces avoidable attrition.
- A decomposed local estimate with ranges is more decision-useful than treating one article's $50,000 result as an industry benchmark.

## Evidence
- Cost decomposition: [[it-costs-50k-to-hire-a-software-engineer-noteworthy-the-journal-blog]] models hiring as recruiting plus engineering interview time, ramp-up, and package costs.
- Worked magnitude: [[it-costs-50k-to-hire-a-software-engineer-noteworthy-the-journal-blog]] estimates $25,000 for an external recruiter, $3,000 for internal recruiting, $2,000 for engineering interviews, and $20,000 for ramp-up.
- Sourcing leverage: [[it-costs-50k-to-hire-a-software-engineer-noteworthy-the-journal-blog]] argues that a $3,000–$5,000 referral bonus can be cheaper than a recruiter percentage fee.
- Retention option value: [[it-costs-50k-to-hire-a-software-engineer-noteworthy-the-journal-blog]] compares a hypothetical $20,000 workplace investment with more than $100,000 in avoided replacement cost if attrition falls by two percentage points across 100 engineers.
- Uncertainty boundary: [[it-costs-50k-to-hire-a-software-engineer-noteworthy-the-journal-blog]] calls $50,000 a wild estimate and says real results can be much higher or lower.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source provides illustrative arithmetic, not company records, a representative sample, or a causal study. Its 2018 salary, recruiter-fee, signing-bonus, relocation, and office assumptions are context-bound. Ramp-up is difficult to define because new hires contribute while learning and mentors may gain as well as lose time; institutional knowledge is valuable but not directly priced here. Fully loaded labor cost, vacancy cost, failed hires, equipment, background checks, candidate travel, management time, and opportunity cost may be omitted or double-counted depending on the model.

The claim that weaker candidates save little assumes candidate quality is observable and predicts ramp time and output. Referral-heavy sourcing can narrow access or reproduce network homogeneity. The office-improvement example assumes rather than demonstrates a causal retention effect, and the broad claim that respect matters more than pay should remain a hypothesis alongside compensation, workload, growth, management, fairness, and outside options.

## What Changed
- Created a decomposed model of engineering acquisition, onboarding, replacement, and retention costs.
- Separated the reusable calculation method from the source's illustrative $50,000 headline.
- Made causal evidence and uncertainty ranges explicit requirements for retention ROI claims.
