## Definition
[[AutomaticAdvertisingInfluence]] is the proposed shaping of brand evaluation or purchase preference through associations and identity meanings that do not require sustained conscious attention or deliberate acceptance of a factual sales message.

## Current Synthesis
The source separates a layered path from possible exposure to engagement: a brand or advertisement can be nearby, briefly perceived, consciously interpreted, or strong enough to motivate further action. Selective attention removes much of the surrounding field from awareness, but the essay argues that conscious screening is not complete psychological immunity. Automatic categorization attaches positive or negative valence to stimuli; evaluative conditioning attempts to transfer that valence by pairing a brand with attractive people, celebrities, animals, landscapes, or other emotionally loaded cues; and cultural imprinting gives products identity, status, and aspirational meaning.

This model usefully distinguishes informational persuasion from associative influence and distinguishes attention capture from later evaluation or behavior. Its evidentiary scope must remain narrow. The described conditioning experiments support resistance to paired cues in controlled tasks, while the source's claims about daily exposure, market behavior, total spending, consumerism, anxiety, and environmental harm require additional measurement. Automatic influence is therefore a plausible mechanism and a public-interest question, not proof that every unnoticed ad changes a purchase or that advertising has uniform effects.

## Key Claims
- Advertising exposure, perception, awareness, and engagement are different stages rather than interchangeable measures.
- Conscious inattention can reduce message processing without proving that every associative effect has been blocked.
- Evaluative conditioning attempts to transfer emotional valence from a paired cue to a brand, face, logo, or product.
- Resistance under cognitive load, counter-instructions, or incentives is evidence for partial automaticity in the described experiments.
- Cultural imprinting makes products inputs to self-concept, status, aspiration, and anticipated social judgment.
- Informational advertising can improve choice, so automatic influence is not a claim that all advertising is deceptive or socially harmful.
- Public-policy concern depends on prevalence, involuntary exposure, behavioral magnitude, and downstream harm, not merely on the existence of a laboratory effect.

## Evidence
Exposure and attention:
- [[ads-just-work-no-matter-what-you-think-noteworthy-the-journal-blog]] separates brand proximity, ad proximity, perception, conscious awareness, and motivated engagement, while acknowledging that its approximate counts lack documented methodology.

Evaluative conditioning:
- [[ads-just-work-no-matter-what-you-think-noteworthy-the-journal-blog]] describes six experiments pairing neutral faces or logos with pleasant or unpleasant cues and reports persistent opinion shifts under memory load, instructions to resist, and a financial incentive.

Identity meaning:
- [[ads-just-work-no-matter-what-you-think-noteworthy-the-journal-blog]] uses watches, wine, handbags, rings, perfume, and sunglasses to argue that products can express an ideal self and desired social image.

Public-interest claim:
- [[ads-just-work-no-matter-what-you-think-noteworthy-the-journal-blog]] connects hard-to-avoid advertising with increased spending, superficial needs, anxiety, competition, consumption, and waste, but does not causally estimate those outcomes.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a popular essay rather than a systematic review and omits a complete bibliography, effect sizes, sample details, replication status, and real-world outcome measures. Its staged daily exposure counts are approximate and unsupported in the article. Laboratory attitude effects do not establish durable brand preference, purchase, aggregate spending, or social harm, and selective attention can sometimes make conspicuous advertising easier to identify and ignore. Consumers also differ by goals, familiarity, context, culture, and prior preference. The informational role of advertising, competing messages, negative reactions, and deliberate comparison all limit a simple stimulus-to-purchase model.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to separate automatic associative influence from conscious message processing and from the economics of ad delivery.
- Preserved the boundary between controlled conditioning effects and unmeasured real-world purchasing or social outcomes.
