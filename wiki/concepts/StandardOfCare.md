## Definition
[[StandardOfCare]] is the professional benchmark for what competent practitioners are expected to do in a given clinical situation, shaped partly by what the clinical community treats as ordinary practice.

## Current Synthesis
The source uses standard of care to show how individual technology habits can become institutional obligations. [[PaulAppelbaum]] argues that if enough mental-health clinicians use online search to assess patient risk, the practice could shift from optional collateral gathering toward an expected part of care. That creates a liability trap: clinicians may be criticized for intruding when they search, but may later face malpractice claims if they fail to find online warning signs that peers increasingly would have checked.

## Key Claims
- Standards of care are partly produced by the clinical community's own repeated practice.
- Patient-targeted online search could become an expected risk-assessment behavior if enough clinicians use it.
- A new standard can create malpractice exposure when clinicians miss preventable online warning signs.
- Standard-of-care drift can occur before consent, documentation, and privacy norms are settled.
- Professional groups therefore need explicit guidance before informal technology habits harden into obligations.

## Evidence
- Community-practice mechanism: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] quotes [[PaulAppelbaum]] saying that what a substantial number of clinicians do can become a standard of care.
- Online search example: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] says patient-targeted Googling may shift the clinical standards to which practitioners are held.
- Malpractice risk: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] raises the possibility that clinicians could be sued after missing a suicidal Facebook message.
- Governance need: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] quotes Appelbaum saying the outcome depends on how clinical professions choose to use the Internet's new abilities.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source frames a possible legal trajectory rather than proving that patient-targeted search has already become a binding legal standard. It does not survey malpractice cases, specialty guidelines, or jurisdiction-specific law. The concept should therefore be read as a risk of professional-practice drift, not a settled doctrine for online patient search.
