## Definition
[[EverydayEconomy]] is [[NextViewVentures]]’s investment framework for technology-enabled redesign of the products, services, and operating systems behind home, transportation, food, work and money, health, apparel, and entertainment - categories that occupy recurring spending and daily experience.

## Current Synthesis
The thesis starts from a platform-timing claim: broadband, search, smartphones, cloud computing, social networks, and real-time location have become a [[TechnologyEnablerStack]] on which applications can reorganize ordinary life. It therefore looks beyond digitizing discovery or checkout toward changing how a meal is prepared and obtained, how mobility is supplied, how a home is maintained, or how work is coordinated. The opportunity is not identical to B2C, because restaurant software and other operator infrastructure can alter the consumer experience indirectly. NextView converts this broad idea into three screens: seek a step-function redesign rather than a linear improvement; prefer products that are used habitually or remain present in everyday life; and require an eventual path to mass consumers or large populations of business users, while allowing a narrow initial market.

## Key Claims
- The thesis covers seven large domains: home, transportation, food, work and money, health, apparel, and entertainment.
- Digital transformation matters when it changes the delivered product, service, or experience, not only the marketing and purchasing funnel.
- B2B enabling systems belong inside the thesis when they materially reshape what end customers experience.
- A qualifying startup should aim for a step-function redesign rather than incremental convenience alone.
- “Everyday” can mean frequent use or persistent lived presence, so an infrequent purchase may qualify when the resulting product is encountered daily.
- Mass relevance is an eventual ambition rather than a requirement that the seed-stage entry market already be broad.

## Evidence
Scope and economic framing:
- [[introducing-nextview-iii-and-our-focus-on-the-everyday-economy-nextview-ventures]] names the seven categories, cites personal consumption at about 70% of U.S. GDP, and claims each category represents more than $1 trillion.
- The retained spending chart in [[introducing-nextview-iii-and-our-focus-on-the-everyday-economy-nextview-ventures]] partitions nearly all category spending and maps portfolio examples across all seven domains.

Experience redesign:
- [[introducing-nextview-iii-and-our-focus-on-the-everyday-economy-nextview-ventures]] contrasts marketing-funnel simplification with changes to meals, lodging, work, mobility, and car ownership.
- [[introducing-nextview-iii-and-our-focus-on-the-everyday-economy-nextview-ventures]] uses [[Uber]] and [[Lyft]] to show how smartphones and location services can reorganize a service without creating a new base computing platform.

Business-model boundary:
- [[introducing-nextview-iii-and-our-focus-on-the-everyday-economy-nextview-ventures]] uses restaurant POS, payment, CRM, loyalty, and analytics software as a B2B system that indirectly changes dining.

Investment screens:
- [[introducing-nextview-iii-and-our-focus-on-the-everyday-economy-nextview-ventures]] explicitly screens for redesign, habitual or continuously encountered value, and relevance to mass consumers or broad business-user populations.
- [[introducing-nextview-iii-and-our-focus-on-the-everyday-economy-nextview-ventures]] cites [[Slack]] and [[Dropbox]] as daily-use business products, and home goods or renovation as less-frequent purchases whose results remain part of daily life.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This is a 2017 venture-firm thesis and fund announcement, not a neutral market taxonomy or an outcome study. The seven categories are broad enough to include much of consumer activity, so the stated screens do more analytical work than the category list itself. The claim that every category exceeds $1 trillion is not broken into comparable market definitions, and the spending chart shows approximate shares rather than dollar values, growth, addressability, or startup-capturable revenue. The “supertechnology” adoption curve is conceptual and has no units or observed series. Portfolio examples create an incentive to present the framework favorably, while no exits, returns, customer outcomes, counterexamples, or later fund performance test whether the thesis selected superior investments.

## What Changed
- Created the concept as a bounded investment framework spanning consumer experiences and the B2B systems that enable them.
- Separated category scope from the stronger selection criteria of redesign, lived frequency, and eventual mass relevance.
- Preserved the source’s allowance for narrow initial markets and infrequent purchases with persistent daily effects.
