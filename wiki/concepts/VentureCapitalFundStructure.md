## Definition
[[VentureCapitalFundStructure]] is the arrangement in which a venture firm invests capital pooled from outside limited partners through a fund with a finite life, earns returns for those partners from startup exits, and keeps a share of the upside.

## Current Synthesis
TechCrunch's one-paragraph sketch of venture economics explains why a VC behaves differently from an individual investor. A VC manages a fund, and the money in that fund belongs to LPs: financial institutions, high-net-worth individuals, pension funds, university funds, and other sources. Many venture firms run on a ten-year fund life and are expected to deliver returns by the end of it. Those returns come from exits such as IPOs and M&A, and the firm keeps a portion of what it returns.

The structure carries an explicit tolerance for failure. It is expected that most portfolio startups will fail, and that the best few will return enough to cover every loss and still leave a profit. That expectation is what makes the asset class coherent: the fund is not trying to be repaid like a lender, it is buying a share of a small number of outsized outcomes, which is why [[VentureCapitalPortfolioSizing]] treats exposure to enough companies as a structural question rather than a matter of taste.

## Key Claims
- Venture capitalists invest a fund's capital rather than their own personal money, which is the defining difference from [[AngelInvesting]].
- Fund capital comes from LPs including financial institutions, high-net-worth individuals, pension funds, university funds, and other institutional or individual sources.
- Venture funds commonly run on a ten-year life cycle and are expected to produce returns by the end of that period.
- Fund returns are realized through exits such as IPOs and acquisitions rather than through interest or repayment.
- The venture firm keeps a portion of the returns it generates for its LPs.
- The model assumes that most portfolio companies fail and that the best performers must cover the fund's losses and produce a profit on top.

## Evidence
- Pooled capital: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says angels invest their own personal money while VCs manage a fund.
- LP composition: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] lists financial institutions, high-net-worth individuals, pension funds, university funds, and other sources as the "LPs" behind a fund.
- Finite fund life: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says many venture firms have a ten-year fund life cycle and are expected to provide returns at the end of that period.
- Exit-driven returns: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says fund returns are earned through exits such as IPOs and M&A.
- Carried interest: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says the VC firm keeps a portion of the returns.
- Failure budget: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says most startups are expected to fail and that the best ones must provide enough returns to cover every loss and more.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source compresses venture fund economics into a few sentences and omits the mechanics that dominate real funds: management fees, carried-interest rates and hurdles, fund vintages and the J-curve, investment periods versus fund life, capital calls, reserves and follow-on policy, DPI and TVPI reporting, and the secondary market for fund interests. It presents the ten-year life as typical without saying that extension options, evergreen vehicles, and special-purpose vehicles are common alternatives. It also does not address what LPs demand beyond returns, such as access, reporting, or portfolio-construction discipline, and it gives no data on realized fund performance.
