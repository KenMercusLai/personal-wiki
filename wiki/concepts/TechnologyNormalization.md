## Definition
[[TechnologyNormalization]] is the process by which a once-novel technical capability spreads through tools, education, and practice until users, builders, and investors treat it as an expected baseline rather than a distinguishing category.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames AI as another foundational computing technique moving from visible novelty toward invisible expectation. Its proposed path starts with enthusiasts and research papers, expands through conferences and commercial education, and ends when mainstream development tools and standard curricula embody the technique. At that point, merely claiming to use the technology should convey little strategic advantage; differentiation moves toward the problem solved, the customer outcome, and the business built around it.

This is a useful directional model but a poor short-horizon clock. The essay predicted in 2017 that explicit investor demand for AI startups would disappear within two years, while later wiki sources still found AI prominent in product marketing in 2019 and investment framing in 2025. A capability can therefore become operationally common while remaining commercially salient, unevenly distributed, or attached to a continuing platform shift.

## Key Claims
- Technical novelty loses signaling power as adoption makes the capability an expected part of competent implementation.
- Adoption can travel from enthusiast experimentation and research through events, training, development tools, and formal curricula.
- Databases, desktop clients, web interfaces, cloud infrastructure, and mobile support illustrate the claimed shift from category label to software baseline.
- Salaries, valuations, and promotional language can become temporarily inflated while a platform shift is still scarce and fashionable.
- When the enabling capability normalizes, strategic distinction must move toward customer value, market position, execution, or another scarce advantage.
- Operational normalization and commercial normalization can occur at different speeds.

## Evidence
- Baseline transition: [[in-a-few-years-no-investors-are-going-to-be-looking-for-ai-startups]] compares AI with earlier capabilities that became assumed parts of important software.
- Diffusion path: [[in-a-few-years-no-investors-are-going-to-be-looking-for-ai-startups]] traces movement from GitHub, Reddit, research papers, and arXiv through conferences, bootcamps, MOOCs, IDEs, and computer-science curricula.
- Temporary scarcity: [[in-a-few-years-no-investors-are-going-to-be-looking-for-ai-startups]] links platform shifts with short-lived froth in specialist salaries, startup valuations, and pitch decks.
- Strategic consequence: [[in-a-few-years-no-investors-are-going-to-be-looking-for-ai-startups]] argues that founders need another basis for differentiation once AI use is assumed.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a short 2017 forecast rather than an empirical adoption study, and its analogies do not establish that AI follows the same diffusion curve as databases, web interfaces, cloud computing, or mobile software. Its two-year forecast proved too aggressive against later wiki evidence: AI labeling remained conspicuous in 2019 product marketing, and AI remained an explicit investment theme in 2025. Normalization can also be uneven by industry, task, company size, capability frontier, regulation, cost, and access to data or talent.

## What Changed
- Created the concept while separating a durable normalization mechanism from the source's unsuccessful two-year timing forecast.
- Distinguished operational ubiquity from continued marketing and investment salience.
