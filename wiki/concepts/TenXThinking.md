## Definition
[[TenXThinking]] is the practice of setting an order-of-magnitude outcome target to expose assumptions, force problem reframing, and search for a mechanism different from local optimization of the current system.

## Current Synthesis
Norton's framework separates the ambition and mechanism of a goal from the steps used to reach it. A 10% target often invites familiar tools, while a 10x target can make the current path visibly inadequate and provoke questions about a different product, architecture, market, cost curve, or behavior. Kodak is the negative case: asking how to defend film preserved existing economics, whereas asking how the world could produce vastly more photographs might have centered digital capture.

The framework still depends on incremental learning. Norton recommends repeated attempts, data, employee voice, openness, mobility, and trend awareness; his ceramics anecdote argues that quality can emerge from making many pieces. The strongest synthesis is therefore not “large leaps instead of small steps,” but radical outcome framing joined to bounded experiments and cumulative execution. Arithmetic expected value, trend extrapolation, or motivational permission alone cannot make an existential bet affordable or identify the right mechanism.

## Key Claims
- Order-of-magnitude targets can reveal that existing assumptions and tools are structurally incapable of reaching the desired outcome.
- Ambition is useful when it changes the problem representation, not merely when it multiplies an effort quota.
- High-upside portfolios require bounded failure, repeated attempts, evidence, and enough survival capacity to learn.
- Impact measures describe the state to achieve; activity counts describe effort and can become misleading substitutes.
- Trend-aware design can make a costly or seemingly extravagant offer viable as enabling costs fall or adoption expands.
- Open information, employee voice, and project mobility widen the search space for unexpected combinations and challenges.

## Evidence
- Incumbent reframing: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] argues that Kodak might have reasoned differently by asking how the world could take one trillion photographs rather than how to protect film.
- Assumption break: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] contrasts incremental mechanical-watch accuracy work with the much more accurate and cheaper quartz watch.
- Portfolio arithmetic: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] compares a 99% chance of $1 million with a 1% chance of $1 billion to show how probability salience can hide higher expected value.
- Learning conditions: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] joins failure tolerance, the ceramics quantity anecdote, data-led testing, openness, and employee mobility.
- Impact framing: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] contrasts the Apollo outcome with counting rocket launches and asks teams to define what will have been accomplished.
- Trend leverage: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] uses internet adoption, falling storage cost, and demographic aging as examples of external trajectories that can support a large bet.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a motivational practitioner essay composed of selected anecdotes, historical summaries, attributed quotations, and 2015 forecasts rather than comparative evidence about goal systems. Extreme goals can encourage fantasy forecasts, unsafe shortcuts, metric gaming, burnout, ruin, or neglect of maintenance and compounding improvements. Expected value does not settle a one-shot decision when variance, timing, liquidity, correlated failure, learning value, or survival matters. Incremental advances can themselves accumulate into structural change, and many apparent breakthroughs depend on long prior sequences. The method is safest when impact is explicit, downside is bounded, experiments are informative, and evidence can change the plan.

## What Changed
- Created the concept as radical outcome framing supported by incremental experimentation rather than as a rejection of all small steps.
- Added survival, variance, and evidence boundaries to the essay's expected-value argument.
