## Definition
[[OpenSourceCommercialization]] is the design of revenue, licensing, and organizational arrangements that fund the continued development of publicly available software without abandoning open community participation.

## Current Synthesis
The MySQL account frames commercialization as a necessary complement to open development rather than its opposite. [[MontyWidenius]] says opening the code let the team give back and invite improvements without assuming that copying would reduce its existing income, while commercial users were expected to buy licenses so [[MySQLAB]] could hire staff and compete with closed-source companies. In that account, technical quality, broad adoption, visible contribution, hiring, and revenue reinforced one another.

The Red Hat account adds a different mechanism: [[BobYoung]] says freely reusable innovation made product exclusivity unavailable, so Red Hat treated boxed Linux as an interim route toward enterprise support and service contracts. The durable proposition is that community development can create adoption, product, and talent advantages, but a company still needs an enforceable and trusted value-capture mechanism suited to what customers actually buy. Licensing and subscriptions are not interchangeable: MySQL's founder narrative emphasizes paid commercial use, while Red Hat emphasizes supported outcomes around open components.

## Key Claims
- Open development and commercial revenue can be complements when revenue funds the staff needed to sustain the product.
- Public source can invite community improvements and expand adoption without necessarily eliminating paid demand.
- Commercial-use licensing was presented as MySQL's mechanism for asking value-capturing enterprises to fund development.
- A viable model needs more than code availability: product quality, customer feedback, community trust, hiring, and organizational execution interact with licensing.
- Licensing descriptions must distinguish founder narrative from actual legal terms, versions, enforcement, and buyer behavior.
- Open-source support and subscriptions can sell accountability, continuity, and enterprise outcomes without making the underlying code exclusive.

## Evidence
- Open motivation: [[founder-stories-a-hackers-hacker-openocean]] quotes Widenius saying the founders wanted to give back and did not expect copying to reduce their current earnings.
- Funding mechanism: [[founder-stories-a-hackers-hacker-openocean]] says enterprises making money with MySQL were expected to purchase a license.
- Organizational purpose: [[founder-stories-a-hackers-hacker-openocean]] connects revenue to hiring staff and competing with closed-source communities.
- Reinforcing system: [[founder-stories-a-hackers-hacker-openocean]] links open contribution to product improvement and visible contributor work to recruiting.
- Commercial outcome: [[founder-stories-a-hackers-hacker-openocean]] reports profitability and MySQL AB's later US$1 billion sale to Sun, without isolating the licensing model's causal contribution.
- Service rationale: [[ibms-old-playbook-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] quotes Young saying Red Hat concluded customers buy services that make them successful, not merely products.
- Business transition: [[ibms-old-playbook-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] says early boxed Linux was intended to create enough scale for enterprise service contracts.
- Portfolio distinction: [[ibms-old-playbook-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] separates Red Hat's RHEL subscription cash flow from OpenShift's strategic platform value.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The MySQL source calls the commercial-use rule an honor system and gives no license text, revenue mix, conversion data, enforcement history, or cost structure. The Red Hat evidence is a founder's retrospective quoted inside a 2018 strategy essay and likewise supplies no customer cohorts, renewal economics, service cost, or causal comparison. Neither source proves openness itself caused the reported value, and neither model should be generalized across licenses, hosted services, foundations, or source-available products without examining the actual rights and buyer need.

## What Changed
- Added Red Hat's support-and-subscription route as distinct from MySQL's reported commercial-use licensing route.
- Reframed commercialization around the customer value being sold rather than code exclusivity alone.
