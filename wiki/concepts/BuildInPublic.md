## Definition
[[BuildInPublic]] is a product-building practice in which creators publicly share progress, decisions, examples, feedback requests, lessons, and business results while the product is being made.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat build-in-public as an operating posture that makes work, participants, and outcomes visible before or during product formation. For a small software creator, it can invite feedback, establish familiarity, and build a reusable launch audience. Zhang Xiaoji applies it across domestic and overseas channels, including Jike, Zhihu, Xiaohongshu, Douyin, X, Reddit, Product Hunt, Indie Hackers, LinkedIn, Medium, and other communities.

[[LoicLeMeur]]'s account of [[ProductHunt]] adds a company-and-community variant. [[RyanHoover]] reportedly shared what the team was building and identified early users, while the platform publicized makers' successes and contributor activity. This suggests that visible participants and outcomes can attract the next cohort. It also establishes an important boundary: public narration does not require unrestricted product access, because Product Hunt reportedly combined transparent sharing with selective submission, voting, and commenting rights.

A bounded product-launch sequence can join public accountability with audience learning. Before releasing [[RefactoringToCollections]], [[AdamWathan]] shared a book landing page, unfinished examples, sample chapters, progress, a launch date, a delay, and free lessons. In his account, public commitments increased his own follow-through, while replies and preorder requests signaled interest and encouraged continued work. The case also separates useful visibility from transparency theater: the shared artifacts taught the same subject as the paid product and led interested people into an owned email list.

Public software experiments can make the work itself both content and market sensing. In [[TonyDinh]]'s portfolio, product-only promotion initially failed to grow his Twitter account; coding experiments, humor, engagement, and concrete demos attracted attention, after which progress posts and releases for [[BlackMagic]], [[Xnapper]], and [[TypingMind]] could reach an established audience. The case also shows that build-in-public is not owned distribution: Twitter supplied compounding reach, but algorithmic attention and API policy remained platform-controlled, and engagement counts do not establish purchases.

## Key Claims
- Public progress updates can attract early users before a formal launch.
- Sharing prototypes and examples can turn audience comments into product feedback.
- Visible building can create trust and founder familiarity when products are otherwise similar.
- Repeated public sharing can become a reusable distribution asset for future products, while remaining exposed to platform control.
- Different products may need different platform tests across domestic and overseas communities.
- Publicly crediting early users and sharing their outcomes can make participation itself part of the product's distribution story.
- Public commitments, working demos, and substantive previews can jointly support creator accountability, audience learning, and pre-launch demand.

## Evidence
- Early users: [[zhang-xiaoji-jian-ru-jia-jing-xie-gang-cheng-xu-yuan-de-shu-zi-you-min-zhuan-xing-zhi-lu]] says interested people can interact during the build process and become first users.
- Feedback: [[zhang-xiaoji-jian-ru-jia-jing-xie-gang-cheng-xu-yuan-de-shu-zi-you-min-zhuan-xing-zhi-lu]] shows prototype, icon, template, and product-progress posts receiving comments and feature suggestions.
- Trust: [[zhang-xiaoji-jian-ru-jia-jing-xie-gang-cheng-xu-yuan-de-shu-zi-you-min-zhuan-xing-zhi-lu]] argues that users may prefer a creator who regularly shares process over an otherwise similar silent product.
- Reusable distribution: [[zhang-xiaoji-jian-ru-jia-jing-xie-gang-cheng-xu-yuan-de-shu-zi-you-min-zhuan-xing-zhi-lu]] says public building accumulates trust and can bring early traffic to the next product.
- Platform testing: [[zhang-xiaoji-jian-ru-jia-jing-xie-gang-cheng-xu-yuan-de-shu-zi-you-min-zhuan-xing-zhi-lu]] compares domestic platforms such as Jike and Zhihu with overseas channels such as X and Reddit.
- Visible formation: [[loic-le-meur-10-lessons-from-product-hunts-success]] says Hoover shared Product Hunt's early work and first users publicly, which Le Meur credits with attracting more participants.
- Participant outcomes: [[loic-le-meur-10-lessons-from-product-hunts-success]] says Product Hunt featured makers' reported launch successes and linked contributor identities to their activity.
- Creator accountability: [[the-61-392-book-launch-that-let-me-quit-my-job]] says Wathan used promises of samples and a public launch date to create pressure to finish.
- Substantive sharing: [[the-61-392-book-launch-that-let-me-quit-my-job]] documents sample chapters, a blog adaptation, a free screencast, progress updates, reader feedback, and preorder interest before launch.
- Audience formation: [[tony-dinh-my-solopreneur-story-zero-to-45kmo-in-2-years]] says product-only tweets drew little interest, while coding experiments, engagement, threads, and humor grew Dinh's audience.
- Product feedback and reusable reach: [[tony-dinh-my-solopreneur-story-zero-to-45kmo-in-2-years]] documents public Black Magic, Xnapper, and Typing Mind demonstrations and reports later launch attention from the accumulated audience.
- Platform boundary: [[tony-dinh-my-solopreneur-story-zero-to-45kmo-in-2-years]] pairs audience-assisted growth with the Twitter API change that made Black Magic's dependency commercially threatening.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources provide screenshots, anecdotes, and retrospective interpretation rather than controlled attribution. Public building may fail when the audience is mismatched, when updates become performative, or when the creator shares progress without enough product value or follow-through. The Product Hunt case also shows that transparency and access are separate choices: selective participation may protect quality but can privilege existing status, hide arbitrary gatekeeping, or limit whose feedback shapes the product. Wathan's case adds a pressure boundary: public dates and promises can motivate, but can also contribute to overwork, shame, or premature scheduling when scope is uncertain. Dinh's follower, view, and like counts show attention rather than causal sales attribution, and his Twitter dependency shows that a public audience is not necessarily portable or owned. The sources do not isolate public sharing from product value, platform momentum, founder networks, existing audience, curation, direct outreach, or launch packaging.

## What Changed
- Added working software demonstrations and interesting public experiments as content that can build an audience before a specific launch.
- Added the distinction between reusable audience reach and owned distribution: platform policy and algorithms remain external dependencies.
- Added attention-versus-sales attribution as an explicit qualification.
