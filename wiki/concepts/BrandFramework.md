## Definition
[[BrandFramework]] is a shared decision vocabulary that connects a durable customer promise with brand personality, attributes, vision, mission, and principles so product, service, communication, and organizational choices express a coherent experience.

## Current Synthesis
[[SarahGibbons]] and [[KateKaplan]] place brand promise at the center: the enduring purpose and value a company commits to delivering. Vision, mission, and principles extend that promise internally by describing the desired future, the route toward it, and the beliefs used to resolve tradeoffs. Personality and its constituent attributes express the promise externally through interaction behavior, visuals, and language, while positioning connects the promise to an audience and set of offerings in a competitive context.

The framework becomes useful when its terms operate at different decision scales. Teams can test an entire omnichannel experience against the promise, use mission and vision to prioritize roadmaps, use principles for everyday tradeoffs, and use three to seven contextualized attributes to critique interface behavior and copy. Shared labels are therefore not the end product; their value depends on specific definitions and consistent application in real decisions.

## Key Claims
- Brand is the enduring customer promise, while branding is the controlled manifestation of that identity.
- Promise is the framework's center and should align both internal direction and external experience.
- Vision states the ideal future or why; mission states the approach or how; principles guide choices along the way.
- Personality is a personified behavioral whole, while attributes are the small set of defined traits composing it.
- Each component works at a different decision scale, from roadmap priorities to interface copy and interaction details.
- A shared vocabulary can reduce fragmentation across specialized teams only when abstract terms are made organization-specific and actionable.

## Evidence
- Framework relationships: [[sarah-gibbons-kate-kaplan-brand-vocabulary-in-the-context-of-ux-key-terms-defined]] defines six components and visually places promise between internal vision, mission, and principles and external personality, attributes, and positioning.
- UX decision scale: [[sarah-gibbons-kate-kaplan-brand-vocabulary-in-the-context-of-ux-key-terms-defined]] connects promise to experience evaluation, attributes to flows and copy, vision and mission to roadmaps, and principles to tradeoffs.
- Operational specificity: [[sarah-gibbons-kate-kaplan-brand-vocabulary-in-the-context-of-ux-key-terms-defined]] shows Mailchimp, Walmart, Spotify, Sesame Street, and Lexus turning abstract terms into behavior, communication, or decision guidance.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This is a normative practitioner taxonomy supported by selected examples, not a controlled study of organizational alignment, UX consistency, customer preference, or financial outcomes. Brand, mission, and vision terminology varies across organizations, so consistency within a team may matter more than adopting these exact labels. Written values can also diverge from deployed products, service behavior, incentives, accessibility needs, and customer interpretation. The article gives no process for resolving conflict among components or changing a promise that evidence no longer supports.

## What Changed
- Created the six-part framework around promise, personality, attributes, vision, mission, and principles.
- Distinguished brand meaning from branding expression and mapped each component to a UX decision scale.
- Preserved the terminology variance and selected-example limits of the source.
