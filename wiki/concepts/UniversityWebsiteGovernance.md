## Definition
[[UniversityWebsiteGovernance]] is the allocation of purpose, authority, staffing, standards, access, measurement, and maintenance responsibility that determines whether a university's web presence works as a coherent public service rather than a collection of competing departmental pages.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats university website quality as an institutional outcome expressed through an interface. Users experience buried schedules, fees, maps, directories, contact details, weak search, obsolete pages, inaccessible or non-mobile layouts, and recruitment-heavy homepages, but the underlying causes include organizational silos, political competition for navigation space, ambiguous ownership, fragmented data access, and web teams too small to maintain the estate. Delegating content to departments does not remove the work; it can increase coordination and support needs while assigning publishing to people without time, skill, access, or incentives.

Good governance therefore begins with purpose and user tasks. The site should help diverse audiences navigate a complex institution without requiring them to know which office owns a service. That requires task-centered information architecture, visible essential links, reliable search, content lifecycles, accessibility and mobile support, feedback channels, measurement, and enough continuing staff capacity to keep information correct. Centralization may improve coherence, but the source does not present it as sufficient: the institution must also resolve competing priorities and fund the web as an operational service rather than a one-time brochure or recruitment campaign.

## Key Claims
- Interface defects often reveal governance defects in ownership, staffing, authority, and institutional priorities.
- Information architecture organized around departments forces users to understand the institution before they can complete ordinary tasks.
- Distributed content maintenance can require more coordination and support, not less, than centralized maintenance.
- Recruitment and prestige goals can crowd out the practical needs of current students, staff, faculty, and public audiences.
- Content accuracy requires continuing ownership, access, review, and retirement practices rather than periodic redesigns.
- Accessibility, mobile compatibility, search, feedback, and measurement belong to the service model, not optional visual polish.
- Coherence requires both adequate web capacity and authority to resist political accumulation on homepages and navigation.

## Evidence
- User-task failures: [[university-websites-the-so-so-the-bad-and-the-egregious-university-affairs]] reports buried schedules, fees, maps, transcript requests, addresses, contacts, directories, jobs, departments, and date information, along with link loops and ineffective search.
- Institutional structure: [[university-websites-the-so-so-the-bad-and-the-egregious-university-affairs]] argues that sites mirror organizational silos and internal judgments of importance rather than users' questions.
- Ownership and maintenance: [[university-websites-the-so-so-the-bad-and-the-egregious-university-affairs]] describes faculty responsible for pages without web expertise, faculty unable to make needed changes, mixed central and departmental control, stale pages, and unstable URLs.
- Resource constraints: [[university-websites-the-so-so-the-bad-and-the-egregious-university-affairs]] includes reader accounts of one person managing more than 40 sites, broad webmaster job descriptions, below-market pay, and short-lived digital teams cut through attrition.
- Priority conflict: [[university-websites-the-so-so-the-bad-and-the-egregious-university-affairs]] contrasts well-funded recruitment content with neglected resources for existing university members and describes political competition for homepage and navigation space.
- Service standard: [[university-websites-the-so-so-the-bad-and-the-egregious-university-affairs]] calls for visible essential links, accessibility, mobile compatibility, user feedback, measurement, and a site purpose centered on helping people navigate institutional complexity.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a 2016 opinion essay informed by Twitter responses and followed by self-selected reader comments, not a systematic audit or comparative study. Its staffing figures and organizational explanations are anecdotal, and institutional constraints vary. Centralization can improve consistency while also creating bottlenecks, and decentralization can work when local owners have training, standards, access, and support. The source identifies recurring symptoms and plausible organizational causes but does not test governance models, quantify accessibility failures, or establish that every university shares the same priority conflict.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to distinguish institutional web governance from page-level visual design.
- Established staffing, ownership, authority, and priority-setting as causes of user-facing information failures.
- Added the qualification that centralization alone does not guarantee user-centered service.
