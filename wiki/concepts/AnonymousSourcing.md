## Definition
[[AnonymousSourcing]] is the journalistic practice of publishing information from a source whose identity is withheld from the audience, usually to protect access or reduce the risk of retaliation.

## Current Synthesis
Walk frames anonymity as a tradeoff between access to consequential information and the reader's ability to evaluate it. Withholding a name can protect a source who would face genuine jeopardy, especially a whistleblower exposing illegal activity. It also hides facts about the source's position, knowledge, incentives, and possible grievance, so the audience cannot independently calibrate the weight of the statement.

The essay therefore proposes a two-stage discipline. An anonymous allegation can be a lead that points a reporter toward documents, named testimony, or other corroboration, but it should not automatically become publishable proof or a convenient quote that manufactures conflict. The stronger the reputational claim, the more important editorial judgment, contextual disclosure, and independent verification become.

## Key Claims
- Anonymity can enable important reporting when identification would expose a knowledgeable source to meaningful harm.
- Withholding identity also removes role, access, incentive, and bias information that readers use to judge credibility.
- Negative quotations from former employees are easy enough to obtain that availability alone is weak evidence of significance.
- Anonymous allegations are more defensible as investigative leads than as self-validating proof.
- Editorial decisions should weigh public importance and source risk against credibility loss and reputational harm.

## Evidence
- Lost context: [[you-can-always-find-an-anonymous-former-employee-to-trash-the-founder-hunter-walk]] argues that readers cannot judge whether an unnamed speaker was positioned to know or motivated by bias.
- Protective value: [[you-can-always-find-an-anonymous-former-employee-to-trash-the-founder-hunter-walk]] identifies whistleblowing about illegal activity as a case where anonymity may unlock important information while protecting the source.
- Investigative use: [[you-can-always-find-an-anonymous-former-employee-to-trash-the-founder-hunter-walk]] recommends using anonymous tips to locate the underlying evidence rather than treating the quotation itself as the story.
- Reputational risk: [[you-can-always-find-an-anonymous-former-employee-to-trash-the-founder-hunter-walk]] criticizes controversy-seeking use of unnamed criticism about technology companies and founders.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a short, interested opinion essay rather than a systematic study of newsroom practice. It does not examine cases where named sourcing is impossible, where corroboration must also remain confidential, or where a newsroom gives readers useful role and access context without revealing a name. Its claim that negative former employees can always be found is rhetorical, not measured, and a source's willingness to be named does not itself establish truth. The practical standard therefore requires more than an anonymity-versus-naming binary: reporters must assess knowledge, motive, corroboration, public interest, proportionality, and the actual risks of identification.

## What Changed
- Created a concept that treats anonymous sourcing as an access-versus-verifiability tradeoff rather than an inherently credible or inherently improper practice.
