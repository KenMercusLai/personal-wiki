## Definition
[[MusicDiscovery]] is the process by which listeners encounter unfamiliar artists, recordings, genres, scenes, and musical histories through platforms, people, publications, places, performances, and credits.

## Current Synthesis
The six interviews describe discovery as a portfolio of complementary paths. Streaming recommendations provide a low-friction starting point, while specialist platforms expose tags, collections, buyer activity, and emerging work. Friends, professional contacts, critics, record-store staff, and scene participants add context and trust; shows, liner notes, and musical lineages create paths that cross genre boundaries and connect a recording to collaborators or history.

The source also distinguishes access from depth. Easy recommendation can surface a track without producing durable interest in its artist, while live or interpersonal discovery may encourage deeper attention. Greater ease of publishing widens the available field but shifts scarcity toward judgment: listeners still need to decide which tags, writers, scenes, people, or historical threads to follow.

## Key Claims
- Discovery is strongest in the source when multiple channels reinforce one another.
- Human recommendation contributes context, trust, and relationships that automated suggestions may not supply.
- Platform structures shape exploration differently through related artists, playlists, tags, collections, purchase trails, and genre-specific upload cultures.
- Physical and documentary routes such as shows, record stores, liner notes, and histories support deeper branching exploration.
- More available music increases access while making attention and path selection harder.

## Evidence
- Mixed-channel practice: [[finding-new-music-in-the-algorithm-age-the-outline]] reports routines combining streaming, social media, publications, submissions, email, live shows, record stores, and word of mouth.
- Human context: [[finding-new-music-in-the-algorithm-age-the-outline]] describes recommendations from friends, artists, managers, lawyers, clerks, journalists, editors, labels, publishers, and local scenes.
- Platform differences: [[finding-new-music-in-the-algorithm-age-the-outline]] contrasts Spotify recommendations with Bandcamp tags and buyer trails and SoundCloud's emerging-rap role.
- Branching exploration: [[finding-new-music-in-the-algorithm-age-the-outline]] connects shows, genre comparison, liner notes, collaborators, production credits, and listening backward through musical histories.
- Abundance: [[finding-new-music-in-the-algorithm-age-the-outline]] records Hua Hsu's judgment that easier publication makes the choice of worthwhile “wormholes” more difficult.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence consists of six selected 2018 interviews, not measured listener behavior or artist outcomes. The speakers differ in age, genre, profession, geography, access, and available time, so their routines should not be treated as one universally superior method. Professional inboxes, licensing relationships, editorial access, and direct submissions are unavailable to many listeners. The article also does not establish that human curation is less biased, more diverse, or more effective than algorithmic recommendation.

## What Changed
- Established music discovery as a mixed human, platform, physical, and historical practice rather than a choice between algorithms and “old” media.
- Distinguished encountering a recommended song from developing deeper attachment to an artist or scene.
