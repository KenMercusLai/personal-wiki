## Definition
[[HiringSystemDesign]] is the deliberate construction of hiring as an end-to-end, measurable organizational system: decide whether and why to hire, define the needed outcomes, attract candidates, gather job-relevant evidence, assign decisions, onboard the hire, observe results, and revise the process over time.

## Current Synthesis
[[DanPupius]] frames hiring as two coupled products: the process candidates experience and the team it produces. The design starts with a specific account of the whole team's needed traits, values, skills, and composition. ROCKETSHP adds an earlier gate: test whether the work requires a permanent hire, connect the role to near-term company goals, and express it through daily responsibilities and outcomes. Together, these sources make role definition a hypothesis about useful organizational capacity rather than a request to fill a familiar title.

The process then spans acquisition, selection, and employment. Employer culture and benefits, inbound content, direct outreach, recruiters, and job boards can create candidates; source tracking and a reusable talent pool can show which channels deserve continued effort. Selection needs plural ways for candidates to show relevant ability, advance disclosure of the rubric, calibrated interviewers, and a single accountable decision-maker who uses the panel's evidence without requiring consensus. Screening questions, skills tests, work samples, and references are useful only when their relevance, burden, and interpretation are explicit.

Measurement operates on different time horizons: source yield, funnel movement, and candidate feedback reveal immediate friction; offer acceptance, onboarding, and employee sentiment provide intermediate signals; performance and retention are delayed and confounded outcomes. The system should remain documented and change through focused retrospectives before repeatable parts are automated. Retention also feeds back into design: culture, transparency, growth opportunity, compensation, and manager attention affect whether a nominally successful hire becomes durable contribution.

Jackson's product-manager process adds a role-specific example. It turns the job into baseline and stage-dependent capabilities, uses a reciprocal screen, cross-functional interviews, an explicit continuation gate, and a product presentation with live questions, then treats frequent direct communication and honest motivation matching as part of the close. This strengthens work relevance and candidate reciprocity, while leaving the broader system responsible for scoring anchors, interviewer calibration, accessibility, burden, adverse-impact review, and post-hire validation.

## Key Claims
- Hiring design should begin by testing the need for a hire and defining the team outcome, not by opening a familiar title or collecting individually impressive candidates.
- Criteria should be defined in behavioral and job-relevant terms, while pedigree and other weak or bias-inducing signals are explicitly excluded or treated as hypotheses requiring stronger evidence.
- Sourcing should use a clear candidate proposition, multiple relevant channels, source measurement, and a reusable talent pool rather than depend on one undifferentiated applicant stream.
- Fair evaluation requires transparent expectations, multiple job-relevant demonstration modes, calibrated interviewers, and explicit controls on candidate burden.
- Decision authority should be explicit so evidence can inform a timely decision without forcing panel consensus.
- Candidate communication, closing, onboarding, contribution, and retention are linked system outcomes and should feed process improvement.
- Metrics and themed iteration can make the system learn, but no single funnel, acceptance, performance, or retention measure proves hiring quality or fairness.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The framework now draws on one operator interview about Medium and early-stage Range plus a broad 2017 practitioner handbook, not controlled comparisons. Neither source supplies selection-rate, performance, retention, demographic, or causal outcome data. ROCKETSHP's statistics, platforms, and tool recommendations are historical, its universal-sounding preference for early generalists has role-specific exceptions, and its equity explanation includes an incorrect claim that dilution makes existing shares worthless.

Behavioral rubrics can still encode disputed values, observable behavior can be interpreted unevenly, alternative exercises can differ in burden and validity, and interviewer shadowing can reproduce a shared bias as easily as a shared standard. Personality quizzes, intuitive unease, and “get a drink” cultural-fit tests are especially vulnerable to affinity and disability bias. Multi-day work trials introduce compensation, confidentiality, accessibility, and legal questions. Offer acceptance reflects compensation and labor-market conditions as well as screening quality, while retention can reflect constrained mobility or exclusion as well as fit. Informal backchannels also raise fairness, privacy, and legal concerns.

The PM-specific source likewise supplies no predictive-validity, interviewer-agreement, demographic, accessibility, or post-hire outcome evidence. Its use of school reputation, prior-company prestige, enthusiasm, technical pedigree, cultural effectiveness, and presentation polish needs the evidence and fairness controls described elsewhere on this page.

## What Changed
- Extended the system boundary upstream to the hire-versus-contractor decision and outcome-based role design.
- Added multi-channel attraction, source tracking, and talent-pool maintenance as measurable acquisition components.
- Extended the feedback loop downstream through onboarding, contribution, culture, growth, and retention.
- Separated potentially useful work evidence from subjective cultural-fit screens and burdensome auditions.
- Added a PM-specific staged example connecting role capabilities, cross-functional evidence, an exercise gate, candidate communication, and motivation-aware closing.
