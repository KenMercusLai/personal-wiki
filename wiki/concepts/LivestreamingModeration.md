## Definition
[[LivestreamingModeration]] is the operational and regulatory work of monitoring, filtering, curating, and governing real-time video broadcasts.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats moderation as an unavoidable part of Chinese livestreaming because real-time personal broadcasts can quickly cross legal, sexual, or platform-quality boundaries. Platforms respond with large monitoring teams, user blocking tools, content flagging, and manual curation, while the Chinese state adds licensing and morality-related pressure. Moderation is therefore not a peripheral safety feature; it helps define what can be discovered, monetized, and sustained.

## Key Claims
- Livestreaming inherits television-like and online-video-like regulation in China.
- Real-time content requires substantial human monitoring capacity.
- Platforms moderate both illegal or inappropriate content and, in some cases, low-quality or boring content.
- Broadcaster tools and top-fan blocking can reduce harassment and abuse.
- Regulation may reshape platform processes, licenses, and broadcaster behavior.

## Evidence
- Regulatory action: [[16-observations-on-livestreaming-in-china-andreessen-horowitz]] says China's Ministry of Culture investigated platforms for content that harmed social morality.
- Platform staffing: [[16-observations-on-livestreaming-in-china-andreessen-horowitz]] says many apps employ hundreds or thousands of people to monitor content.
- Curation scope: [[16-observations-on-livestreaming-in-china-andreessen-horowitz]] says monitoring can include flagging inappropriate content and manually curating which broadcasts are featured.
- Abuse control: [[16-observations-on-livestreaming-in-china-andreessen-horowitz]] describes tools that let broadcasters and top fans permanently block misbehaving users.
- Licensing uncertainty: [[16-observations-on-livestreaming-in-china-andreessen-horowitz]] expects further clarification about internal processes and required platform or broadcaster licenses.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The article describes early Chinese regulatory pressure in 2016 and should not be read as a current legal guide. It does not compare later platform enforcement systems, automated moderation, labor costs, appeal processes, or policy outcomes.
