## Definition
[[OrganizationalTransparency]] is the deliberate availability of priorities, decisions, calendars, meeting context, and operating information so people can coordinate and decide with less dependence on private access.

## Current Synthesis
The Zappos case frames transparency as decision infrastructure rather than disclosure for its own sake. When employees can inspect the context leaders use, they may ask fewer duplicative questions, skip meetings whose record is sufficient, and exercise distributed authority with better information. The article connects this logic to [[TonyHsieh]]'s public schedule and priorities, Zappos's Holacracy rollout, Slack's archived discussions, Buffer's public metrics, and open calendars at Exact Media.

The [[JetCom]] case extends inspectability into [[SalaryTransparency]]. Its reported combination of visible individual pay, fixed salary levels, standardized level-based equity, no bonuses, and promotion-only raises made the compensation rule easier to see and challenge while moving discretion into classification and promotion. Across both cases, the useful default is transparent-first rather than indiscriminate disclosure: employee privacy, legal duties, proprietary information, timing, context, information overload, and the fairness of the underlying decision process remain separate constraints.

## Key Claims
- Shared decision context can reduce information asymmetry between leaders and employees.
- Durable meeting records can provide awareness without requiring every interested person to attend.
- Transparent priorities and calendars can make ownership, availability, and tradeoffs easier to inspect.
- Distributed authority depends on access to enough common information for situational decision-makers to act.
- Compensation transparency can expose inconsistent outcomes, but fixed bands leave consequential discretion in level assignment, promotion, and market calibration.
- Transparency needs explicit confidentiality, privacy, timing, and proprietary-information boundaries.

## Evidence
- Decision parity: [[i-studied-the-zappos-ceos-schedule-for-a-year-heres-what-i-learned]] argues that teams need access to the information leaders use if they are expected to make comparable decisions.
- Meeting substitution: [[i-studied-the-zappos-ceos-schedule-for-a-year-heres-what-i-learned]] cites readable meeting discussions as a way for employees to learn they did not need to attend.
- Operating examples: [[i-studied-the-zappos-ceos-schedule-for-a-year-heres-what-i-learned]] points to Hsieh's public schedule and priorities, archived Slack discussions, Buffer disclosures, and Exact Media's shared metrics and calendars.
- Organizational design: [[i-studied-the-zappos-ceos-schedule-for-a-year-heres-what-i-learned]] links transparency with Zappos's effort to distribute authority through Holacracy.
- Compensation inspectability: [[jet-employees-know-each-others-salaries-business-insider]] reports that Jet employees knew salaries and could challenge perceived unfairness.
- Rule visibility: [[jet-employees-know-each-others-salaries-business-insider]] describes 13 fixed levels, equal equity within a level, no bonuses, and promotion-only raises.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources supply leadership examples and uncontrolled observations, not evidence that more disclosure reliably reduces workload, improves decisions, or creates fair compensation. Transparency can produce information overload, performative openness, surveillance, privacy loss, premature interpretation, or decision ambiguity when ownership and summaries are weak. Employee data, legal duties, security, negotiations, proprietary parent-company information, and sensitive personnel matters require bounded access. The reported decline in Hsieh's emails and work hours after Holacracy is correlational and does not isolate transparency from staffing, delegation, seasonality, or other organizational changes. Jet's fixed bands can limit negotiation differences while concealing unequal level assignment or promotion; the article provides no employee perspectives, pay audit, or measured outcomes.

## What Changed
- Created a transparent-first model centered on shared decision context rather than indiscriminate disclosure.
- Preserved confidentiality and causal-evidence limits around the Zappos example.
- Extended the model to compensation, distinguishing visible rules from fairness in classification and promotion.
