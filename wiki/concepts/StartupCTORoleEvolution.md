## Definition
[[StartupCTORoleEvolution]] is the recurring redesign of a startup technology leader's responsibilities as the company's dominant technical, product, people, and coordination constraints change.

## Current Synthesis
[[JoshuaWohle]]'s [[SuperAwesome]] retrospective presents the startup CTO less as a stable functional office than as a moving constraint-solving role. He progressed from incident response and inherited-infrastructure repair to trusted-network recruiting, product management, product vision, customer strategy, cross-company coordination, and forward-looking scale work. The decisive leadership move was repeatedly transferring yesterday's core responsibility to a Chief Architect, Head of Engineering, or Chief Product Officer who could give it deeper expertise and attention.

The Ivy Exec role model supplies a complementary target state: a CTO concentrates on technical edge, exploration, intellectual property, external representation, and shared technical strategy, while a VP Engineering owns people, delivery programs, roadmaps, cross-functional execution, and the engineering budget. The pattern is not simple upward delegation. Each handoff depends on recognizing that company growth has changed the job and transferring real decision ownership to a leader suited to the sustained constraint. The source's suggested 15–20-employee trigger is best treated as a prompt to inspect founder management load, not a universal threshold.

## Key Claims
- A startup CTO's effective job is determined by the company's current bottleneck more than by a fixed title.
- Role transitions can recur every six to twelve months during rapid growth.
- Technical founders may need to learn product management and stakeholder communication before those functions can support dedicated leadership.
- Hiring specialists is a transfer of real decision ownership, not merely additional capacity beneath the founder.
- The CTO's enduring responsibility is to anticipate the next product-and-engineering scaling constraint after prior duties have been handed off.
- A VP Engineering can preserve CTO attention for technical direction by taking primary ownership of engineering people, programs, roadmaps, and budgets.

## Evidence
- Infrastructure survival: [[being-a-start-up-cto-or-how-i-fired-myself-enough-times-to-finally-become-cto]] describes restoring unstable acquired systems, migrating physical hardware, and refactoring to reduce repeated outages in 2013.
- Recruiting and product expansion: [[being-a-start-up-cto-or-how-i-fired-myself-enough-times-to-finally-become-cto]] says Wöhle recruited trusted contacts, learned product management, and later led prioritization, technical decisions, and company-wide product communication.
- Specialist ownership: [[being-a-start-up-cto-or-how-i-fired-myself-enough-times-to-finally-become-cto]] links the Chief Architect, Head of Engineering, and Chief Product Officer hires to architecture depth, people/process focus, and full-time product-portfolio strategy respectively.
- Changing cadence: [[being-a-start-up-cto-or-how-i-fired-myself-enough-times-to-finally-become-cto]] explicitly says a scaling startup can make the role materially different every six to twelve months.
- Forward-looking scope: [[being-a-start-up-cto-or-how-i-fired-myself-enough-times-to-finally-become-cto]] describes the later CTO role as strategic customer and partner work, key hiring, and pre-empting the next product-and-engineering scaling challenges.
- Functional split: [[cto-vs-vp-engineering-whats-the-difference-ivy-exec-blog]] assigns technical-edge, exploration, patent, external, and due-diligence work to the CTO while placing personnel, program, roadmap, cross-functional execution, and budget ownership with the VP Engineering.
- Hiring signal: [[cto-vs-vp-engineering-whats-the-difference-ivy-exec-blog]] proposes rising people and project-management load around 15–20 company employees as a reason to consider a VP Engineering.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence consists of one founder's retrospective at one fast-growing kidtech company and one 2015 practitioner framework. The eight stages may reflect SuperAwesome's acquisition history, category creation, customer geography, and Wöhle's own strengths rather than a universal CTO ladder. The Ivy Exec headcount thresholds do not control for business model, technical-team size, regulation, hardware, or founder capability, and its neat title split does not cover every Head of Engineering, Chief Architect, CPO, or combined leadership model. Creating more executive roles can also add coordination cost, unclear authority, or premature specialization; neither source provides comparative team, delivery, innovation, reliability, retention, budget, or business outcomes.

## What Changed
- Added a functional CTO/VP Engineering boundary to complement SuperAwesome's constraint-driven handoff sequence.
- Qualified the 15–20-employee hiring signal as a context-dependent heuristic.
