## Definition
[[VisualAttention]] is the selective allocation of limited perceptual processing to some visible stimuli rather than others, shaped by both stimulus-driven signals and the viewer's current goals.

## Current Synthesis
The source organizes attention capture into nine overlapping families: salience, motion, people, animals, spatial cues, high arousal, unpredictability, self-relevance, and goal relevance. The useful design principle is not to maximize every trigger but to create a legible priority within a specific environment. Color, orientation, size, onset, implied movement, gaze, arrows, novelty, names, and first-person imagery can establish an entry point or redirect the eyes, but piling them together can erase the contrast that made any one of them salient.

The bottom-up and top-down distinction sets the boundary. When people browse without a narrow goal and have spare capacity, conspicuous or biologically meaningful signals are more likely to intrude. During focused search, viewers favor information resembling the target and can suppress locations or visual treatments learned to be irrelevant. A highly distinctive banner may therefore attract in one setting and identify itself as ignorable advertising in another.

## Key Claims
- Attention is selective because visible input exceeds conscious processing capacity.
- Salience depends on contrast with surrounding stimuli, and color, orientation, and size differences can combine.
- Motion, people, animals, social direction cues, arousal, novelty, and self-relevance can bias selection before deliberate evaluation.
- Learned cues such as arrows and directional words can redirect attention alongside biologically meaningful cues such as gaze and body orientation.
- Habituation makes repeated locations and requests easier to ignore, so novelty can interrupt a learned filtering routine.
- Viewer goals and perceptual load determine whether conspicuous difference helps, fails, or makes a stimulus easier to reject.
- Capturing a glance is not equivalent to comprehension, preference, trust, conversion, safety, or durable value.

## Evidence
- Capacity and selection: [[visual-attention-an-enormous-guide]] uses selective-attention research to frame consciousness as a narrow subset of available visual input.
- Feature contrast: [[visual-attention-an-enormous-guide]] illustrates color, orientation, and size oddballs and recommends focal hierarchy rather than uniform vibrance.
- Motion and embodied forms: [[visual-attention-an-enormous-guide]] distinguishes onset, looming, animate, implied, and biological motion, then extends the same form sensitivity to faces, bodies, body parts, and animals.
- Direction and social attention: [[visual-attention-an-enormous-guide]] compares gaze, head and body orientation, pointing, arrows, and directional words as spatial cues.
- Arousal, novelty, and the self: [[visual-attention-an-enormous-guide]] connects threat, taboo language, unusual combinations, names, faces, and imagined interaction to preferential processing.
- Goal boundary: [[visual-attention-an-enormous-guide]] contrasts low-load browsing with goal-directed search and warns that ad salience can reveal irrelevance.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This synthesis rests on one marketer's secondary review of more than 300 papers, not an independently audited systematic review. The source combines laboratory tasks, neural activation, evolutionary interpretation, anecdotes, and speculative design extrapolation without consistently separating effect size, replication status, or outcome type. Some claims—especially sex-specific color sensitivity, evolutionary threat templates, dominance explanations, and demographic outgroup tactics—risk overgeneralization or harmful application. Attention effects vary with task, load, culture, literacy, familiarity, accessibility, motion sensitivity, and individual differences. Designers should test comprehension and downstream outcomes rather than treat a captured fixation as success.

## What Changed
- Created a context-sensitive model joining bottom-up capture with top-down goal selection.
- Preserved the source's warning that excessive stimulus stacking can destroy salience.
- Separated perceptual capture from persuasion, usefulness, and other downstream outcomes.
