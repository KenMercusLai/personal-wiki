## Definition
[[ComputationalLanguage]] is a precise symbolic representation that can talk about a broad range of things in the world and can be executed unambiguously by a computer, so that what it specifies can be computed rather than interpreted socially.

## Current Synthesis
The source contrasts computational language with human language on precision. Human language is flexible and vague because it is not tethered to an implementation and its meaning rests on a social contract between speakers - whether "planet" includes exoplanets depends on usage. A computational language has to be precise about every distinction it makes, because its statements can be run. That precision is bought with a representational commitment: the language needs an underlying ontology of the kinds of things and relations that exist, which is why the essay calls this the hard, largely unsolved part of the project.

The essay presents [[WolframLanguage]] as an existing partial realization with decades of accumulated coverage: symbolic representations for cities, molecules, images, and neural networks, each with built-in computation. Its acknowledged gap is everyday discourse - the system can represent "two pounds of apples" and compute nutrition, but has no symbolic representation for "I bought". Closing that gap would require general calculi about moving, having, and doing. Once available, such a language could be used to make standalone statements, ask Wolfram|Alpha-style questions, specify things one wants to make so, or make assertions about the actual world or a fictional one. The essay frames this as an ultimate compression of representation - talking about the essence of what is possible without the turns of phrase - and proposes combining it with a model like ChatGPT, which would supply a sense of what is popular and how it is usually said.

## Key Claims
- Computational language is distinguished by executability: what it specifies can be run unambiguously on a computer.
- Precision is a requirement rather than a style choice: the language must make every distinction explicit, unlike human language's socially negotiated vagueness.
- Precision depends on an ontology - a principled account of which kinds of things and relations the language recognises - which the essay identifies as the difficult unsolved question.
- Existing computational languages already cover large areas symbolically, including entities such as cities and molecules and structures such as images and neural networks.
- The missing area is everyday discourse: actions such as buying are not yet represented, and adding them requires general calculi about movement, possession, and similar relations.
- A complete version could be used for statements, questions, actuation requests, and assertions about real or fictional worlds.
- The essay treats computational language as a form of compression, and proposes pairing it with a language model that supplies fluency and popularity while the language supplies what is possible and computable.

- Named gap: [[what-is-chatgpt-doing-and-why-does-it-work]] says the system can represent and compute on "two pounds of apples" but does not have a symbolic representation for "I bought".

- Compression claim: [[what-is-chatgpt-doing-and-why-does-it-work]] describes computational language as representing the essence of what is possible without the turns of phrase of ordinary language.
- Pairing with a model: [[what-is-chatgpt-doing-and-why-does-it-work]] proposes combining a language model's sense of what is popular with computational language's access to irreversible computation and correctness.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is presented as a research programme motivated by one author's product, and the source does not demonstrate a general symbolic discourse language, an ontology, or an evaluation of coverage. Its claims about human-language vagueness are broad, and the essay does not address how ambiguity, metaphor, social meaning, or translation would be handled if language were replaced by explicit symbolic forms.
