## Definition
[[StartupBoardGovernance]] is the allocation of information, challenge, support, evaluation, and decision authority among founders, directors, and investors as a young company changes stage.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames effective governance as a balance between two asymmetric forms of knowledge. Founder-operators are closer to customers, employees, products, and markets, so their detailed context and accumulated intuition should constrain board intervention. Experienced directors can contribute comparisons across companies and growth stages, standards for unfamiliar executive roles, and independence when leadership or conduct threatens the company. The board's purpose is therefore neither founder subordination nor unconditional founder protection: it is candid partnership oriented toward the company's durable interests.

This balance becomes most difficult when founder and company needs diverge. A CEO who excels at discovery and product-market fit may not be suited to incentive design, organizational scale, or product-line expansion; strong operating skill also does not excuse misconduct. Formal oversight and personal coaching can both help, but a director's duty to evaluate creates a role conflict with being an entirely confidential adviser. Governance design should make that difference explicit rather than hiding it behind the ambiguous label “founder friendly.”

## Key Claims
- Founder operating proximity creates an information advantage that boards should respect.
- Directors add value through cross-company pattern recognition, standards for executive excellence, and willingness to make difficult judgments.
- CEO suitability is stage-dependent, so founder succession can be a company-building decision rather than a punishment or betrayal.
- Boards should protect founders from investor abuse without promising unconditional support regardless of performance or conduct.
- Formal evaluation and confidential coaching are distinct functions whose combination can create role conflict.
- Governance failure can emerge jointly from founder ego and director passivity, with costs borne by employees and customers as well as capital providers.

## Evidence
- Information asymmetry: [[founder-friendly-no-thanks-sam-gerstenzang-medium]] says founders have more operational detail and market repetitions than directors normally do.
- Board contribution: [[founder-friendly-no-thanks-sam-gerstenzang-medium]] assigns experienced directors role-level standards and pattern recognition across comparable stages of growth.
- Leadership transition: [[founder-friendly-no-thanks-sam-gerstenzang-medium]] distinguishes taking a company from an idea to product-market fit from designing incentives and scaling multiple product lines.
- Accountability boundary: [[founder-friendly-no-thanks-sam-gerstenzang-medium]] rejects unconditional founder support while also rejecting a board that pushes a founder around without sufficient information.
- Role conflict: [[founder-friendly-no-thanks-sam-gerstenzang-medium]] contrasts grading with teaching to explain why some coaching investors avoid board seats.
- Stakeholder cost: [[founder-friendly-no-thanks-sam-gerstenzang-medium]] argues that founder ego and board cowardice can harm founders, directors, employees, and customers by limiting company potential.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is one investor-practitioner essay rather than a comparative study of boards, founder replacements, misconduct interventions, or company outcomes. A company-centered standard does not itself determine who can reliably identify the company's interests, how to weigh founder vision against investor time horizons, or when directors have enough information to intervene. Directors may carry financial conflicts, pattern-match incorrectly, or use “professionalization” to rationalize power shifts; founders may also invoke unique context to resist warranted accountability. The article's Uber conclusion is a counterfactual assertion, not evidence that earlier action by Benchmark would have produced a better or longer-lasting company.

## What Changed
- Established founder information advantage and director pattern recognition as complementary governance inputs.
- Distinguished protection from investor abuse from unconditional founder support.
- Added stage-dependent CEO fit and misconduct as separate reasons a board may need to act.
- Separated confidential coaching from the board's formal evaluative role.
