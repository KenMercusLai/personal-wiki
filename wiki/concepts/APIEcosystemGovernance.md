## Definition
[[APIEcosystemGovernance]] is the continuing design of product boundaries, access, policies, incentives, communication, distribution, and support that determines how a provider, third-party developers, and users share value and risk around an API.

## Current Synthesis
A public API creates more than an integration surface: it invites external investment under expectations about access and strategic welcome. Costa's Twitter case shows the danger of opening core product surfaces before business-model and ownership boundaries are clear; later advertising needs, first-party client strategy, quality concerns, and client consolidation turned some complements into competitors. His Slack comparison shows the opposite posture: scopes, review, discovery, roadmap signals, customer-problem feedback, promotion, and capital can steer developers toward complementary work. The durable principle is reciprocal benefit under explicit boundaries, not unrestricted access or ecosystem size alone.

## Key Claims
- API publication is a long-term product and governance commitment because developers invest under expectations that cannot be withdrawn without trust costs.
- Providers should distinguish experiences they must own from complementary capabilities they want external developers to supply.
- Business-model changes can convert formerly aligned API participants into competitors.
- Ecosystem health depends on reciprocal benefit for users, developers, and the platform provider.
- Review, scoped permissions, directories, roadmaps, feedback channels, promotion, and funding can actively shape complementary development.
- Keystone integrations can establish quality and value-chain direction when providers reward them transparently.

## Evidence
- Commitment and reversal cost: [[a-tale-of-2-api-platforms-ggv-capital-medium]] argues that Twitter's early permissive API created expectations that made later v1.1 restrictions damaging beyond the directly affected apps.
- Core-surface conflict: [[a-tale-of-2-api-platforms-ggv-capital-medium]] connects Twitter's advertising and first-party client strategy to conflict with third-party clients replicating the central experience.
- Consolidation risk: [[a-tale-of-2-api-platforms-ggv-capital-medium]] presents UberMedia's client acquisitions as a possible external control point over tweet creation and consumption.
- Complement steering: [[a-tale-of-2-api-platforms-ggv-capital-medium]] describes Slack using OAuth scopes, review, an app directory, a roadmap, customer feedback, promotion, and an investment fund.
- Reciprocal-value test: [[a-tale-of-2-api-platforms-ggv-capital-medium]] says ecosystem design should benefit the provider, developers, and users rather than maximizing API openness by itself.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The framework comes from one 2016 practitioner essay and compares selected historical episodes rather than equivalent outcome data. Twitter launched its API before its business model and client-consolidation risks were clear, poorly made third-party clients could harm users, and the source says only a subset of developers was directly affected by v1.1. Slack's directory and fund demonstrate ecosystem investment but do not by themselves prove superior durability, developer economics, innovation, or user outcomes. Some restrictions can be necessary for privacy, safety, reliability, abuse prevention, or business survival; the governance test is clarity, proportionality, expectation management, and reciprocal value rather than permanent unrestricted access.

## What Changed
- Established API governance as an ongoing product-boundary and incentive-design responsibility.
- Added provider business-model conflict and third-party control-point consolidation as ecosystem risks.
- Added directories, review, scopes, roadmaps, feedback, promotion, and funding as ecosystem-shaping mechanisms.
