## Definition
[[AIAssistedWriting]] is a writing workflow where AI systems help with ideation, source processing, drafting, fact-check support, revision, variants, voice cleanup, titles, images, and layout while a human author retains responsibility for the question, material, argument, taste, verification, and final publication.

## Current Synthesis
The sources frame AI-assisted writing as accountable workflow design rather than a binary replacement for authorship. [[FengRuohang]] describes a layered process: the human chooses the topic from reading, thinking, and conversations; defines the structure and claim logic; lets AI expand an initial draft; cross-checks facts through [[Gemini]] and [[ChatGPT]]; performs several revision rounds; and uses AI again for titles, image prompts, generated visuals, and Codex-assisted layout. [[Hanyang]] adds a humanities-production version: prepare clean material, define audience and standards, decompose nonfiction work into narrow steps, generate multiple candidates, and keep the process traceable enough that the human remains willing to sign the final work.

The working-blogger version of the same pattern treats ChatGPT as scaffolding rather than finished authorship. Its drafts can lack examples, statistics, story, and personal voice, but flawed text still gives the writer something to criticize and rewrite. Brainstorming is also a partial-hit-rate activity: lists of questions, outlines, and candidate angles can be valuable even when many items are dull, because the writer only needs enough prompts to unlock better thinking. Chen's later writing-output note pushes this further into cadence: AI is useful when it creates more candidate topic sentences, angle lists, or cleaned voice material that a writer can curate inside a broader habit of small posts and audience feedback.

Together, the sources shift the question from "Did AI write this?" to "Did the author design, supervise, verify, and materially own the work?" AI can accelerate drafting and packaging, but its most reliable role is often upstream or midstream: extracting structure, compressing rich source material, testing openings, grouping stories, generating outlines or questions, and giving the author a better set of options to judge. The voice-input prompt and Chen's talk-out-loud workflow add a capture-side version of the same pattern: spoken text can enter a writing workflow faster when AI cleanup remains faithful enough for later human editing.

Reorx's 2023 experiment supplies an early, compact example of this division of labor. Glarity summarizes a YouTube transcript in English, Bob translates the summary into Chinese, and a Drafts action asks ChatGPT for a social recommendation under 140 Chinese characters. The author estimates that the pipeline reduced at least 30 minutes of watching and writing to about five, but he still rearranged the translated material himself. His own boundary is explicit: AI should absorb non-creative labor so the creator has more time for creative work, not automate away the thinking, learning, accomplishment, and frustration that make writing valuable.

Späti and [[AlexWoods]] add the strongest dependency warnings in the set. Both accept deliberate AI use but argue that outsourcing the writing itself can remove the research, grappling, voice, conviction, and manual practice through which writers think. Woods adds a social test: a PRD, technical specification, or essay is evidence that its author found the question, structured ambiguity, and developed judgment, so model-written prose can weaken trust even when it approximates an acceptable answer. This does not negate AI-assisted workflows; it narrows their healthiest role toward research, checking, critique, capture, transcription, structure, divergent ideation, and optionality rather than finished authorship.

[[BryanCantrill]] makes the reader-side boundary more severe. His argument is not only that delegated composition weakens the writer's learning, but that detectable LLM prose can cause readers to abandon both the piece and the named author because they cannot tell what intellectual work is real. This qualifies workflow accounts that accept generated drafts as scaffolding: private drafting may still help a writer think, but public trust depends on how much of the resulting language and reasoning readers reasonably attribute to the human. Cantrill permits LLM editing and proposes [[AIWritingDetection]] as an institutional confidence mechanism, though his survey and Pangram-accuracy evidence are not representative or independently benchmarked.

Revision introduces a distinct risk: a rewrite can remain fluent and factually plausible while losing the rare metaphor, exact term, subtext, qualification, or non-linear structure that carried the author's signal. This makes fidelity a first-class review criterion. A polished draft should be compared with its input for retained meaning and voice, not accepted because readability improved; however, the attribution of this pattern to greedy decoding and human-feedback tuning remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated causal result.

A layered voice-input case makes this boundary concrete: an essay began as spoken thought, passed through model-mediated transcription, and then received another AI polishing pass. The author accepts the result provisionally because the originating ideas and judgments remain his, but cannot say how much "human flavor" survived. This sharpens authorship into two related tests: whether the human still owns the proposition and whether the workflow preserves the lived voice, emotional texture, and idiosyncrasy through which that proposition became worth reading.

Mai Yang adds an operational route for preserving that signal: derive a writing Skill from verbatim Markdown, select exemplars separately for structure and tone, put generative skeletons ahead of banned-word lists, and improve the guide from diffs between an AI draft and the writer's accepted revision. Her strongest constraint is independent of voice: work documents must expose unknown numbers, owners, commitments, risk levels, and quotation uncertainty rather than turn missing evidence into confident action language. This extends workflow accountability from fact checking after drafting into the instructions that govern drafting itself.

Losses Don adds a complementary live-dialogue method. The writer first supplies a short or medium stream of thought in their own register, asks the model to keep questioning until omitted considerations and execution details are explicit, requests an outline only after the material is exhausted, and then manually rewrites unfamiliar expressions. This makes interrogation and omission discovery more valuable than one-shot prose generation. It also reinforces Mai Yang's warning about blacklist-first style control: banning visible phrases may suppress symptoms while leaving the model's rhetorical structure intact. Blind model comparison, a roughly three-day cooling-off interval, and whole-article rereading are proposed as practical checks for stylistic habituation, though no controlled quality comparison validates the routine.

Laixintao supplies a purpose-based test stricter than output quality. If the reason for writing is to preserve a lived record, discover what one thinks, satisfy the need for expression, or let another person encounter the writer directly, then a generated article can fail even when its prose is acceptable. This does not settle whether transcription, critique, checking, or other bounded assistance preserves those purposes; it asks that a workflow be judged against the human function at stake, not only speed, fluency, or factual correctness.

## Key Claims
- AI assistance can be deep without eliminating human authorship when topic choice, argument structure, final judgment, and credible ownership of the reasoning remain human-owned.
- Draft generation is only one layer of writing; revision, taste, source verification, packaging, and accountability still determine publication quality.
- Multi-model checking can support factual review, but critical claims still require original-source verification.
- "AI written" is a weak critique when it substitutes a tool label for engagement with claims, evidence, and reasoning.
- AI acts as a multiplier of the user's thought quality, but an efficient and accurate output can still fail when the intended value lies in the writer's own memory, reasoning exercise, expression, or direct relationship with readers.
- Stable AI-assisted writing improves by iterating the upstream workflow and accepted-output diffs, whether it starts from notes, verbatim style exemplars, source material, imperfect drafts, brainstorming lists, cleaned voice transcripts, author-written streams of thought, or small publishing experiments.
- As fluent answers become cheap, question selection, material quality, lived experience, angle, insight, personal voice, noise filtering, protected manual practice, fidelity-preserving revision, and credible reader attribution become more valuable creator skills.

## Evidence
- Human-owned workflow: [[shi-de-wo-yong-ai-xie-wen-zhang-za-di]] says the author owns topic choice, viewpoint, evidence selection, logical structure, and final title direction while using AI to draft and package faster.
- Fact checking: [[shi-de-wo-yong-ai-xie-wen-zhang-za-di]] describes checking drafts with [[Gemini]] and [[ChatGPT]], then manually verifying critical facts against original sources.
- Iterative revision: [[shi-de-wo-yong-ai-xie-wen-zhang-za-di]] says three to five revision rounds are normal, including argument repair, wording adjustment, and structural rewrites.
- Visual and layout production: [[shi-de-wo-yong-ai-xie-wen-zhang-za-di]] and its screenshots show Claude extracting publishable topics from chat history, drafting image-scene prompts, Gemini producing candidate visuals, and Codex handling layout.
- Critique standard: [[shi-de-wo-yong-ai-xie-wen-zhang-za-di]] argues that meaningful disagreement should identify mistaken claims, weak logic, or bad facts instead of stopping at an AI-use label.
- Process ownership: [[gei-ren-wen-gong-zuo-zhe-de-ai-shi-yong-zhi-nan]] says AI-assisted work should be traceable, supervisable, verifiable, controllable, and signable by the human author.
- Pipeline decomposition: [[gei-ren-wen-gong-zuo-zhe-de-ai-shi-yong-zhi-nan]] describes breaking nonfiction writing into many steps, with the screenshot showing fact extraction, story-bank merging, grouping, layout, ordering, and AB testing.
- Candidate generation: [[gei-ren-wen-gong-zuo-zhe-de-ai-shi-yong-zhi-nan]] recommends asking for multiple summaries, openings, structures, topics, and phrasings so the writer can select from a distribution rather than accept a mediocre default.
- Scarce questions and material: [[shi-de-wo-yong-ai-xie-wen-zhang-za-di]] argues that when AI makes fluent text and retrieval easier, choosing worthwhile questions and angles becomes the creator's core advantage; [[gei-ren-wen-gong-zuo-zhe-de-ai-shi-yong-zhi-nan]] adds that real archives, field interviews, oral histories, and photographs still anchor stronger work.
- Voice capture: [[ai-yu-yin-shu-ru-gong-ju-ti-shi-ci]] gives a transcript-cleanup prompt that adds punctuation, removes filler and repetition, resolves self-corrections, and structures lists only when the original speech warrants it.
- Blank-page reduction: [[andrew-chen-how-i-use-ai-when-blogging-and-writing]] says even a stiff draft can be useful because finding flaws, replacing paragraphs, and rewriting from a starting point is easier than beginning from an empty page.
- Brainstorming and outlines: [[andrew-chen-how-i-use-ai-when-blogging-and-writing]] uses ChatGPT for lists of questions, blog outlines, podcast-interview prompts, skeptical critique prompts, and book-structure prototypes where a minority of useful ideas can justify the exercise.
- Workflow limits: [[andrew-chen-how-i-use-ai-when-blogging-and-writing]] wants fresher data, long-document context, personal style training, generated figures, and social-media integration, showing that the workflow still depends on missing or imperfect capabilities.
- Cadence support: [[andrew-chen-how-to-write-more]] uses [[ChatGPT]] for aligned idea lists and topic sentences, while treating the outputs as material to curate inside a larger writing routine.
- Spoken capture: [[andrew-chen-how-to-write-more]] says voice-to-text tools can turn five to ten minutes of rambling into acceptable prose for later editing.
- Skill-atrophy warning: [[blog-simon-spati-will-ai-replace-human-thinking]] argues that writing is a thinking exercise and that outsourcing too much of it can produce generic prose, shallow summary habits, and weaker authorial judgment.
- Revision fidelity: [[why-ai-writing-is-so-generic-boring-and-dangerous-semantic-ablation]] warns that AI polishing can replace distinctive metaphor, terminology, subtext, and reasoning structure with smoother but lower-information prose.
- Thinking and trust boundary: [[dont-let-ai-write-for-you]] argues that model-written documents can skip the effort that develops understanding and fail to demonstrate that the sender has grappled with the ideas; it instead endorses research, checking, transcription, capture, and low-cost idea generation.
- Modular social-post workflow: [[yong-ai-gong-ju-kuai-su-zhuan-xie-fen-xiang-xing-tui-wen-reorxs-forge]] and its screenshots show transcript-grounded summarization, English-to-Chinese translation, and a Drafts system prompt that compresses the result under a 140-Chinese-character constraint, followed by the author's own rearrangement.
- Reader response: [[the-revolt-of-the-reader]] cites a self-selected 668-developer survey in which 78% reportedly stop reading detected AI prose, 71% avoid the author later, and 98% prefer imperfect human writing to LLM-polished prose.
- Detection-backed authorship: [[the-revolt-of-the-reader]] reports that Pangram 4's perceived error rates led Oxide to require its public writing to receive a human-authored classification.
- Verbatim style evidence: [[ru-he-cong-zi-ji-xie-guo-de-wen-zhang-li-ti-lian-xie-zuo-feng-ge-wo-fan-le-12-pian-jiu-wen-zui-hou-zhi-you-2-pian-neng-dang-gu-jia]] shows how a thematic summary can retain a point while deleting the bullet form, idiom, brevity, punctuation, and other evidence needed for voice imitation.
- Dimension-specific exemplars: [[ru-he-cong-zi-ji-xie-guo-de-wen-zhang-li-ti-lian-xie-zuo-feng-ge-wo-fan-le-12-pian-jiu-wen-zui-hou-zhi-you-2-pian-neng-dang-gu-jia]] selects two posts for structure while drawing candid tone from structurally weaker posts instead of averaging all twelve.
- Style feedback and factual red lines: [[ru-he-cong-zi-ji-xie-guo-de-wen-zhang-li-ti-lian-xie-zuo-feng-ge-wo-fan-le-12-pian-jiu-wen-zui-hou-zhi-you-2-pian-neng-dang-gu-jia]] proposes real-task editing diffs while requiring unresolved data, ownership, commitments, risk classifications, and exact quotations to remain explicit.
- Layered voice processing: [[ai-shi-dai-hai-you-shi-me-zhi-de-zi-ji-xie]] reports that one essay passed from spoken input through AI-mediated transcription and a second polishing pass.
- Human-owned boundary: [[ai-shi-dai-hai-you-shi-me-zhi-de-zi-ji-xie]] accepts AI processing when the core ideas and judgments remain the author's while explicitly questioning how much personal voice survives.
- Purpose-based authorship test: [[wei-he-xie-zuo]] argues that generated diaries and blog posts cannot substitute for writing when the practice is meant to record a life, expose unclear thought, satisfy expression, or create direct human connection.
- Interrogative drafting: [[sspai-110102]] starts from author-written material, uses repeated model questions to surface omitted considerations, and delays outlining until the writer has supplied the substance.
- Style-control limit: [[sspai-110102]] argues that banned phrases do not reliably remove the underlying rhetorical pattern, while its retained chart records frequent praise and conversational stock phrases across aggregated English and Chinese model outputs.
- Delayed revision: [[sspai-110102]] recommends blind model comparison, a roughly three-day cooling-off interval, full rereading, and manual rewriting of unfamiliar expressions before publication.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are practitioner accounts from experienced creators, so their claims are strongest where the author has prior domain knowledge, taste, editorial discipline, and willingness to verify facts. Chen's account also shows a quality boundary: if the user publishes the default draft rather than revising it, AI can produce generic, example-poor, poorly voiced prose. Reorx's 30-minute-versus-five-minute comparison is one estimated experiment rather than repeated timing, and it excludes setup, tool maintenance, source verification, and later human rearrangement. Multi-model agreement is useful evidence but not proof; shared model blind spots, prompt framing, weak source grounding, transcript errors, translation choices, and compression can still create confident distortion. Workflow decomposition and style externalization also require the writer to understand their own craft well enough to specify and judge it. Mai Yang's method is a first-pass self-report: only eight of twelve posts were available verbatim, the two structural exemplars were selected subjectively, the files are not supplied in the source, and the proposed edit-and-diff loop has not yet been completed. Losses Don's questioning, cooling-off, and multi-model-critique routine is likewise a personal method without blinded reader ratings, matched prompts, or factual-error measurements; its model-version rankings are task-specific snapshots. YeungYeah's account supplies no before-and-after drafts, reader evaluation, or independent measure of retained voice, so it establishes the author's acceptance criterion and uncertainty rather than the fidelity of the result. Späti, Woods, Cantrill, and Laixintao add that even accurate or fluent support can become harmful when it replaces the practice the author needs to think, learn, remember, express a voice, or earn reader confidence. Their accounts do not define a universal threshold between assistance, editing, rewriting, and delegated authorship; effects may differ by purpose, genre, disclosure, editing intensity, writer skill, and reader expectations. Laixintao's categorical language does not separately test bounded assistance or establish that every model-mediated workflow defeats memory, expression, or connection. Cantrill's cited developer survey is self-selected, and his Pangram assessment is personal use rather than an independent benchmark, so neither establishes universal reader response or detector reliability. The semantic-ablation article is an opinion argument with no reported experiment, so its type-token-ratio test and causal attribution to decoding and reinforcement learning should be treated as hypotheses; simpler wording can improve communication without necessarily destroying meaning. The critique of "AI written" comments does not remove legitimate concerns about disclosure, originality, plagiarism, accuracy, labor displacement, overproduction, or craft dependency in other publishing contexts.

## What Changed
- Added interrogative drafting: rich author-written material and repeated model questions can expose omissions before outlining.
- Narrowed blacklist-first style control because surface phrase bans may leave deeper rhetorical patterns unchanged.
- Added delayed rereading and manual rephrasing as practitioner safeguards, while preserving their unvalidated, task-specific status.
