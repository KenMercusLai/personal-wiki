## Definition
[[SocialDriverHierarchy]] is [[ChristianLimon]]'s proposed ordering of consumer social-network motivations: utility and tools, content and media, community, then vanity or concern for how others perceive oneself.

## Current Synthesis
The framework claims that each step upward creates stronger network attachment and greater resistance to feature copying. Utility and media can be commoditized, community adds belonging, and vanity adds visible recognition, identity, or local celebrity through likes, views, follows, comments, performance, and association. The cases suggest a useful product question—what social reward does a contribution make legible?—but do not establish that vanity is always dominant, that the four levels are mutually exclusive, or that stronger engagement produces healthier communities or durable businesses.

## Key Claims
- Utility, content, community, and vanity are presented as progressively stronger social-network motivations.
- Visible feedback can convert contribution into affirmation, reputation, or status.
- A familiar media utility can become more compelling when it makes the user the subject of the content.
- Redistribution can express both support for an idea and desired association with it.
- Status motivation can reinforce focused communities, including wellness communities, rather than only entertainment.
- Shifts in cultural norms and incumbent priorities can open space for new forms of self-presentation.

## Evidence
- Hierarchy and defensibility: [[vanity-is-good-a-hierarchy-of-social-drivers-christian-limon-medium]] claims that higher levels create stronger glue and are harder to replicate than utility features.
- Visible feedback: [[vanity-is-good-a-hierarchy-of-social-drivers-christian-limon-medium]] identifies likes, views, follows, retweets, and comments as reinforcement for personal sharing.
- Performer-centered media: [[vanity-is-good-a-hierarchy-of-social-drivers-christian-limon-medium]] uses [[MusicalLy]] and [[Flipagram]] to argue that the user can matter more than the shared song.
- Idea distribution and support: [[vanity-is-good-a-hierarchy-of-social-drivers-christian-limon-medium]] interprets [[Twitter]] posting and reposting as affirmation-seeking and association-seeking, and [[PumpUp]] as a wellness-community application.
- Incumbent and norm shifts: [[vanity-is-good-a-hierarchy-of-social-drivers-christian-limon-medium]] contrasts reduced personal sharing on [[Facebook]] with [[Snapchat]]'s normalization of frequent self-recording and illustrates a 1956–2016 expansion in acceptable celebrity self-presentation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The hierarchy comes from one 2016 practitioner essay and is not supported by controlled comparisons, retention data, network-structure evidence, or business outcomes. Utility, content, community, identity, reputation, creativity, and belonging can coexist rather than form a strict ladder. “Vanity” may also collapse distinct motives—self-expression, affiliation, feedback, reputation, and extrinsic status—and stronger status loops can create comparison pressure, manipulation, low-quality contribution, or exclusion. The dated platform examples do not establish current product behavior.

## What Changed
- Created the framework and preserved its four-level ordering.
- Qualified the claim that vanity is universally the strongest or most defensible driver.
- Distinguished user status motivation from [[VanityMetrics]] as an analytical measurement failure.
