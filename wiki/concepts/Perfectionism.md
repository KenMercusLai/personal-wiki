## Definition
[[Perfectionism]] is the demand that work meet unrealistically complete or error-free standards, often tying performance to self-worth and making ordinary uncertainty or developmental shortcomings feel unacceptable.

## Current Synthesis
The sources show two related perfectionism loops. In the impostor-phenomenon account, fear of inadequate work produces procrastination or excessive preparation. When extreme effort ends in success, the person credits the anxiety and ritual rather than competence, making self-punishing effort feel necessary next time.

A second loop appears in creative projects. A creator can idealize a future work, its reception, and the identity it will confer until the gap between that imagined result and present ability becomes too threatening to confront. This [[IdeaDebt]] grows through lore, research, discussion, and planning that do not yield a finished artifact. The longer the ideal is protected from reality, the harder an ordinary imperfect start can feel.

Both sources favor bounded experiments over reassurance alone. A person can define “good enough,” reduce preparation gradually, share an imperfect draft, finish work on a recurring deadline, and observe whether feared failure occurs. Repeated completion can build skill, but conscious abandonment is also legitimate when an old project no longer fits current priorities. Neither response means ignoring consequential quality standards.

## Key Claims
- Perfectionistic fear can produce avoidance, excessive preparation, or long-term project fantasizing without proportionate making.
- Success after extreme effort may reinforce the false belief that anxiety was necessary.
- Idealizing finished work can turn the normal gap between taste and current skill into a reason not to begin.
- Gradual reductions in effort rituals and bounded finished work can test feared predictions.
- “Good enough” is a context-sensitive stopping rule, not a rejection of care or competence.
- Deliberately abandoning a stale project can be healthier than preserving it solely to protect an earlier identity.

## Evidence
- Effort cycle: [[feel-like-a-fraud]] reports procrastination and overpreparation as common responses to perfectionistic standards and says later success can be misattributed to the preceding anxiety.
- Practical experiments: [[feel-like-a-fraud]] recommends reducing preparation incrementally, sharing imperfect drafts, doing tasks well enough, and deliberately registering success.
- Idealized-project loop: [[imagining-your-future-projects-is-holding-you-back]] defines idea debt as imagined creative achievement and reward growing while little artifact-making occurs.
- Developmental gap: [[imagining-your-future-projects-is-holding-you-back]] uses Ira Glass's account of taste outpacing early ability to recommend a high volume of deadline-bound finished work.
- Release alternative: [[imagining-your-future-projects-is-holding-you-back]] argues that creators can consciously drop projects that belong to earlier priorities instead of treating persistence as mandatory.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are secondary or practitioner accounts and do not quantify prevalence or intervention effects. Procrastination, overwork, and stalled projects may arise from depression, anxiety, executive-function difficulty, workload, unclear requirements, discrimination, material insecurity, missing skills, or rational risk assessment rather than perfectionism alone. Planning and incubation can be productive parts of complex creative work, while safety-critical, ethical, professional, or expensive tasks can demand exacting preparation. Volume does not guarantee learning without feedback and reflection, and difficulty alone does not show that a project should be abandoned.

## What Changed
- Added idealized future projects and creator identity as a second perfectionism loop beyond impostor-related effort rituals.
- Added recurring finished work as a way to tolerate and gradually close the gap between taste and skill.
- Added deliberate project abandonment as a valid alternative when continued commitment reflects an earlier identity rather than present value.
