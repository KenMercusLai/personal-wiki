## Definition
[[Perfectionism]] is the demand that work meet unrealistically complete or error-free standards, often tying performance to self-worth and making ordinary uncertainty feel unacceptable.

## Current Synthesis
In the source, perfectionism is a maintenance mechanism for [[ImposterSyndrome]]. Fear of producing inadequate work can lead either to procrastination or to spending far more time preparing than the task requires. When the result succeeds, the person may attribute it to the anxiety and extreme effort rather than to competence, making the same ritual feel necessary next time.

The proposed response is experimental rather than purely reassuring. A person can set a “good enough” stopping rule, shorten preparation gradually, share a draft before it feels perfectly polished, and deliberately register or celebrate success. These practices test whether acceptable outcomes truly depend on self-punishing effort while preserving room for legitimate skill development and quality standards.

## Key Claims
- Perfectionistic fear can produce both avoidance and excessive preparation.
- Success after extreme effort may reinforce the false belief that anxiety was necessary.
- Global self-condemnation should be separated from a realistic inventory of strengths and development needs.
- Gradual reductions in effort rituals can test feared predictions more tolerably than abrupt change.
- “Good enough” is a context-sensitive stopping rule, not a rejection of care or competence.

## Evidence
- Dual response: [[feel-like-a-fraud]] reports procrastination and overpreparation as two common reactions to perfectionistic standards.
- Reinforcement cycle: [[feel-like-a-fraud]] says successful outcomes can be misattributed to the preceding anxiety and self-torture.
- Practical experiments: [[feel-like-a-fraud]] recommends cutting preparation time incrementally, sharing imperfect drafts, doing tasks well enough, and celebrating success.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is a secondary magazine synthesis and does not quantify the prevalence of either response or the effects of the proposed practices. Some tasks have legitimately exacting safety, ethical, or professional standards, and underpreparation is not always irrational. Procrastination and overwork may also arise from depression, anxiety, executive-function difficulty, workload, unclear requirements, discrimination, or material insecurity rather than perfectionism alone.

## What Changed
- Created a source-scoped synthesis of perfectionism as a self-reinforcing effort ritual in impostor feelings.
- Distinguished good-enough experimentation from indiscriminate lowering of standards.
