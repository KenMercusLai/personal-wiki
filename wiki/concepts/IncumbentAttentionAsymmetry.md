## Definition
[[IncumbentAttentionAsymmetry]] is the competitive condition in which a market is survival-critical to a focused specialist but only one of many product lines or priorities for a larger company, giving the specialist greater urgency and concentrated attention despite fewer resources.

## Current Synthesis
[[BrettFox]] presents attention asymmetry as a more credible defense than claiming a giant cannot enter. Large companies can assign excellent people, copy features, price aggressively, and bring substantial resources; the startup's advantage is that every deal and decision can matter to its survival while the same market competes with the incumbent's larger revenue pools and internal priorities. Fox's [[Maxim]] examples make the mechanism reversible rather than inherently pro-startup: Maxim won when it was the focused challenger against larger semiconductor companies, but later failed to unseat AMCC and Vitesse in communications because those specialists depended on the market and Maxim did not. The strategic implication is to choose and defend a segment the startup can make central before expanding, while recognizing that attacking an incumbent's core business may eliminate the attention advantage by provoking a concentrated response.

## Key Claims
- The relevant advantage is concentrated commitment, not an assumption that the incumbent lacks talent or the ability to copy.
- Focus can improve speed, reduce political drag, and make every customer opportunity consequential to the specialist.
- Attention asymmetry is positional: a growing company can lose it when it enters a specialist's core market as a secondary line.
- A narrow, neglected entry segment is safer than attacking the revenue pool the incumbent is organized to defend.
- Product superiority and lower pricing may still fail when customer ties, execution, and defender commitment outweigh those advantages.

## Evidence
- Investor response: [[brett-fox-when-vcs-say-giants-will-enter-your-market]] says founders should acknowledge that entry cannot be prevented and then explain how they will compete.
- Challenger focus: [[brett-fox-when-vcs-say-giants-will-enter-your-market]] attributes Maxim's early wins against larger semiconductor companies to focus, execution, and attention to every deal.
- Reversal with scale: [[brett-fox-when-vcs-say-giants-will-enter-your-market]] says Maxim later failed to displace AMCC and Vitesse because communications was existential to those companies but only another Maxim product line.
- Segment choice: [[brett-fox-when-vcs-say-giants-will-enter-your-market]] recommends first dominating smaller segments outside the incumbent's main revenue focus, then expanding.
- Core-market retaliation: [[brett-fox-when-vcs-say-giants-will-enter-your-market]] recounts Fox opposing an investment aimed at roughly 80% of a major competitor's business because that target would provoke a full defense.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is grounded in one founder-investor's retrospective semiconductor anecdotes, not comparative outcome data. Focus and urgency do not neutralize capital, distribution, patents, switching costs, ecosystem control, customer relationships, regulation, or network effects. Fox's account does not name the failed portfolio company, quantify the market segments, or independently establish that Maxim's products and prices were superior. The liquidation example supports caution about core-market attacks but does not prove that incumbent retaliation caused the failure.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to distinguish survival-critical specialist focus from a diversified incumbent's optional product-line attention.
- Made the advantage reversible: a former challenger can become the distracted entrant when it expands into another specialist's core market.
