Cofounder alignment rituals are recurring, deliberately informal conversations that keep founders synchronized around both company direction and the personal context beneath it. The "How-do-you-feel dinners" source argues that founding teams can appear to be arguing about titles, responsibilities, product strategy, or hiring while actually carrying unspoken fears, envy, overload, or mismatched life goals.

The described format uses three prompts: personal life, work, and what the company should do differently. Each founder answers one prompt at a time while others listen and ask clarifying questions. Keeping the setting equal and casual matters because the goal is not a chaired decision meeting; it is to make invisible context discussable before strategic debate starts.

The order is the mechanism. Personal life comes first because work tensions often encode personal constraints: travel can mean time away from family, R&D focus can mean access to meaningful work, quality ownership can mean fear of being alone with risk, and fundraising stress can mean anxiety about runway. Once those meanings are visible, proposed changes can be judged against the underlying need rather than defended as status claims.

The ritual also has a hard diagnostic use. The source's closing anecdote shows that the conversation may reveal irreconcilable divergence about product purpose, near-term direction, and founder motivations. In that case, the ritual does not save the company; it helps the founders recognize the mismatch before another year of work damages the relationship more.

## Practice Signals

- Founders can explain their current personal constraints and motivations, not only their product opinions.
- Each person gets uninterrupted time, and others are limited to clarifying questions before debate.
- The group discusses desired changes only after personal and work context are surfaced.
- Notes capture actions, but no role such as chair or secretary outranks the rest of the founders.
- The cadence adapts to life constraints, such as turning evening dinners into lunches when founders have small children.
