## Definition
[[WebCentralization]] is the concentration of web traffic, discovery, publishing leverage, commerce, hosting, browser choice, and user attention inside a small number of large platform networks rather than across many independently reachable sites and user agents.

## Current Synthesis
[[AndreStaltz]] argues that the Web can lose relevance even while internet usage grows. In his view, the pre-2014 Web approximated [[TimBernersLee]]'s multilateral publishing vision: many parties could publish and consume information without depending on one gatekeeper. After 2014, mobile usage, publisher dependence on Google and Facebook traffic, Amazon's commerce scale, cloud concentration, proprietary app surfaces, and AI/assistant strategies made independent websites less central to ordinary internet life. The Firefox campaign adds browser share and Google's advertising incentives; the Chrome retrospective adds the mechanism by which product leadership can become infrastructure power. Architecture, developer tooling, extensions, distribution, and enterprise support expanded Chrome's utility, but dominant adoption encouraged Chrome-first implementation and made Chromium the practical base even for competitors such as Edge.

## Key Claims
- The Web's health is not measured only by user count or number of websites; traffic control and dependency matter.
- Google and Facebook gained enough discovery and traffic influence that media sites became dependent on them for page views.
- Amazon adds a commerce and cloud dimension because market leadership, apps, Echo, and cloud hosting reduce the practical independence of smaller sites.
- Browser-market concentration matters because a dominant browser can become the default path into a dominant advertising and search company.
- Product and ecosystem advantages can turn browser share into de facto standards power without a formal grant of control.
- Centralization can preserve user convenience while weakening anonymity, equal access, small-business independence, and the ability to route around account bans.
- The Web may decay gradually by becoming less necessary, not by suddenly vanishing.

## Evidence
- Traffic concentration: [[andre-staltz-the-web-began-dying-in-2014-heres-how]] says Google and Facebook had direct influence over more than 70% of internet traffic in the article's cited frame.
- Publisher dependence: [[andre-staltz-the-web-began-dying-in-2014-heres-how]] says media traffic shifted from broad web referrals and search toward dependence on Facebook and Google.
- Commerce concentration: [[andre-staltz-the-web-began-dying-in-2014-heres-how]] frames Amazon as the e-commerce company pursuing market leadership rather than ordinary profit optimization.
- Browser concentration: [[browse-against-the-machine-the-official-unofficial-firefox-blog-medium]] says Chrome had about four times Firefox's desktop browser market share in the cited period and connects Chrome to Google's advertising business.
- Browser-choice counterweight: [[browse-against-the-machine-the-official-unofficial-firefox-blog-medium]] presents Firefox and Mozilla as independent alternatives meant to keep web power from concentrating further.
- Platform formation: [[from-0-to-70-market-share-how-google-chrome-ate-the-internet]] links Chrome's clean-slate architecture, Chromium, extensions, mobile reach, and enterprise tooling to its historical share growth.
- Implementation concentration: [[from-0-to-70-market-share-how-google-chrome-ate-the-internet]] says developers increasingly optimized first or only for Chrome, while Edge's Chromium migration reduced independent engine diversity.
- Identity integration: [[from-0-to-70-market-share-how-google-chrome-ate-the-internet]] uses the Gmail/Chrome sign-in controversy to show how a dominant browser can connect navigation, identity, and advertising interests.
- Open-Web ideal: [[andre-staltz-the-web-began-dying-in-2014-heres-how]] invokes Tim Berners-Lee's multilateral publishing vision as the contrast case.
- Gradual decay: [[andre-staltz-the-web-began-dying-in-2014-heres-how]] argues that the Web's loss of necessity would resemble technological obsolescence rather than immediate disappearance.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are two 2017 arguments and a 2019 retrospective. Their traffic figures, market shares, platform shares, and net-neutrality implications are source-scoped and should not be read as current measurements. Staltz emphasizes Google, Facebook, and Amazon more than Apple, Microsoft, TikTok, regulation, protocol renewal, decentralized systems, or later AI search interfaces. The Firefox source is campaign positioning from a competitor, while the Chrome history contains chronology errors and does not isolate architecture, defaults, promotion, bundling, brand, or incumbent failure as causes. Dominant share and open-source stewardship create influence, but do not by themselves prove unilateral control of every standard or hostile intent.

## What Changed
