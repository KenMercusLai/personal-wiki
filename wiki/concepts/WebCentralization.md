## Definition
[[WebCentralization]] is the concentration of web traffic, discovery, publishing leverage, commerce, hosting, and user attention inside a small number of large platform networks rather than across many independently reachable sites.

## Current Synthesis
[[AndreStaltz]] argues that the Web can lose relevance even while internet usage grows. In his view, the pre-2014 Web approximated [[TimBernersLee]]'s multilateral publishing vision: many parties could publish and consume information without depending on one gatekeeper. After 2014, mobile usage, publisher dependence on Google and Facebook traffic, Amazon's commerce scale, cloud concentration, proprietary app surfaces, and AI/assistant strategies made independent websites less central to ordinary internet life.

## Key Claims
- The Web's health is not measured only by user count or number of websites; traffic control and dependency matter.
- Google and Facebook gained enough discovery and traffic influence that media sites became dependent on them for page views.
- Amazon adds a commerce and cloud dimension because market leadership, apps, Echo, and cloud hosting reduce the practical independence of smaller sites.
- Centralization can preserve user convenience while weakening anonymity, equal access, small-business independence, and the ability to route around account bans.
- The Web may decay gradually by becoming less necessary, not by suddenly vanishing.

## Evidence
- Traffic concentration: [[andre-staltz-the-web-began-dying-in-2014-heres-how]] says Google and Facebook had direct influence over more than 70% of internet traffic in the article's cited frame.
- Publisher dependence: [[andre-staltz-the-web-began-dying-in-2014-heres-how]] says media traffic shifted from broad web referrals and search toward dependence on Facebook and Google.
- Commerce concentration: [[andre-staltz-the-web-began-dying-in-2014-heres-how]] frames Amazon as the e-commerce company pursuing market leadership rather than ordinary profit optimization.
- Open-Web ideal: [[andre-staltz-the-web-began-dying-in-2014-heres-how]] invokes Tim Berners-Lee's multilateral publishing vision as the contrast case.
- Gradual decay: [[andre-staltz-the-web-began-dying-in-2014-heres-how]] argues that the Web's loss of necessity would resemble technological obsolescence rather than immediate disappearance.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The article is a 2017 argument and forecast. Its traffic figures, platform shares, and net-neutrality implications are source-scoped and should not be read as current measurements without later sources. It also emphasizes Google, Facebook, and Amazon more than Apple, Microsoft, TikTok, regulation, protocol renewal, decentralized systems, or later AI search interfaces.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to capture Staltz's open-Web decline thesis.
