## Definition
[[FounderOriginStories]] are retrospective accounts that explain how a company began, often by selecting a memorable idea or founding moment from a much longer sequence of skill building, failed attempts, constraints, and adaptation.

## Current Synthesis
The sources argue that a company's legal, branded, or idea-level beginning is often the wrong beginning for learning. A clean story such as [[ReedHastings]] conceiving [[Netflix]] after a late fee is memorable because it compresses causality into one insight, but that compression can make aspiring founders overvalue the idea and undervalue prior capability, persistence, relationships, market response, and contingency.

[[SamWalton]] provides the extended historical counterexample. By the time the first [[Walmart]] opened, he was 44 and had operated retail stores for 15 years. His more instructive history includes franchise learning, pricing and sourcing experiments, a catastrophic lease mistake, forced relocation, failed side bets, disaster recovery, capital rejection, and incremental improvement.

A complementary account from [[JamieSiminoff]] shows how a memorable problem-led origin can be real but incomplete. DoorBot was not one of his 300 written ideas; he says he built it because he could not hear the doorbell from his garage. That immediate prompt became actionable after childhood making and selling, factory work, six earlier startups, failed fundraising, and investor-network formation. [[SharkTank]]'s rejection and the sales response to the broadcast further show that origin stories often contain several different thresholds—problem discovery, prototype, commercial validation, financing, survival, and scale—rather than one decisive beginning.

## Key Claims
- A memorable corporate founding moment may be the endpoint of a long capability-building period rather than the true beginning.
- Idea-first narratives can misallocate attention from execution psychology, accumulated skill, and persistence toward finding one supposedly decisive insight.
- Mistakes, rejected experiments, recoveries, and routine improvement are causally important even when they do not fit a clean story.
- Historical learning improves when the learner asks which starting point exposes the relevant capability formation.
- A genuine problem-discovery moment can matter without being a sufficient explanation of later company formation or success.
- Contemporary obscurity can coexist with later importance, so later fame should not be projected backward onto early decisions.

## Evidence
- Compressed myth: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] disputes the familiar Netflix late-fee account and argues that it teaches learners to wait for a great idea.
- Hidden apprenticeship: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] says Walton had 15 years of retail operating experience before opening the first Walmart.
- Narrative limbo: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] recounts a lost lease, relocation, a premature shopping-mall experiment, tornado recovery, repeated improvement, and financing rejection before Walmart.
- Retrospective fame: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] uses brief 1969 and 1970 newspaper mentions to show how little contemporary attention Walmart initially received.
- Problem plus apprenticeship: [[jamie-siminoff-ring-dfjvc-medium]] says a garage doorbell problem prompted DoorBot, while childhood businesses, hands-on making, six prior startups, and investor outreach preceded it.
- Multiple thresholds: [[jamie-siminoff-ring-dfjvc-medium]] separates product creation, failed on-air fundraising, post-broadcast sales, engineering expansion, product-line growth, and later venture financing.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence consists of an argumentative historical essay and an edited founder interview. It does not show that every founder account is false, that memorable moments never matter, or that persistence and prior experience are sufficient for success. Siminoff's garage problem is evidence that a specific triggering event can be meaningful; the qualification is that it sits inside a longer causal sequence. Both extended histories can still be selected retrospectively, so they need independent evidence and should preserve market, capital, team, distribution, timing, and survivorship effects rather than replacing one heroic myth with another.

## What Changed
- Added the distinction between a real triggering problem and a complete explanation of company development.
- Added multiple thresholds—prototype, demand, financing, survival, and scale—to the analysis of what counts as a beginning.
