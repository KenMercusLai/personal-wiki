## Definition
[[FounderOriginStories]] are retrospective accounts that explain how a company began, often by selecting a memorable idea or founding moment from a much longer sequence of skill building, failed attempts, constraints, and adaptation.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that a company's legal or branded beginning is often the wrong beginning for learning. A clean story such as [[ReedHastings]] conceiving [[Netflix]] after a late fee is memorable because it compresses causality into one insight, but that compression can make aspiring founders overvalue the idea and undervalue the prior capabilities and persistence that made execution possible.

[[SamWalton]] provides the extended counterexample. By the time the first [[Walmart]] opened, he was 44 and had operated retail stores for 15 years. His more instructive history includes franchise learning, pricing and sourcing experiments, a catastrophic lease mistake, forced relocation, failed side bets, disaster recovery, capital rejection, and incremental improvement. The origin story becomes more useful when it is expanded backward to include that "narrative limbo."

## Key Claims
- A memorable corporate founding moment may be the endpoint of a long capability-building period rather than the true beginning.
- Idea-first narratives can misallocate attention from execution psychology, accumulated skill, and persistence toward finding one supposedly decisive insight.
- Mistakes, rejected experiments, recoveries, and routine improvement are causally important even when they do not fit a clean story.
- Historical learning improves when the learner asks which starting point exposes the relevant capability formation.
- Contemporary obscurity can coexist with later importance, so later fame should not be projected backward onto early decisions.

## Evidence
- Compressed myth: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] disputes the familiar Netflix late-fee account and argues that it teaches learners to wait for a great idea.
- Hidden apprenticeship: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] says Walton had 15 years of retail operating experience before opening the first Walmart.
- Narrative limbo: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] recounts a lost lease, relocation, a premature shopping-mall experiment, tornado recovery, repeated improvement, and financing rejection before Walmart.
- Retrospective fame: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] uses brief 1969 and 1970 newspaper mentions to show how little contemporary attention Walmart initially received.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is an argumentative essay built mainly from one extended Walton case and one asserted correction to the Netflix story. It does not show that every founder account is false, that memorable moments never matter, or that persistence alone produces success. Expanded histories can also become selective narratives; they need independent evidence and should preserve market, capital, team, timing, and survivorship effects rather than replacing one heroic myth with another.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to distinguish branded company beginnings from the longer period in which founder capability develops.
