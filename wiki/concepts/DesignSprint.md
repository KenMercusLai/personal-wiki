## Definition
[[DesignSprint]] is a short, structured product-design process that turns a bounded question into explicit assumptions, alternative concepts, a realistic prototype, and—canonically—behavioral user evidence within roughly one working week.

## Current Synthesis
The sources present the Design Sprint as an alternative to front-loading a bounded project with weeks of interviews, personas, empathy maps, benchmarking, and documentation. Its advantage is not that assumptions disappear, but that the team makes them inspectable through goals, questions, maps, examples, sketches, decisions, and prototypes before production. The Oak case shows these moves can work remotely when shared artifacts carry more context and alignment is checked repeatedly. It also clarifies the process boundary: a four-week engagement that expands scope and ends in client approval without documented user testing may use sprint techniques, but it does not supply the complete rapid evidence cycle claimed for a canonical sprint.

## Key Claims
- A sprint can reach a high-fidelity prototype and user test in four to five days.
- Rapid tangibility makes assumptions observable sooner than a documentation-heavy process.
- Starting without a brief, benchmarking, or prior research can work when the product question is sufficiently bounded.
- Sprint evidence comes from behavior with the prototype rather than the existence of design artifacts.
- The method does not remove the need for broader discovery in ambiguous or high-consequence domains.
- Remote execution depends on explicit shared artifacts, staged decisions, and more frequent alignment.
- Client buy-in reduces delivery uncertainty but does not substitute for representative-user evidence.

## Evidence
- Timebox and output: [[user-research-is-overrated-muzli-design-inspiration]] describes a four-to-five-day process ending in realistic prototype testing.
- Consulting comparison: [[user-research-is-overrated-muzli-design-inspiration]] reports that AJ&Smart's five-day client sprint advanced further than its prior six-to-eight-week package.
- Learning mechanism: [[user-research-is-overrated-muzli-design-inspiration]] argues that observed use reveals comprehension and rejection more directly than research documentation.
- Applicability boundary: [[user-research-is-overrated-muzli-design-inspiration]] preserves up-front research when the underlying problem is unclear.
- Remote sequence: [[from-idea-to-app-store-a-design-sprint-case-study-ux-planet]] documents expert interview, goal and questions, mapping, lightning demos, sketches, voting, prototypes, first-week alignment, screen production, and handoff.
- Coordination controls: [[from-idea-to-app-store-a-design-sprint-case-study-ux-planet]] recommends highly explicit notes, extra check-ins, stronger inspiration-sharing, and a shared toolbox.
- Validation gap: [[from-idea-to-app-store-a-design-sprint-case-study-ux-planet]] reports client feedback and launch recognition but no representative-user test or product outcome metric.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence consists of consultancy retrospectives without matched projects, independent outcome measures, or long-term product results. A five-day test can reveal reactions to the chosen hypothesis but may miss whether the team chose the right problem, recruited the right users, represented a complex workflow, or accounted for accessibility, safety, organizational constraints, and rare failure modes. The Oak case also shows how the label can stretch: scope expanded from one screen to a total redesign, design handoff took four weeks, and no user test is described. Prototype fidelity and stakeholder enthusiasm can therefore create false confidence if simulated behavior or client preference differs from representative use.
