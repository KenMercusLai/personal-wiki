## Definition
[[CommunityGovernanceDebt]] is the accumulated set of promises, precedents, informal decision rights, policy ambiguities, and trust deficits that makes later platform change costly even when the product or business needs to adapt.

## Current Synthesis
LiveJournal shows governance debt forming when a young product relies on founder responsiveness, volunteer judgment, personalized support, and promises to consult users before important changes. These practices create belonging and local legitimacy, but they can also teach power users that historical defaults are rights and that every decision is negotiable. Once commercialization, scale, safety, or competition demands change, unresolved authority and weak trust turn advertising, moderation, interface evolution, and new feed mechanics into identity conflicts.

Kickstarter supplies an earlier and more constructive stage of the same mechanism. Close support, selective approval, exemplary projects, and editorial curation helped creators internalize useful norms and teach one another. Yet community use moved toward large hardware launches, raising delivery-accountability and mission questions that self-guidance could not settle. Governance debt is therefore not community formation itself: it appears when examples, permissions, promises, and user-created practice accumulate without a credible account of which values are fixed, who decides contested boundaries, how policies can change, and how affected participants can challenge decisions.

## Key Claims
- Early responsiveness and volunteer governance can create community value while also establishing implicit decision rights.
- Early examples and curation can seed healthy norms, but they also establish precedents about what the platform welcomes and rewards.
- Promises about advertising, consultation, personalized support, or permissible content become expensive constraints when no revision process is defined.
- Literal rule enforcement fails when reports are strategic, context changes meaning, or policy categories are too crude.
- Power-user resistance becomes more severe when owners treat attachment as obstruction instead of evidence about the product's identity.
- Governance debt is repaid through explicit authority, principled standards, transparent change processes, consistent enforcement, and credible preservation of core community value.
- Community self-regulation cannot decide every conflict involving safety, accountability, commercial pressure, or mission drift.

## Evidence
- Informal authority: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] says volunteer support staff often set policy because the small paid team was occupied with keeping the site running.
- Promise lock-in: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] reports commitments to consult the community and avoid advertising that later conflicted with Six Apart's commercial requirements.
- Context failure: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] describes “Nipplegate,” where a malicious reporter exploited a nudity rule and the team applied it to breastfeeding icons despite recognizing the different context.
- Trust breakdown: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] quotes former staff describing antagonism and distrust between emotionally invested power users and product decision-makers.
- Adaptation cost: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] says LiveJournal delayed a news-feed-like feature for fear of backlash while Facebook changed social privacy expectations.
- Constructive seeding: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] says close support and exemplary early campaigns gave later Kickstarter creators a guidebook that peers extended through advice and workshops.
- Mission boundary: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] says hardware growth forced Kickstarter to weigh user-led creative practice against the risk that product launches would crowd out art, theater, and music.
- Accountability boundary: [[employee-1-kickstarter]] connects very large overfunded projects and delivery trouble to creator resources, reward caps, and demands for transparency.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
User resistance can be rational when owners seek extractive monetization, weaken privacy, erase subcultures, or break prior commitments without consent. The sources do not show that faster change, advertising, stricter policy, a public feed, or stronger Kickstarter restrictions would have produced better outcomes; some interventions could have destroyed the qualities users valued. “Debt” should therefore describe accumulated rigidity and unclear governance, not dismiss dissent, community invention, or disagreement with management. Nor should it convert every precedent into a liability: stable commitments can be valuable trust infrastructure when their scope and revision process are credible.

## What Changed
- Added Kickstarter as a case where deliberate norm seeding worked, but user-led hardware growth and delivery risk still required explicit mission and accountability decisions.
- Distinguished valuable stable commitments from debt caused by ambiguous authority and revision processes.
