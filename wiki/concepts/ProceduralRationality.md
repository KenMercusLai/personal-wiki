## Definition
[[ProceduralRationality]] is the use of orderly, rational-looking procedures as proof that work is being handled properly, even when the procedure's substantive contribution is unclear.

## Current Synthesis
Barnett applies [[MaxWeber]]'s procedural-versus-substantive rationality distinction to modern meeting culture. Scheduled meetings, committees, and recurring calendar blocks can signal that work is being managed rationally, but the signal can detach from actual purpose when people schedule first and ask why later. Procedural rationality therefore explains why meetings persist as legitimate organizational behavior even when they lack a clear problem, agenda, decision, or output.

The Rands in Repose source supplies a more constructive boundary. A recurring [[StaffMeetings|staff meeting]] can be substantive when a real coordination problem warrants it, the agenda is tied to question-producing metrics and participant-supplied work, the group records and shares its output, and leaders repeatedly test whether the original need still exists. These practices do not prove value, but they expose a recurring forum to evidence and expiration rather than treating its place on the calendar as self-justification.

## Key Claims
- Procedures can symbolize rational work without guaranteeing useful work.
- Recurring meetings are a common organizational form of procedural rationality.
- Meeting-first behavior can turn procedure into a solution looking for a problem.
- Substantive rationality requires asking why the meeting exists before protecting it as a calendar norm.
- Informal interaction can avoid some procedural drag because it ends when participants stop finding value.
- Structured recurring meetings can resist procedural drift when they retain a concrete coordination need, observable outputs, participant ownership, and explicit reassessment.

## Evidence
- Weber frame: [[bill-barnett-delete-all-meetings]] says meetings exemplify Weber's procedural rationality.
- Symbolic work: [[bill-barnett-delete-all-meetings]] argues meetings show that work is being done rationally whether or not they help get things done.
- Agenda inversion: [[bill-barnett-delete-all-meetings]] says people often set meetings and then decide what should be on the agenda.
- Informal contrast: [[bill-barnett-delete-all-meetings]] argues informal chats continue only while they matter and can stop when value disappears.
- Start conditions: [[gossip-rumors-and-lies-rands-in-repose]] ties a staff meeting to interdependence, growth, management layers, avoidable coordination failures, or organizational disruption.
- Continuing value test: [[gossip-rumors-and-lies-rands-in-repose]] says uninformative metrics, an exhausted agenda, or a vanished founding reason should prompt reconsideration.
- Observable output: [[gossip-rumors-and-lies-rands-in-repose]] recommends recorded actions and broadly shared notes, making the meeting's substance visible beyond attendance itself.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Barnett treats meeting overload polemically, while the Rands in Repose source is prescriptive and supplies no comparative outcome evidence for its proposed format. The useful distinction is not formal versus informal in every case, but procedure that serves a known purpose versus procedure protected because it looks rational. Agendas, minutes, metrics, and open-topic segments can themselves become performative artifacts; substantive value still depends on better decisions, follow-through, learning, and resolution of the underlying problem.
