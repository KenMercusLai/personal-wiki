## Definition
[[CognitiveCuriosity]] is curiosity aimed at understanding how and why something works, in contrast to curiosity that only seeks surface novelty or gossip.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats curiosity as the directional force behind creation. Limboy distinguishes perceptual curiosity, which asks "what happened," from cognitive curiosity, which asks "how" and "why" and therefore produces deeper project directions. High standards determine how far a creator pushes the vector, but curiosity determines where the vector points. In that framing, a lack of curiosity is often a bigger obstacle to creating than lack of tools.

## Key Claims
- Curiosity is one of the main drivers of sustainable creation.
- Cognitive curiosity is more useful for making than surface-level novelty seeking.
- Asking how and why helps creators find mechanisms, consequences, and project directions.
- Hard work without curiosity can produce force without direction.
- High standards and curiosity reinforce each other: curiosity finds worthwhile problems, standards push the work further.

## Evidence
- Obstacle framing: [[blog-limboy-make-good-new-things]] lists lack of curiosity, perfectionism, and scattered energy as three major barriers to creation.
- Curiosity distinction: [[blog-limboy-make-good-new-things]] separates perceptual curiosity about what happened from cognitive curiosity about how and why.
- Direction metaphor: [[blog-limboy-make-good-new-things]] quotes the idea that hard work is vector magnitude while curiosity is direction.
- Standards link: [[blog-limboy-make-good-new-things]] says cognitive curiosity gives creation direction while high standards determine the vector's length.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source gives a motivational model rather than a psychological taxonomy. Perceptual curiosity can still seed useful work, especially in journalism, ethnography, product discovery, or art; the article's stronger claim is that mechanism-seeking curiosity is more reliable for sustained creation.
