## Definition
[[SkillStacking]] is the career strategy of combining several useful skills into a distinctive profile rather than trying to become world-class at a single skill.

## Current Synthesis
The sources present skill stacking as the practical individual version of differentiation strategy. Instead of competing head-to-head on one crowded axis, a person can combine ordinary competence in complementary skills so their work becomes harder to compare. Foroux borrows the idea from [[ScottAdams]] and applies it to career positioning: a surgeon who writes and speaks well, or a specialist who can also sell and communicate, can become distinctive without claiming to be the absolute best at the core profession.

The junior-developer source turns that abstract mechanism into a later-career transition case. The author is technically junior but not inexperienced as a worker: prior management, communication with bosses, public speaking, teaching, and confidence from age and life experience become complementary assets around programming.

## Key Claims
- Skill stacking makes differentiation achievable because multiple "good" skills can create a rare combination.
- The strategy shifts career competition from single-axis excellence toward distinctive category fit.
- Writing, public speaking, and selling are high-leverage stack components because they make expertise easier to communicate, lead, and monetize.
- Skill combinations are context-specific: a skill becomes valuable when it is uncommon in a particular field.
- Career changers can stack prior professional experience, communication, teaching, and public speaking onto new technical skill.

## Evidence
- Practical mechanism: [[being-different-beats-being-better-darius-foroux]] says the easiest way to be different is to combine skills that are new in a given area.
- Merely good is enough: [[being-different-beats-being-better-darius-foroux]] cites [[ScottAdams]] for the claim that market value can rise through being good, not extraordinary, at more than one skill.
- Communication stack: [[being-different-beats-being-better-darius-foroux]] names writing, public speaking, and selling as broadly useful ways to stand out.
- Distinctive specialist: [[being-different-beats-being-better-darius-foroux]] uses [[AtulGawande]] as an example of a surgeon whose writing and public communication make him unusually legible.
- Older junior developer case: [[being-a-junior-developer-at-30-by]] says the author had previously managed more than 60 people, then entered tech as a junior developer.
- Public communication: [[being-a-junior-developer-at-30-by]] says the author became a speaker in Vienna, taught others, and spoke at a conference in front of more than 1000 people; the inspected stage image supports the large-audience public-speaking claim.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are advice-oriented and autobiographical; they do not measure how much each additional skill changes career outcomes. Skill stacking can also become shallow if it substitutes surface versatility for core competence, or if the selected skills are not actually scarce or valued in the target field. The junior-developer case also shows that stacked experience does not erase insecurity or the need to practice the new core skill.
