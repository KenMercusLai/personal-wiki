## Definition
[[PrivacyProtectionResourceInequality]] is the unequal ability to protect, repair, understand, or strategically manage personal data because privacy self-defense requires unevenly distributed time, money, skills, tools, and advice.

## Current Synthesis
Privacy protection itself is a resource-intensive activity. Low-income users may be more concerned about scams, harassment, financial-data loss, and unfair targeting, but they often have less access to authoritative advice, paid record-scrubbing services, legal help, and the time or skill needed to adopt protections. Thompson's proposal to disclose the processed profiles produced by [[DataFactories]] would improve the information boundary, but it also creates interpretation, correction, complaint, and switching work. Transparency is therefore an enabling condition, not a substitute for usable controls, accountability, or institutional protection.

## Key Claims
- Privacy and security behaviors impose time, cognitive, skill, and money costs.
- Low-income people can be both more exposed to concrete privacy harms and less equipped to mitigate them.
- Advice quality is stratified: higher-income users more often access authoritative workplace or professional security guidance.
- Reputation repair and data cleanup can become luxury services.
- Policy and corporate accountability become more important when individual self-defense is unrealistic.
- Transparency remedies remain unequal when acting on disclosed information requires scarce time, expertise, money, legal help, or a viable substitute service.

## Evidence
- Survey concern: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] reports that lowest-income households were more concerned than highest-income households about unknown data collection, financial-information loss, harassment, and scams.
- Learning-resource gap: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] says low-income respondents wanted to learn privacy strategies but felt they had less access to learning resources.
- Time-resource tradeoff: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] quotes [[MaryMadden]] on the tradeoff between resources and ability to protect information.
- Security usability: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] cites [[ElissaRedmiles]] on higher costs of two-factor authentication for low-SES and low-skill users.
- Paid repair: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] contrasts wealthy users' reputation-management options with low-income users' exposure to data-broker records and scams.
- Output disclosure: [[data-factories-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] proposes giving users access to inferred and matched profiles, not merely the raw information they submitted.
- Agency tension: [[data-factories-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] treats informed user choice as the ultimate regulatory force, while [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] documents unequal capacity to understand, refuse, or remediate data practices.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Neither source quantifies every cost of privacy protection or compares the effectiveness of specific controls. The poverty source predates later mainstreaming of some security and privacy tools, while Thompson does not test whether processed-profile disclosure is understandable, actionable, safe around third-party data, or sufficient to change platform behavior.

## What Changed
- Added processed-profile disclosure as useful transparency that still transfers interpretation and enforcement work to users.
- Added the tension between user-generated regulation and unequal capacity to act or exit.
