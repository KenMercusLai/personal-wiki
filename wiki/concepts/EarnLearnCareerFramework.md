## Definition
The [[EarnLearnCareerFramework]] is a career-decision heuristic that asks whether an opportunity's primary realistic return is current wealth or accumulated career capital.

## Current Synthesis
[[is-it-time-for-you-to-earn-or-to-learn-both-sides-of-the-table]] argues that most startup employees outside the founding and senior-executive group should not treat small option grants as a likely path to financial independence. They should instead decide whether the role offers enough learning capital through stronger peers, broader responsibility, useful skills, industry relationships, mission, enjoyment, and improved future options.

An “earn” choice requires a different standard: credible ownership, a central role, favorable capitalization, sufficient vesting and liquidity prospects, and personal capacity to absorb failure. Life stage and financial obligations matter because the same founder-level gamble is easier to bear without dependents or a mortgage. The distinction is best used as a dominant-purpose test rather than a strict binary: jobs can yield cash, learning, meaning, and uncertain equity at the same time.

## Key Claims
- Evaluate a role against its primary plausible return rather than its best-case story.
- Treat non-senior startup equity as uncertain upside unless the cap table and payout scenarios support a stronger conclusion.
- Judge learning roles by responsibility, peers, skills, relationships, mission, enjoyment, and increased future option value.
- Wealth-scale startup upside usually requires founder-level or senior-executive ownership and exposure to substantial failure risk.
- Match entrepreneurial risk to skills, ambition, age, dependents, housing obligations, and financial runway.
- Avoid cultures that recruit through speculative equity riches instead of meaningful work, development, mission, and reasonable cash compensation.

## Evidence
- Primary-return distinction: [[is-it-time-for-you-to-earn-or-to-learn-both-sides-of-the-table]] repeatedly asks candidates whether they are choosing the next role to earn or to learn.
- Career-capital return: [[is-it-time-for-you-to-earn-or-to-learn-both-sides-of-the-table]] names network, increased responsibility, technical or industry skills, partnerships, resume value, mission, and enjoyment as learning-role outcomes.
- Equity realism: [[is-it-time-for-you-to-earn-or-to-learn-both-sides-of-the-table]] works through small-grant scenarios and then reduces nominal proceeds for time, vesting, tax, dilution, and preferences.
- Ownership threshold: [[is-it-time-for-you-to-earn-or-to-learn-both-sides-of-the-table]] argues that founder or top-three-to-four status is usually necessary for a credible chance at wealth-scale proceeds.
- Personal constraints: [[is-it-time-for-you-to-earn-or-to-learn-both-sides-of-the-table]] contrasts the risk capacity of a person under 21 with that of someone carrying children and a mortgage.
- Structural diligence: [[is-it-time-for-you-to-earn-or-to-learn-both-sides-of-the-table]] advises a prospective spinout CTO to reject a structure with about 5% for the CEO and no management option pool.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The framework comes from one 2009 practitioner essay and offers anecdotes rather than comparative career or compensation outcomes. “Earn” and “learn” overlap: a mature employer can teach, a startup can pay strong cash compensation, and a learning-led role can unexpectedly create wealth. Conversely, founding or joining the executive team raises potential upside without making success likely. The article's numerical examples use dated, simplified assumptions about valuation, exits, tax, vesting, dilution, and housing costs. Personal responsibilities should inform risk capacity without being used as a categorical age or family-status rule.

## What Changed
- Added the framework as a dominant-purpose test that separates credible career capital from speculative startup-equity upside.
- Qualified the earn/learn distinction as overlapping rather than mutually exclusive.
