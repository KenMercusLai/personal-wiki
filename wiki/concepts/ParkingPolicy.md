## Definition
[[ParkingPolicy]] is the set of rules, prices, public investments, and land-use choices through which cities govern where vehicles wait, who bears that cost, and how parking demand affects housing, traffic, public space, and other transport modes.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents parking as a priced-or-hidden allocation problem rather than a free amenity. Minimum requirements transfer construction costs into housing and commercial goods while encouraging excess supply; underpriced curb space produces scarcity, cruising, and a subsidy concentrated among vehicle owners. Removing minimums lets demand and location shape how much parking is built, while variable curb prices aim to maintain a small number of available spaces and eliminate search traffic.

Autonomous and shared vehicles make parking policy part of a larger mobility regime. Shared fleets can raise utilization and free central land, but private autonomous vehicles can avoid parking charges by circulating empty or can induce longer commutes. Parking reform therefore works with congestion pricing, transit integration, accessibility obligations, and mobility-data access. Technology changes the available choices, but policy determines whether private convenience consumes or releases shared urban capacity.

## Key Claims
- Parking supply carries construction, land, housing, environmental, and opportunity costs even when drivers do not pay at the point of use.
- Minimum parking requirements can force supply above observed demand and bundle its cost into rent and other prices.
- Demand-responsive curb pricing can trade nominally free access for reliable availability and less cruising.
- Reducing parking can unlock centrally located land for housing, parks, schools, healthcare, commerce, and walkable infill.
- Autonomous mobility does not guarantee lower parking or traffic: ownership, empty travel, induced demand, and fleet utilization determine the direction of change.
- Congestion pricing, transit integration, access rules, and platform data sharing complement parking reform.

## Evidence
- Hidden cost and oversupply: [[no-parking-here-mother-jones]] cites Seattle housing-cost estimates, unused nighttime residential spaces, and US parking-area estimates to argue that mandated supply is neither free nor tightly matched to demand.
- Search traffic and pricing: [[no-parking-here-mother-jones]] reports that SFpark's variable prices reduced cruising, parking-search time, driving, and emissions in pilot areas while increasing meter revenue.
- Land reuse: [[no-parking-here-mother-jones]] uses parklets, downtown parking area, Seoul's reclaimed corridor, and New York public space as examples of alternative uses for transport land.
- Autonomous scenarios: [[no-parking-here-mother-jones]] contrasts highly utilized shared fleets with private vehicles that enable long commutes or circulate empty to avoid parking.
- Governance bundle: [[no-parking-here-mother-jones]] combines parking-minimum reform with congestion fees, transit connections, public-interest service, and private-platform data access.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is a journalistic synthesis published in 2016, not a current comparative review. Its national parking estimate, generational driving trends, ride-sharing adoption figures, autonomous-vehicle timelines, and modeled fleet benefits depend on historical datasets and assumptions. SFpark supports the narrower claim that prices can change curb occupancy and search behavior in its pilot context; it does not by itself establish that every city will achieve the same environmental, revenue, or political results. Reduced parking demand can also coexist with more vehicle travel if empty trips or easier long commutes create rebound effects.

## What Changed
- Created the concept by separating parking's hidden cost, supply mandates, curb allocation, land reuse, and autonomous-vehicle rebound into one policy framework.
- Added the claim that parking reform needs congestion, transit, accessibility, and data governance rather than operating as an isolated pricing measure.
