## Definition
[[SAPSRewardFramework]] is [[GabeZichermann]]'s gamification reward hierarchy: Status, Access, Power, and Stuff, ordered from the rewards he considers most sticky and cheapest to fulfill toward the least preferred tangible rewards.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents SAPS as a practical alternative to defaulting to cash, prizes, or physical goods in [[Gamification]]. Its core judgment is that reward designers should first look for non-material levers: status that recognizes standing, access that grants special interaction or early opportunity, and power that lets users moderate, influence, or change part of the system. Stuff remains available, but the article places it last because physical or cash-like rewards can be expensive, operationally burdensome, unimpressive at low dollar values, and weak unless backed by enough money to feel genuinely meaningful.

This makes SAPS useful as a reward-design checklist, especially for resource-constrained products or brand programs. It is not presented with empirical evidence in the source, and the captured discussion explicitly includes criticism that SAPS is incomplete and lacks research backing. The framework should therefore be treated as a practitioner heuristic, not as a universal theory of motivation.

## Key Claims
- Status rewards can motivate by making a user's standing, achievement, or contribution visible.
- Access rewards motivate by giving users special contact, private experiences, or early opportunities.
- Power rewards motivate by granting users influence over other users or over part of the product environment.
- Stuff is the least preferred reward category because tangible rewards are costly, logistically heavy, and often disappointing at small values.
- SAPS is most useful as a constrained-design heuristic rather than as validated motivation science.

## Evidence
- Reward ordering: [[cash-is-for-saps-gamification-co]] defines SAPS as Status, Access, Power, and Stuff, and states that customers want those rewards in that order.
- Cost and stickiness: [[cash-is-for-saps-gamification-co]] says the same order moves from stickier and cheaper rewards toward less sticky and more expensive ones.
- Access examples: [[cash-is-for-saps-gamification-co]] gives CEO dinners, office tours, and early access to deals as special-interaction rewards.
- Power examples: [[cash-is-for-saps-gamification-co]] gives moderator roles and product-changing privileges as power rewards.
- Stuff qualification: [[cash-is-for-saps-gamification-co]] says material giveaways make sense only after other reward options are exhausted or when the giveaway budget is strong.
- Evidence limit: [[cash-is-for-saps-gamification-co]] includes later captured criticism that SAPS is a personal theory without research or data behind it.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a short practitioner blog post, not a controlled study. It does not segment users by context, culture, product category, or task type, and it does not prove that status, access, and power always outrank money or goods. The criticism preserved in the captured article means SAPS should be used as a prompt for reward exploration, not as a complete account of intrinsic motivation, fairness, habit formation, or long-term engagement.
