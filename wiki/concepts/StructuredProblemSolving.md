## Definition
[[StructuredProblemSolving]] is the practice of turning a vague difficulty into an explicit desired-state gap, organizing causes and options, and choosing an executable response with visible assumptions.

## Current Synthesis
The Wulc note combines several familiar methods into one sequence. A problem is first described as the gap from current state to desired state. The solver then calibrates the goal, questions the method or system that produced the current outcome, and searches for relevant variables. Deduction, induction, and analogy create candidate causal links; pyramid structures and MECE-style decomposition organize them; [[SystemsThinking]] checks feedback, delay, and second-order effects. Decisions become inspectable when criteria and options leave the mind and enter a written comparison, while reframing around the underlying objective can reveal a third option outside the original choice set. Execution ranges from task capture and goal decomposition to coordinated resource plans, with adaptive evolution preferred when uncertainty makes a fixed plan brittle.

## Key Claims
- A useful problem statement specifies the desired state, current state, and gap between them.
- Goal calibration, method redesign, and variable analysis address different reasons the gap may persist.
- Deduction, induction, and analogy generate different kinds of causal hypothesis and should not be treated as interchangeable proof.
- Hierarchy and MECE-style decomposition make a complex analysis easier to inspect for overlap and omission.
- Written criteria and option comparison can expose tradeoffs and reduce dependence on an immediate intuitive reaction.
- Reframing around the underlying objective can reveal options hidden by the initial either-or framing.
- Planning should match uncertainty: decomposition improves executable work, while variation, selection, and iteration improve adaptability.

## Evidence
- Gap definition: [[blog-wulc-ren-zhi-hong-li-yue-du-bi-ji-2-da-nao-sheng-ji]] defines a problem through desired state, current state, and the transition between them.
- Intervention types: [[blog-wulc-ren-zhi-hong-li-yue-du-bi-ji-2-da-nao-sheng-ji]] separates goal calibration, reconstruction of the prior method, and removal or management of relevant variables.
- Reasoning toolkit: [[blog-wulc-ren-zhi-hong-li-yue-du-bi-ji-2-da-nao-sheng-ji]] groups deduction, Mill-style inductive comparisons, and analogy as ways to form causal links.
- Structural toolkit: [[blog-wulc-ren-zhi-hong-li-yue-du-bi-ji-2-da-nao-sheng-ji]] combines top-down and bottom-up pyramid construction with MECE-style partitioning.
- Choice process: [[blog-wulc-ren-zhi-hong-li-yue-du-bi-ji-2-da-nao-sheng-ji]] recommends slow thinking, external records, reminders, bias awareness, explicit criteria, and reframing toward the real goal.
- Execution modes: [[blog-wulc-ren-zhi-hong-li-yue-du-bi-ji-2-da-nao-sheng-ji]] moves from GTD-style task handling to goal decomposition and resource-intensive coordinated planning, then contrasts plans with adaptive evolution.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source synthesizes methods without showing that the combined sequence outperforms simpler experiments, expert intuition, collaborative inquiry, or domain-specific practice. Desired states may be contested or change during discovery; present states and causes may be only partly observable; and variables may interact rather than permit clean removal. MECE decomposition can impose false boundaries, analogy can transfer the wrong causal structure, induction can mistake correlation for cause, and quantitative scoring can hide value judgments inside weights. In uncertain environments, learning from a bounded experiment may be cheaper than completing a full analysis.
