## Definition
[[MarketplaceReviewFraud]] is coordinated manipulation of marketplace ratings or written reviews through payment, reimbursement, free products, fabricated experience, competitor attacks, or pressure to revise criticism.

## Current Synthesis
The Amazon investigation presents review fraud as an adversarial market rather than isolated dishonest posts. Sellers seek ranking and conversion gains; recruiters and moderators aggregate reviewer supply; reviewers earn commissions, free goods, or resale income; and transactions move through private social groups and off-platform payments. The network can generate favorable reviews for one product and one-star attacks on competitors.

The central weakness is signal substitution. Buyers use stars, review volume, prose, photos, and verified-purchase badges as evidence of product quality, but fraudsters can reproduce those visible signals without genuine use. Enforcement can therefore create adaptation: banning disclosed incentives pushed coordination underground, while ordinary accounts, real purchases, delayed refunds, varied wording, and private channels made commissioned activity resemble authentic participation.

The resulting harm is distributed. Buyers may choose inferior or unsafe products, honest sellers face pressure to cheat or lose algorithmic visibility, and the platform must police activity that occurs partly outside its own systems while continuing to depend economically on seller participation and transaction volume.

## Key Claims
- Review fraud is a coordinated supply chain linking sellers, recruiters, moderators, reviewers, payment systems, and resale markets.
- Reputation signals become unreliable when attackers can cheaply imitate verified purchases, detailed prose, photographs, and apparent reviewer histories.
- Rule changes can displace abuse into less observable channels without removing the underlying ranking and sales incentive.
- Fraud includes both promotion and sabotage: purchased five-star reviews and commissioned one-star reviews of competitors.
- Marketplace dependence can pressure legitimate sellers toward manipulation because rankings and reviews materially affect livelihoods.
- Consumer harm rises when manufactured trust reaches health, safety, or otherwise difficult-to-evaluate products.
- Effective response requires product design, cross-signal detection, account sanctions, seller enforcement, off-platform intelligence, and incentive-aware governance rather than review-text filtering alone.

## Evidence
Coordinated market:
- [[inside-amazons-fake-review-economy]] documents recruitment through Facebook groups, Reddit, Discord, and Slack, with moderators taking a percentage and reviewers receiving refunds, fees, products, and resale opportunities.

Signal imitation and adaptation:
- [[inside-amazons-fake-review-economy]] shows reviewers buying through their own eligible Amazon accounts and receiving later PayPal refunds so commissioned reviews could retain verified-purchase status.
- [[inside-amazons-fake-review-economy]] reports that Amazon's 2016 ban removed disclosure and pushed paid coordination into private channels rather than ending it.

Promotion, sabotage, and seller pressure:
- [[inside-amazons-fake-review-economy]] includes paid five-star campaigns, a Reddit offer for one-star competitor reviews, and sellers describing sudden sales losses after suspicious review activity.

Consumer and safety harm:
- [[inside-amazons-fake-review-economy]] contrasts commissioned endorsements of health-related products with adverse customer reports and a physician's warning about boric-acid toxicity and irritation.

Enforcement limits:
- [[inside-amazons-fake-review-economy]] describes Amazon's machine learning, review weighting, purchase thresholds, investigations, lawsuits, and account sanctions alongside continued evasion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a 2018 investigation built from five anonymous reviewers, seller and customer cases, observed private groups, company statements, and ReviewMeta analysis; it does not estimate the representative prevalence of fraud across Amazon. ReviewMeta's “unnatural” label is algorithmic suspicion, not proof that every flagged review was paid, while Amazon's less-than-1% figure covered fraud the company identified and excluded undetected cases. A paid or unnatural review does not by itself prove the product is defective, and suspicious review velocity or repeated reviewer overlap is evidence for investigation rather than conclusive attribution. The article does not evaluate later policy, legal, detection, or marketplace changes.
