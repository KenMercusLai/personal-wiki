## Definition
[[WorkplaceSexismInTech]] is the pattern of gendered harm, exclusion, credibility denial, harassment, and role stereotyping that makes technical work harder or less safe for women and other marginalized people.

## Current Synthesis
The sources show sexism operating at several levels. In research, an advisor tied mentorship to private dinners, escalated unwanted attention, and made [[AmyWibowo]] feel unsafe, while institutional investigation still placed some blame on her behavior. In startup engineering, blame for outages, ignored suggestions, git-blame reversals, and advice to pursue fashion instead of tech undermined her confidence. [[KristenKoster]] adds a game-design case: male colleagues with less multiplayer experience questioned or bypassed her work, a promotion for her husband displaced her from design under the reporting structure, and the alternative support role ignored the realities of new motherhood. Dramatic incidents therefore sit on top of routine credibility denial, role stereotyping, and unequal institutional remedies.

## Key Claims
- Harassment can convert a research environment from intellectually enabling to physically unsafe.
- Gatekeeping mentorship through romantic or sexual attention damages technical development.
- Credibility denial can appear through ignored suggestions, gendered blame, and mistaken assumptions about who wrote code.
- Gendered role stereotypes can push women away from engineering even when they have technical evidence of competence.
- Institutional responses can improve policy while still reproducing victim-blaming narratives.
- The cumulative effect, compounded by caregiving constraints and public harassment, can erode the joy and confidence that drew someone to computing.
- Formally neutral reporting or conflict rules can impose unequal career costs when the available remedy displaces the lower-power worker.

## Evidence
- Research harassment: [[a-career-retrospective-10-years-working-in-tech-sailor-mercury-medium]] says the advisor refused advice outside private dinners, repeatedly asked the author out, followed her, and tried to kiss her.
- Mentorship loss: [[a-career-retrospective-10-years-working-in-tech-sailor-mercury-medium]] says she completed the project without real advice because she refused unsafe private conditions.
- Startup credibility denial: [[a-career-retrospective-10-years-working-in-tech-sailor-mercury-medium]] describes being blamed for outages, having suggestions accepted only after men repeated them, and finding criticized changes had been made by her manager.
- Stereotyping: [[a-career-retrospective-10-years-working-in-tech-sailor-mercury-medium]] says founders told her to leave tech for fashion at an exit interview.
- Cumulative cost: [[a-career-retrospective-10-years-working-in-tech-sailor-mercury-medium]] says repeated sexism made the magic of computers disappear for her.
- Game-design credibility: [[gamasutra-the-great-video-game-exodus]] says less-experienced male colleagues questioned or ignored [[KristenKoster]] and bypassed her data-driven system.
- Unequal remedy: [[gamasutra-the-great-video-game-exodus]] says Koster lost her design-team position when her husband was selected as lead and was offered irregular live-support work after becoming a mother.
- Harassment context: [[gamasutra-the-great-video-game-exodus]] reports that women in games face gender-amplified online abuse in addition to workplace pressure.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The page is grounded in two autobiographical accounts embedded in essays or reported features, not a statistical measure of technology or game-industry prevalence. Koster's source does not include colleague or employer perspectives, and the wider online-harassment claim is stated without survey data. The sources nevertheless provide convergent mechanism-level evidence across research, startup engineering, and game design.
