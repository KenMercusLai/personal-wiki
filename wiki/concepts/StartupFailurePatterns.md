## Definition
[[StartupFailurePatterns]] are recurring mechanisms that cause young companies to shut down, especially when weak market evidence, capital pressure, strategic confusion, operational complexity, or external shocks compound faster than the team can adapt.

## Current Synthesis
The CB Insights compilation turns individual shutdown stories into a broad qualitative pattern map. The strongest recurring theme is that failure rarely has one clean cause. A startup may begin with real technology, early users, press, funding, or founder conviction, yet still fail when the business cannot prove large enough demand, make unit economics work, finance the next iteration, survive timing mismatch, or defend a distribution channel. Hardware, robotics, AR/VR, autonomous-vehicle, healthcare, food, and marketplace cases add a second layer: physical operations, regulation, trust, supply chains, reimbursement, seasonal demand, or complex stakeholder adoption can make iteration slower and more expensive than software founders expect.

The source also sharpens how to read post-mortems. Founder accounts are valuable because they reveal what decisions looked like from inside the company, but they are retrospective and sometimes self-protective. Investigative takedowns add missing governance, fraud, safety, billing, labor, or legal context. The useful synthesis is therefore not a moral ranking of failed founders, but a pattern vocabulary: missing pull, burned runway, premature scale, platform dependence, market timing, focus loss, governance failure, and business-model fragility.

## Key Claims
- Startup failure is usually a compound outcome rather than a single mistake, even when practitioner accounts single out one primary cause such as founder disputes or a premature claim of product-market fit.
- Missing or weak [[ProductMarketFit]] can coexist with good technology, early traction, press, or passionate users.
- [[StartupRunway]] is only valuable when it funds meaningful learning and adaptation before the next financing, revenue, or shutdown deadline.
- [[MarketTiming]] failure can make a correct long-term thesis unusable for a venture-backed company with near-term burn.
- Hardware, regulated industries, healthcare, food delivery, marketplaces, and platform-dependent products carry extra adoption, trust, logistics, capital, and policy risk.
- Premature scaling, pivot churn, channel dependence, and split strategies convert uncertainty into faster cash burn, and scaling after mistakenly concluding that fit has been reached is a common version of that mistake.
- Public post-mortems are evidence, but they need qualification because they mix founder self-explanation, investor framing, journalism, and company shutdown notices.

## Evidence
- Baseline failure rates: [[323-startup-failure-post-mortems]] reports that 70% of upstart tech companies fail, often around 20 months after first financing, and that consumer hardware startup outcomes are especially brutal.
- Multi-cause framing: [[323-startup-failure-post-mortems]] lists repeated factors including funding drop-offs, competition, unsustainable business models, regulatory pressure, platform dependence, legal disputes, fraud allegations, and market timing.
- Product-market fit weakness: [[323-startup-failure-post-mortems]] includes cases that cite failing to find product-market fit, solving a pain instead of a real problem, changing user behavior too much, weak retention, or no scalable demand.
- Runway pressure: [[323-startup-failure-post-mortems]] repeatedly describes companies unable to secure new financing, running out of cash, entering Chapter 7, winding down after failed acquisition or investment talks, or returning capital when the next phase looked unfinanceable.
- Timing mismatch: [[323-startup-failure-post-mortems]] includes VR, drones, lidar, battery, diagnostics, and telemedicine examples where markets or buying systems developed more slowly than the company could afford.
- Operational and hardware complexity: [[323-startup-failure-post-mortems]] includes consumer robotics, smoke alarms, laundry robots, connected juicers, wearables, drones, and medical kiosks where physical production, support, deployment, or seasonal demand made failure more likely.
- Platform dependence: [[323-startup-failure-post-mortems]] cites dependence on SEO, Facebook, YouTube/Facebook creator distribution, ebook platform economics, and gatekeeper policy as business fragility.
- Governance and trust failures: [[323-startup-failure-post-mortems]] includes examples involving billing practices, fraud allegations, regulatory enforcement, whistleblower suits, legal disputes, and misleading product claims.
- Presumed-fit scaling: [[y-combinator-ceo-if-you-are-not-drowning-in-demand-you-dont-have-product-market-fit-capital-growth-blog]] says the major cause of failure for YC companies is thinking product-market fit has been attained, then scaling up as expenses rise.
- Founder disputes: [[y-combinator-ceo-if-you-are-not-drowning-in-demand-you-dont-have-product-market-fit-capital-growth-blog]] names founder disputes as the leading cause of failure for startups in general.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The compilation does not prove a statistical causal ranking for all startups. It is a curated public archive, so visible venture-backed failures, dramatic shutdowns, founder essays, and media investigations are more likely to appear than quiet small-business failures or companies that changed form without public closure. Many cases are reported through interested narrators: founders may rationalize, journalists may emphasize conflict, and company notices may minimize blame. The accelerator source adds priority claims without supporting data — it comes from an edited AMA with one program's CEO, so the ranking of founder disputes and presumed fit as leading causes is a practitioner judgment drawn from a selected portfolio rather than a measured base rate. The concept should therefore be used as a pattern vocabulary and diagnostic checklist, not as a predictive model.

## What Changed
- Created a multi-case startup failure concept that groups the wiki's existing single-company lessons into broader recurring mechanisms.
- Added platform dependence, governance failure, and hardware/regulated-industry complexity as explicit failure-pattern categories.
- Qualified post-mortems as retrospective evidence rather than neutral causal proof.
- Added founder disputes and premature claims of product-market fit as named priority causes from accelerator-side practice.

## Related Concepts

- [[StartupRunway]] - cash limits how many iterations a startup gets before financing, revenue, or shutdown decisions arrive.
- [[MarketTiming]] - timing mismatch can defeat technically plausible companies.
- [[StartupFocus]] - focus loss, split strategies, and pivot churn can accelerate failure.
- [[StartupScaling]] - premature growth turns uncertainty into organizational and cash-burn risk.
- [[CustomerLedProductDevelopment]] - customer evidence can reduce imagined-demand failures.
- [[MinimumViableProduct]] - smaller validation tests can expose demand before large buildout.
- [[TroughOfSorrow]] - many failures occur in the difficult pre-fit period after launch attention fades.
- [[ConnectedProductSystems]] - hardware-plus-service products often fail through total-system cost, complexity, or value skepticism.
- [[PlatformBusinessModelObstacle]] - platform economics and gatekeeper policy can make otherwise useful products unviable.
- [[CoFounderConflict]] - founder disputes are the mechanism behind the accelerator source's leading general cause of failure.
