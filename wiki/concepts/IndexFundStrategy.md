## Definition
[[IndexFundStrategy]] is the use of low-cost broad-market funds or ETFs as a simpler investing approach that relies on diversified market exposure rather than frequent stock selection, market timing, or complex product access.

## Current Synthesis
The engineer-investing source presents low-cost S&P 500 exposure as the simpler strategy and contrasts it with higher-risk thematic or single-stock bets. The inspected slides show VOO's long rise from 2011 to 2025 and an example mix of 50% S&P 500 ETF, 40% Nasdaq 100 ETF, and 10% ARK innovation ETF, with ETF screenshots exposing how much top holdings overlap across Microsoft, NVIDIA, Apple, Amazon, Meta, Broadcom, Google, and Tesla. Wulc's Zhang Xiaoyu course summaries add the ordinary-investor rationale and the qualification: broad funds can implement patient domestic and global diversification because investors cannot reliably pick winners, but U.S.-market index-fund lessons should not be copied mechanically into A-share markets, and even indexes can disappoint for long periods when bought at euphoric valuations.

## Key Claims
- Low-cost S&P 500 funds are presented as a simple default that can outperform many high-fee active funds.
- Broad-market ETFs reduce reliance on selecting individual companies at the right time.
- Nasdaq and innovation ETFs add growth exposure but also increase concentration in technology and volatile themes.
- ETF holdings should be inspected because broad labels can hide overlap in the same mega-cap companies.
- Global index exposure can help ordinary investors reduce home-country concentration while reducing the need for timing and manager selection.
- Index-fund strategy is market-context sensitive; A-share cycles and active-versus-index performance may make "buy any broad index automatically" too crude.
- Fund fees, sales charges, custody costs, and trading frequency materially affect the long-term result.

## Evidence
- Simple default: [[gong-cheng-shi-ru-he-geng-hao-tou-zi]] says low-cost S&P 500 index funds can beat most high-fee active managers.
- Long-term example: [[gong-cheng-shi-ru-he-geng-hao-tou-zi]] shows a Vanguard S&P 500 ETF chart from 2011 to 2025 with large cumulative appreciation.
- Portfolio mix: [[gong-cheng-shi-ru-he-geng-hao-tou-zi]] presents 50% S&P 500, 40% Nasdaq 100, and 10% ARK innovation as a simple example allocation.
- Holding overlap: [[gong-cheng-shi-ru-he-geng-hao-tou-zi]] lists VOO and QQQ top holdings with repeated mega-cap technology names.
- Global exposure: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] points to ACWI-style funds as a way for ordinary investors to access major global stock markets.
- Timing reduction: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-1-shi-chang-gui-lu]] recommends avoiding market timing and using broad, diversified allocation instead.
- Market-context sensitivity: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-2-tou-zi-gong-ju-yu-zi-wo-ju-xian]] says index-fund advice associated with Buffett and U.S. markets may not apply cleanly to A-share markets, especially where long bear markets and active-fund comparisons differ.
- Valuation risk: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-2-tou-zi-gong-ju-yu-zi-wo-ju-xian]] notes that Nasdaq, S&P 500, and CSI 300 index levels all took years to revisit prior peaks after expensive entry points.
- Fee discipline: [[blog-wulc-zhang-xiao-yu-de-ge-ren-tou-zi-ke-2-tou-zi-gong-ju-yu-zi-wo-ju-xian]] breaks fund costs into management, custody, subscription/redemption or trading, and sales-service fees, warning that small annual fee differences compound.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not make broad-market funds risk-free or universally sufficient. ETF examples are date-specific, global funds can carry currency and jurisdictional complications, broad markets can still draw down, and adding Nasdaq or ARK exposure increases concentration and valuation risk. Wulc's second Zhang Xiaoyu note explicitly cautions that index-fund enthusiasm should be adjusted for local market structure rather than treated as a one-size-fits-all rule.
