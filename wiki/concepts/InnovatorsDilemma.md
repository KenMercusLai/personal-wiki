## Definition
[[InnovatorsDilemma]] is the incumbent's conflict between optimizing a profitable product for current customers and investing in a smaller, weaker, uncertain alternative that may define the next market.

## Current Synthesis
The source locates the dilemma at the overlap between [[TechnologySCurve|technology S-curves]]. From the incumbent's profitable middle or mature stage, the next architecture has worse numbers, fewer customers, and uncertain economics. Rational-looking responses to current customers, quarterly earnings, sales targets, and departmental metrics can therefore concentrate resources on incremental feature expansion while a challenger learns in neglected niches.

The problem is organizational as well as perceptual. When a company defines itself by selling more of its current product rather than solving an underlying customer problem, scale and specialization make the existing model easier to optimize and harder to question. By the time revenue visibly declines, viable successors may already have emerged and the incumbent may be left with a single desperate bet.

## Key Claims
- Incumbents compare emerging technologies against mature-product economics and therefore underrate weak early signals.
- Revenue, profit, and market share are lagging indicators when customer behavior and technology architecture are changing.
- Quarterly pressure and current-customer demands reward incremental optimization over uncertain transition work.
- Functional silos and sales dominance can narrow the organization around local metrics and the current product.
- Moving from customer-problem focus to product-defense focus makes disruption harder to recognize.
- Repeated experiments and several viable bets reduce dependence on one late rescue attempt.

## Evidence
- BlackBerry lag: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] says BlackBerry revenue peaked in 2011 even though the iPhone had changed the market in 2007.
- Unattractive successor: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] argues that a just-forming S-curve looks small and inconsequential from the peak of the incumbent curve.
- Organizational narrowing: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] describes feature expansion, upmarket movement, functional silos, sales influence, and protection of the existing audience.
- Late-bet risk: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] quotes Jeff Bezos distinguishing continuous experimentation from a desperate end-stage "Hail Mary" bet.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a practitioner synthesis built around selected company stories rather than a comparative study. Serving current customers, improving a mature product, or rejecting a weak entrant can be correct; most experiments and new products fail, and early niches do not identify winners automatically. Regulation, ecosystems, distribution, execution, pricing, and technical quality can matter as much as incumbent psychology.

## What Changed
- Created the concept as a combined measurement, incentive, and organization problem rather than a simple failure to notice technology.
- Added the distinction between continuous option-building and one late company-saving bet.
