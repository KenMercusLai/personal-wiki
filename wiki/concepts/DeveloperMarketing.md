## Definition
[[DeveloperMarketing]] is the work of helping developers discover, evaluate, trust, and adopt a technical product through useful technical material, credible participation, product-quality web experiences, and channels matched to the company's stage.

## Current Synthesis
The PostHog source frames developer marketing as a sequence rather than a channel checklist. Before [[ProductMarketFit]], a small team should build trust and learning through a few unusually useful articles, maintained SEO assets, tutorials, a product-like website, substantive editorial review, careful outsourcing, and direct attribution questions. After fit, the company can add a developer-writer, paid media, newsletter sponsorships, events, and focused social experiments without making them the center of the system.

The [[FlawlessApp]] case adds a concentrated launch mode. Its founders treated [[ProductHunt]] as a timed operating event layered over an audience they had already helped and known, especially in iOS, macOS, and design communities. Preparation, founder voice, targeted posts, user email, live support, response, and personal thanks turned the day into both distribution and research. Reported votes, traffic, trials, sales, and feedback differed sharply in value: first place and more than 1,300 votes created visibility, while nine sales, extensive feedback, and no major press qualified rank as neither revenue nor public-relations proof. Across both sources, the common principle is technical-audience credibility: useful participation and audience fit precede channel amplification, and outcome quality matters more than traffic alone.

## Key Claims
- Content depth, usefulness, and maintenance are more defensible for a small team than trying to match a large competitor's publication volume.
- The marketing surface is part of the developer experience, so website governance and editorial review should protect clarity and utility.
- Channel sequencing should follow company evidence: inexpensive learning comes first, while paid and sponsorship-heavy channels become more plausible after fit.
- Outsourcing should add missing expertise without transferring the team's critical judgment or creating more management work than it saves.
- Developer credibility matters across channels because technical writers, engineers at events, and personal voices can connect product detail with audience trust.
- Repeatable acquisition and qualified signup behavior matter more than attention spikes or platform-reported credit.
- A launch platform can concentrate discovery and feedback when it is supported by relevant communities, prepared operations, and fast response rather than treated as a self-contained traffic source.

## Evidence
- Content system: [[dev-tool-marketing-for-early-stage-startups-what-weve-learned]] recommends a rough mix of SEO articles, tutorials, and freer pieces, with ongoing investment in the best search assets.
- Product-quality surfaces: [[dev-tool-marketing-for-early-stage-startups-what-weve-learned]] gives the website team final say and requires another person to review every article at a substantive level.
- Stage sequencing: [[dev-tool-marketing-for-early-stage-startups-what-weve-learned]] separates pre-fit content and learning from post-fit paid ads, sponsorships, and specialist hiring.
- Credible participation: [[dev-tool-marketing-for-early-stage-startups-what-weve-learned]] favors a developer who writes, engineers at events, and personal over company social accounts.
- Measurement discipline: [[dev-tool-marketing-for-early-stage-startups-what-weve-learned]] contrasts unreliable Hacker News traffic with repeatable marketing and supplements click attribution with free-text signup responses.
- Relevant-community launch: [[is-product-hunt-useful-for-developer-tools-flawless-ios-medium]] links two years of participation in technical and startup communities with a scheduled Product Hunt campaign, user email, founder posts, live support, and follow-up.
- Outcome separation: [[is-product-hunt-useful-for-developer-tools-flawless-ios-medium]] reports more than 1,300 votes and 3,200 sessions alongside 97 trials, nine two-day sales, extensive feedback, and no major press, showing why visibility, learning, conversion, and publicity should be judged separately.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are company-authored practitioner accounts rather than controlled channel comparisons. PostHog does not provide cohort results, content-production costs, or sponsorship returns; Flawless does not separate Product Hunt discovery from email, founder audiences, or targeted external communities and supplies no retention or later-revenue evidence. The latter is also a selected success case whose “zero-dollar” framing excludes years of relationship building and 40 combined founder hours on launch day. Platform economics and audience behavior can change, and neither account establishes that its channel mix generalizes across open-source, self-serve, enterprise, infrastructure, or consumer-developer products.

## What Changed
- Added concentrated launch-day operations as a distinct mode built on prior audience trust rather than a substitute for it.
- Separated launch visibility, feedback, trials, sales, and press as different outcome classes.
- Added uncompensated labor, mixed-channel attribution, and success-case selection as launch-evidence limits.
