## Definition
[[AccessJournalism]] is reporting shaped by dependence on continued proximity to influential subjects, creating a tradeoff between information gained through access and scrutiny that might endanger it.

## Current Synthesis
The sources show access as a social, institutional, and commercial incentive rather than only an explicit bargain. Exclusive invitations, friendship, hospitality, repeated contact, subscriber community, paid access, events, boards, investors, and family ties can make sources easier to understand and approach while normalizing their assumptions or raising the cost of confrontation. The risk is diffuse: coverage may become softer, selectively limited, or appear compromised without any direct censorship or agreement.

[[KaraSwisher]] provides an important non-deterministic case. Long relationships, continual contact, social fluency, and conference participation helped her gather information and exercise influence, yet the profile also documents adversarial scoops, explicit criticism, recusal, disclosure, and relationships that survived harsh coverage. Access therefore creates incentives and role conflicts rather than proving corruption. Reader or media-company funding can reduce advertiser or venture pressure, but each model introduces its own dependencies when subscribers, sources, speakers, directors, funders, and covered elites overlap.

## Key Claims
- Access can improve information gathering while simultaneously weakening adversarial distance.
- The expectation of future invitations or interviews can shape questions and coverage before any source applies explicit pressure.
- Shared elite settings can normalize subjects' premises and status, turning extraordinary claims into agenda-setting statements rather than claims to test.
- Transparency about relationships and incentives helps readers evaluate coverage but does not remove their effects.
- Subscription community can align revenue with reader value yet create access pressure when subscribers, sources, and covered elites overlap.
- Recusal, source confirmation, financial separation, non-industry funding, and willingness to publish adverse stories can constrain access pressure without eliminating role conflict or perceived compromise.

## Evidence
- Proximity tradeoff: [[ground-control-to-silicon-valley]] argues that getting closer can mean knowing more and saying less to preserve access.
- Conference mechanism: [[ground-control-to-silicon-valley]] describes curated attendance, luxury hospitality, mentoring and friendship ties, and informal access to executives and investors.
- Coverage effect: [[ground-control-to-silicon-valley]] contrasts intensive coverage of Musk's speculative remarks with limited scrutiny of antitrust, diversity, platform governance, and political influence.
- Membership overlap: [[inside-the-information-columbia-journalism-review]] describes [[TheInformation]] selling community events and higher-priced reporter access to a professional audience overlapping the technology industry it covered.
- Credibility counterpressure: [[inside-the-information-columbia-journalism-review]] also reports tough investigative work and Lessin's argument that subscriber trust makes adversarial reporting commercially necessary.
- Relationship mechanism: [[kara-swisher-is-silicon-valleys-most-feared-and-well-liked-journalist-how-does-that-work]] describes Swisher maintaining contact between stories, using humor and pressure to obtain candor, and preserving relationships with people she criticized.
- Institutional overlap: [[kara-swisher-is-silicon-valleys-most-feared-and-well-liked-journalist-how-does-that-work]] joins reporting with conference revenue, covered-industry participation, board membership, media backers, friendships, and a spouse employed by a major covered company.
- Mitigation and residual risk: [[kara-swisher-is-silicon-valleys-most-feared-and-well-liked-journalist-how-does-that-work]] documents Google recusal, extensive disclosure, financial separation, confirmation thresholds, and avoidance of venture funding, while quoted peers still identify coverage and appearance constraints.
- Adversarial counterexample: [[kara-swisher-is-silicon-valleys-most-feared-and-well-liked-journalist-how-does-that-work]] reports repeated damaging scoops and says rivals could not identify a major story Swisher abandoned, showing that close access does not necessarily produce favorable coverage.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are reported profiles and interpretations from 2014 and 2016 rather than comparative studies of question quality, sourcing, corrections, story selection, or unpublished decisions. Access can enable informed questioning: The Information's Nest investigation and Swisher's repeated adverse scoops counter any automatic inference that proximity produces soft coverage. The Swisher profile also relies on reputation and anecdotes from allies, rivals, and subjects rather than an audit. Social ties, events, funding, board roles, or paid briefings therefore identify incentives and appearance risks, not proof that particular journalism was compromised.

## What Changed
- Added conferences, boards, backers, family ties, recusal, and source discretion to the access-incentive model.
- Added Swisher as a counterexample to deterministic claims that close relationships necessarily produce favorable coverage.
- Distinguished disclosure and structural mitigation from complete removal of conflict or appearance risk.
