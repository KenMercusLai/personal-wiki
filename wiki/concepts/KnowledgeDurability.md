## Definition
[[KnowledgeDurability]] is the degree to which information remains useful across time and contexts, with durable knowledge usually explaining recurring mechanisms, incentives, or relationships rather than only reporting a current event or measurement.

## Current Synthesis
Housel's distinction is functional rather than absolute. Expiring facts such as a recent acquisition, quarterly growth rate, vacancy rate, or manager result can be necessary for a decision now, but their usefulness decays quickly when they are detached from a model of what produced them. Long-term knowledge asks why industries consolidate, what sustains a competitive advantage, which forces drive demand, or how managers respond to recurring problems.

Durability creates value through accumulation and transfer. A causal framework can organize later observations, combine with ideas from other domains, and make transient information easier to interpret. The practical reading filter is therefore not "books good, news bad" but whether a source supplies a reusable explanation, a current fact that can be placed inside one, or noise with little likely future value.

## Key Claims
- Knowledge durability varies with the persistence and recurrence of the relationship being explained.
- Current facts retain more decision value when interpreted through durable causal context.
- Explanations of mechanisms, incentives, and defended advantages can transfer across companies, industries, and time periods.
- Durable frameworks compound by helping a learner organize and interpret later information.
- A future-relevance question can guide attention without requiring the rejection of useful short-term information.

## Evidence
- Time-horizon distinction: [[expiring-vs-long-term-knowledge-collaborative-fund]] contrasts recent acquisition, performance, and vacancy facts with questions about consolidation, sustainable advantage, and demand drivers.
- Business example: [[expiring-vs-long-term-knowledge-collaborative-fund]] treats a historical Microsoft growth figure as a temporary expression of a more durable competitive moat.
- Management example: [[expiring-vs-long-term-knowledge-collaborative-fund]] contrasts one-off company problems with recurring questions about managerial motivation, tenacity, and execution.
- Compounding mechanism: [[expiring-vs-long-term-knowledge-collaborative-fund]] argues that reusable explanations connect with other knowledge and improve interpretation of later events.
- Reading practice: [[expiring-vs-long-term-knowledge-collaborative-fund]] asks whether material will matter over one-, ten-, or eighty-year horizons while allowing that short-lived information can still be useful.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source provides an essayistic framework, not comparative evidence that consuming durable explanations improves retention or decisions. What appears timeless can be a stale model whose assumptions no longer hold, while a short-lived fact can be decisive in operations, safety, policy, or markets. Books can contain obsolete detail and journalism can uncover durable mechanisms, so medium is only a rough proxy. Explanatory knowledge also needs periodic testing against current evidence; otherwise a memorable framework can filter out genuine change.

## What Changed
- Created the concept by separating the useful life of information from its medium.
- Framed transient facts and durable explanations as complements rather than substitutes.
- Added accumulation, cross-domain transfer, and later interpretation as the proposed compounding mechanisms.
