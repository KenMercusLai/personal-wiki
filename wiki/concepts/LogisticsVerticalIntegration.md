## Definition
[[LogisticsVerticalIntegration]] is the selective internalization of transportation, warehousing, sorting, packaging, fulfillment, or last-mile capacity when outside networks constrain a company's customer promise, economics, category reach, or peak-volume resilience.

## Current Synthesis
The Amazon case shows vertical integration as a layered response to bottlenecks rather than an immediate all-or-nothing replacement of carriers. Prime increased shopping frequency and promised fast delivery, while the 2013 holiday failure demonstrated that aircraft, facilities, and labor cannot be added at the last minute. Amazon responded with leased cargo aircraft, trailers, sorting centers, delivery stations, local hubs, flexible couriers, postal handoffs, and freight-forwarding authority.

Internal capacity can provide operational control, peak resilience, learning, bargaining leverage, and the option to sell logistics services later. Yet the source also shows Amazon continuing to demand outside-carrier and Postal Service capacity. A retailer may therefore internalize the layers most important to its promise without immediately replacing every external provider.

The Fall River case broadens this model beyond carrier capacity. Product geometry itself can justify internal specialization: Amazon used a non-sort facility with fewer conveyors, no warehouse robots, more human handling, industrial trucks, adapted storage, and custom-box production for big or irregular goods. Wayfair's dedicated bulky-goods delivery network shows a comparable category-specific choice at the last mile, while other retailers retained freight fees or store pickup. Vertical integration is therefore granular both by network layer and by product category.

## Key Claims
- Customer promises can turn supplier capacity into a strategic dependency when demand grows faster or peaks more sharply than the supplier network can absorb.
- Vertical integration may begin by supplementing bottlenecked layers rather than replacing an external network end to end.
- Peak-capacity failures justify earlier investment because aircraft, facilities, labor, and routing capacity have long lead times.
- Internal logistics can improve control and bargaining power even before it becomes a standalone external service.
- Internal and external networks can remain complementary even when their owners become potential competitors.
- Local employment gains can coexist with traffic, pollution, siting, labor, and small-business costs.
- Product size, shape, fragility, and service requirements can determine which warehouse, packaging, and delivery layers are worth internalizing.

## Evidence
- Capacity trigger: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] links Prime growth, expensive expedited shipping, and the 2013 holiday failure to Amazon's decision to create alternatives.
- Layered buildout: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] documents aircraft, trailers, sorting centers, delivery stations, Prime Now hubs, Flex couriers, postal delivery, and ocean-freight authority.
- Complement before replacement: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] quotes Bezos saying Amazon wanted all available UPS and Postal Service capacity and still needed supplementation.
- Platform option: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] reports a former Amazon executive's expectation that the internal supply chain could eventually serve other businesses, while placing that prospect a decade or more away.
- Continuing dependence: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] quotes Bezos saying Amazon's business with UPS and the Postal Service was growing while internal capacity supplemented them.
- Externalities: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] reports public resistance in Hamburg and Paris over siting, traffic, air quality, and effects on local commerce.
- Warehouse specialization: [[inside-an-amazon-warehouse-that-ships-your-supersized-purchases-cnet]] describes Amazon internalizing a non-sort facility, product-specific storage, custom packaging, and high-reaching handling equipment for unusual goods.
- Category-specific delivery: [[inside-an-amazon-warehouse-that-ships-your-supersized-purchases-cnet]] says Wayfair operated a dedicated network for bulky and fragile home goods while other retailers used freight charges or store pickup.
- Mixed boundary: [[inside-an-amazon-warehouse-that-ships-your-supersized-purchases-cnet]] also describes specialist-carrier partnerships, showing that owned and outsourced capacity can be combined within one customer proposition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence consists of a 2016 carrier-network analysis and a May 2018 warehouse tour. Neither establishes later profitability, service quality, labor effects, market share, external-customer adoption, or the relative economics of owned and outsourced capacity. Amazon, UPS, and Royal Mail offered conflicting accounts of capacity failures, forecasts of an AWS-like logistics service were speculative, and Fall River's operating figures were reported without comparative cost, safety, damage, or automation data. Selective and category-specific integration is therefore better supported than the stronger claim that Amazon would replace carriers or that internal operation is universally superior.

## What Changed
- Extended the concept from transport networks into specialized warehousing, storage, packaging, and bulky-item delivery.
- Added product geometry and service requirements as reasons to internalize one category-specific layer while outsourcing others.
