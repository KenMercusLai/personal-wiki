## Definition
[[LogisticsVerticalIntegration]] is the selective internalization of transportation, sorting, fulfillment, or last-mile capacity when outside networks constrain a company's customer promise, economics, or peak-volume resilience.

## Current Synthesis
The Amazon case shows vertical integration as a layered response to bottlenecks rather than an immediate all-or-nothing replacement of carriers. Prime increased shopping frequency and promised fast delivery, while the 2013 holiday failure demonstrated that aircraft, facilities, and labor cannot be added at the last minute. Amazon responded with leased cargo aircraft, trailers, sorting centers, delivery stations, local hubs, flexible couriers, postal handoffs, and freight-forwarding authority.

Internal capacity can provide operational control, peak resilience, learning, bargaining leverage, and the option to sell logistics services later. Yet the source also shows Amazon continuing to demand outside-carrier and Postal Service capacity. A retailer may therefore internalize the layers most important to its promise without immediately replacing every external provider.

## Key Claims
- Customer promises can turn supplier capacity into a strategic dependency when demand grows faster or peaks more sharply than the supplier network can absorb.
- Vertical integration may begin by supplementing bottlenecked layers rather than replacing an external network end to end.
- Peak-capacity failures justify earlier investment because aircraft, facilities, labor, and routing capacity have long lead times.
- Internal logistics can improve control and bargaining power even before it becomes a standalone external service.
- Internal and external networks can remain complementary even when their owners become potential competitors.
- Local employment gains can coexist with traffic, pollution, siting, labor, and small-business costs.

## Evidence
- Capacity trigger: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] links Prime growth, expensive expedited shipping, and the 2013 holiday failure to Amazon's decision to create alternatives.
- Layered buildout: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] documents aircraft, trailers, sorting centers, delivery stations, Prime Now hubs, Flex couriers, postal delivery, and ocean-freight authority.
- Complement before replacement: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] quotes Bezos saying Amazon wanted all available UPS and Postal Service capacity and still needed supplementation.
- Platform option: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] reports a former Amazon executive's expectation that the internal supply chain could eventually serve other businesses, while placing that prospect a decade or more away.
- Continuing dependence: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] quotes Bezos saying Amazon's business with UPS and the Postal Service was growing while internal capacity supplemented them.
- Externalities: [[will-amazon-kill-fedex]] reports public resistance in Hamburg and Paris over siting, traffic, air quality, and effects on local commerce.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a 2016 snapshot taken at the start of Amazon's branded air-network buildout. It does not establish the later profitability, service quality, labor effects, market share, or external-customer adoption of Amazon logistics. Amazon, UPS, and Royal Mail offered conflicting accounts of capacity failures, and forecasts of an AWS-like logistics service were speculative. Selective integration is therefore better supported than the stronger claim that Amazon would replace FedEx or UPS.

## What Changed
- Created the concept around selective capacity internalization rather than total carrier replacement.
- Added continuing carrier dependence and public externalities as limits on the integration thesis.
