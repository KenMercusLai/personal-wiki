Startup hypothesis throughput treats early startup execution as a learning-rate problem. In the Maderight reflection, a startup begins as a belief that a real problem exists and that someone will pay for a solution. Those beliefs are hypotheses, so the team's operating system should keep naming, testing, and evaluating them.

Maderight eventually adopted a recurring framework around seven questions: which assumptions need testing, how they will be tested, when results will be evaluated, what must be built for the test, why current work is essential, what progress or test result arrived last week, and how the team can move faster. The value of the framework was that it pulled discussions out of daily operator mode and back toward product-market-fit learning.

The source's runway lesson follows from the same idea. Maderight tried to conserve cash because the apparel manufacturing problem was large and sales cycles were long. In retrospect, the author argues that the goal should have been to maximize the number of hypotheses tested per dollar. The choice between a leaner China service team and a more expensive micro-factory illustrates the tradeoff: the service team preserved time, while a factory might have exposed lead-time and quality problems more directly and supported faster iteration.

## Operating Questions

- Which assumptions would actually move the company closer to product-market fit if answered?
- What test can produce behavioral evidence instead of opinion?
- What must be built, sold, or manually operated to run that test?
- How soon will the team evaluate the result and change direction?
- Is the current runway being spent to increase learning speed, or only to delay shutdown?
