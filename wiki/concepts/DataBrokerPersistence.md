## Definition
[[DataBrokerPersistence]] is the tendency for personal records to continue circulating through commercial databases after they become outdated, incomplete, corrected, or officially expunged.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents data brokers as a persistence layer for poverty-linked harms. Once welfare, criminal-justice, employment, or other personal data is collected and shared, it can be purchased, copied, and reused in ways that outlast official correction. For low-income people, that persistence can turn a non-conviction arrest record or inaccurate database entry into ongoing exclusion from housing, jobs, public benefits, and other opportunities.

## Key Claims
- Database errors are especially harmful when they are shared across government and commercial systems.
- Official correction or expungement may not remove records already purchased by data brokers.
- Persistent records can convert temporary contact with welfare or criminal-justice systems into long-term market exclusion.
- Data-broker opacity makes it hard for affected people to know why they were denied opportunities.
- Privacy law and record-repair tools are unequally accessible because wealthier people can buy reputation-management help.

## Evidence
- Cross-database sharing: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] describes welfare data being shared and compared across government and commercial databases.
- Record inaccuracy: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] says these databases can be outdated, inaccurate, and incomplete.
- Expungement gap: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] reports [[MicheleGilman]]'s students expunging arrest records that can still persist after data-broker purchase.
- Opportunity harm: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] connects persistent records to housing, jobs, and other opportunities.
- Wealth repair market: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] contrasts low-income users with wealthy users who can hire reputation-management firms.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source describes the harm pattern but does not identify particular data brokers, error rates, or enforcement outcomes. It also does not evaluate later broker-registration or privacy-law developments beyond brief mentions of Vermont and California.
