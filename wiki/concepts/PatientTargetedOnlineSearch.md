## Definition
[[PatientTargetedOnlineSearch]] is the practice of clinicians deliberately looking up a patient's online presence to gather information relevant to diagnosis, risk assessment, treatment, or safety.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents patient-targeted search as a clinically tempting but ethically unstable practice. In emergency psychiatry, online traces can help clinicians understand immediate risk when a patient is suicidal, psychotic, intoxicated, unavailable, or withholding information. Yet the same search can violate expected privacy, force awkward disclosure, undermine trust, and convert informal online material into charted evidence. The practice therefore sits between collateral care work and surveillance: it may protect patients in high-risk situations, but it needs consent norms, disclosure practices, documentation restraint, and professional boundary rules before it can become ordinary clinical infrastructure.

## Key Claims
- Online search can supply collateral clinical information when direct patient communication is incomplete or unreliable.
- Emergency psychiatry is the strongest case because online evidence can reveal acute self-harm risk, substance use, supports, or warning signs.
- Consent and disclosure matter because hidden search can damage therapeutic trust.
- Search can become legally consequential if frequent use shifts expectations about the [[StandardOfCare]].
- Documentation choices determine whether ephemeral online content becomes durable [[MedicalRecordPersistence]].
- Patient-requested search changes the privacy calculus but does not remove professional boundary obligations.

## Evidence
- Collateral value: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] says online information can help when patients cannot or will not communicate important clinical facts.
- Emergency use: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] reports that staff and residents searched most often in ER settings and that [[PaulAppelbaum]] saw special value when patients are psychotic, intoxicated, or suicidal.
- Trust risk: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] describes a patient angered by Facebook-based hospitalization and cites [[JohnLuo]] on risks when disclosure affects a paranoid patient.
- Standard drift: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] quotes [[PaulAppelbaum]] arguing that what substantial numbers of clinicians do can become the clinical standard of care.
- Documentation consequence: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] warns that copied, summarized, or screenshotted online content can become part of the medical chart.
- Patient-requested use: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] reports patients sometimes ask clinicians to view YouTube or other profiles because relevant information is already online.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source relies on one hospital survey, a conference presentation, expert commentary, and an extreme case report; it does not establish national prevalence, outcome benefit, or the right legal rule. The practice may be more justified for imminent safety assessment than for curiosity during routine care. The article also frames social media use in cancer care more positively, showing that online clinician-patient engagement is not reducible to surveillance.
