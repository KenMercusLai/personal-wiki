## Definition
[[ResponsibilityDiffusion]] is the reduction in felt personal obligation that can occur when several people could act but no individual is clearly assigned ownership.

## Current Synthesis
[[MarkSuster]] applies this social mechanism to ordinary email. A request addressed to several possible responders creates an ownership gap: every recipient incurs the cost of reading and deciding, yet each can infer that another person will answer. A named primary recipient makes the action boundary explicit, while copied recipients can remain informed without being treated as co-owners. If the first owner does not respond, sequential reassignment preserves clarity better than simultaneous ambiguity.

This is best treated as a coordination risk rather than a universal law. Shared review, collective decisions, incident response, and safety-critical work may need several participants or deliberate redundancy, but they still benefit from an explicit coordinator, decision rule, or division of actions.

## Key Claims
- Group size can weaken response accountability when a request does not identify who should act.
- Ambiguous group requests duplicate attention costs while leaving the actual task unowned.
- Naming one action owner makes the requested obligation and the consequence of nonresponse more salient.
- Informational recipients should be distinguished from action owners through explicit wording and recipient roles.
- Sequential escalation can add a new owner without recreating simultaneous ambiguity.

## Evidence
- Unowned group request: [[never-ask-two-people-to-do-one-persons-job-both-sides-of-the-table]] describes deal introductions sent to several investors where each recipient can assume another will respond.
- Named ownership: [[never-ask-two-people-to-do-one-persons-job-both-sides-of-the-table]] recommends addressing one person for action and using copies only to inform.
- Internal recovery: [[never-ask-two-people-to-do-one-persons-job-both-sides-of-the-table]] reports that recipients sometimes repair ambiguity by declaring “I'll own this one” or nominating a colleague.
- Routing model: [[never-ask-two-people-to-do-one-persons-job-both-sides-of-the-table]] describes one recipient forwarding a deal to the colleague with the best expertise or capacity and recording the new owner.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The available evidence is one practitioner's short essay and does not measure response rates, group size, urgency, organizational hierarchy, or alternative explanations for nonresponse. The article invokes the bystander effect and diffusion of responsibility but does not summarize the research or establish that every email failure has the same mechanism. Multiple recipients can be appropriate when work is divisible, independent judgments are required, availability is uncertain, or redundancy is a safety control; in those cases, explicit coordination and action boundaries matter more than forcing a single participant.

## What Changed
- Established responsibility diffusion as the mechanism connecting ambiguous group requests to missing ownership.
- Distinguished one action owner from informational recipients and legitimate multi-person participation.
- Added sequential escalation and explicit internal claiming as ways to restore ownership.
