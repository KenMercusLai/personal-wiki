## Definition
[[ActiveInvesting]] is an investing mode where the investor supplies capital and also commits time, energy, intellect, control, governance, or operating work to improve the outcome.

## Current Synthesis
Wilson frames active investing as capital plus concentrated participation. The examples range from seeking board seats after buying a meaningful public-company stake to developing vacant land, making hard-money loans, or leading a seed round and taking a board role. The model can work, and Wilson prefers it for large gains, but its constraint is attention: active investing is difficult to scale because each position requires real work.

His USV pacing account makes that constraint operational. One to two new early-stage investments per partner per year, held for an assumed average of seven years, imply roughly seven to fourteen concurrent companies. If the firm promises partner-level service throughout that period, annual deal count, portfolio load, team support, and selection discipline must be designed together. The arithmetic illustrates Wilson's model; it does not establish a universal capacity ceiling or superior-return result.

## Key Claims
- Active investing is defined by participation and influence, not just by the asset being risky or private.
- Active investors often operate in illiquid markets where they cannot easily exit a bad position.
- Active investing requires time, energy, and intellect in addition to capital.
- The model favors concentration and controlled pacing because meaningful involvement cannot be spread across unlimited long-duration deals.
- Venture and angel investors can mislabel broad passive exposure as active support when portfolios become too large.

## Evidence
- Participation boundary: [[active-vs-passive-investing-avc]] gives board-seat activism, real-estate development, hard-money lending, and led seed rounds as active cases.
- Illiquidity: [[active-vs-passive-investing-avc]] says active investors are most likely investing in illiquid markets and must work to make things go well.
- Effort requirement: [[active-vs-passive-investing-avc]] argues that large gains require time, energy, and intellect as well as capital.
- Concentration: [[active-vs-passive-investing-avc]] says active investing limits the number of investments one can make.
- Mislabeling risk: [[active-vs-passive-investing-avc]] criticizes investors and firms that try to scale while pretending to be active.
- Operational capacity: [[investment-pace-avc]] connects one to two annual deals and a seven-year average hold to roughly seven to fourteen concurrent portfolio companies per partner.
- Service commitment: [[investment-pace-avc]] says partner bandwidth and supporting-team capacity are necessary to serve early-stage companies over seven to ten years.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources state and illustrate an active-investing capacity limit but do not test it across fund sizes, stages, portfolio workloads, operating models, team structures, or return cohorts. The seven-to-fourteen-company range is simple workload arithmetic rather than an observed performance threshold. Wilson's claim that thesis-driven active early-stage investing has always produced the best returns is not supported with comparative data, and the sources do not claim passive investing cannot earn good long-term returns.

## What Changed
- Added annual pacing, holding period, and concurrent portfolio load as operational dimensions of the attention constraint.
- Distinguished Wilson's firm-specific workload model from a universal capacity or return benchmark.
