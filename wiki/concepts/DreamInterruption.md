## Definition
[[DreamInterruption]] is the apparent loss of continuity in a remembered dream, which may reflect a change in sleep state or awakening, incomplete memory formation or retrieval, or another disruption of the dream experience.

## Current Synthesis
The available source proposes four possible routes by which a sexual dream may seem to end: REM sleep reaches a transition, an environmental or bodily stimulus wakes the sleeper, emotional or physiological excitement triggers awakening, or anxiety and conflict disturb the dream. Its most useful analytical distinction is between experience and report. A remembered narrative can be incomplete because the dream was interrupted, because waking occurred before the experience continued, or because later recall preserved only fragments.

None of the proposed mechanisms is established by the supplied evidence. The source is a short social-media explanation without citations, measurements, case observations, or a method for discriminating among sleep-stage transition, sensory awakening, physiological arousal, psychological disruption, and memory loss. Its author explicitly calls the account personal speculation. The current judgment is therefore that dream discontinuity and dream-recall discontinuity should be kept separate, while the causal explanations remain unverified hypotheses.

## Key Claims
- Apparent dream interruption can describe either a break in the experience or a gap in later memory.
- Sleep-stage transition and waking are proposed as possible boundaries that end a remembered dream episode.
- Environmental and bodily stimuli may matter insofar as they cause awakening.
- Strong emotion or sexual arousal is proposed as a possible awakening route, but no threshold or evidence is supplied.
- Anxiety, stress, guilt, and discomfort are proposed as psychological disruptions without a demonstrated mechanism.
- The source's explicit disclaimer makes every physiological and psychological explanation source-scoped rather than established.

## Evidence
- Sleep-state account: [[chun-meng-zhong-duan-de-sheng-li-ji-zhi]] links dream endings to REM completion, transition to another sleep stage, and awakening.
- External and bodily triggers: [[chun-meng-zhong-duan-de-sheng-li-ji-zhi]] names noise, light, discomfort, turning, and bladder pressure as possible causes of awakening.
- Arousal and psychological hypotheses: [[chun-meng-zhong-duan-de-sheng-li-ji-zhi]] proposes physiological excitement and anxiety, stress, guilt, or discomfort as possible disruptions.
- Memory distinction: [[chun-meng-zhong-duan-de-sheng-li-ji-zhi]] states that fragmentary recall may be mistaken for an actually interrupted dream.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence inventory contains one concise social-media post whose author explicitly says the explanation is personal speculation rather than scientific evidence. It cites no sleep study and provides no operational definitions, prevalence estimates, polysomnography, awakening protocol, dream reports, or comparison across REM and non-REM stages. “Light sleep,” an arousal “threshold,” and a psychological “reset” are explanatory labels rather than measured mechanisms in this source. Dream content and dream recall are also difficult to infer retrospectively, so an incomplete report cannot by itself reveal when or why the underlying experience ended. The page should not be used to diagnose sleep disturbance or infer an individual's psychological state.

## What Changed
- Established the distinction between interruption of dream experience and fragmentation of later dream recall.
- Organized the source's sleep-stage, sensory, bodily, arousal, and psychological proposals as separate hypotheses.
- Preserved the author's explicit disclaimer as the governing evidence boundary.
