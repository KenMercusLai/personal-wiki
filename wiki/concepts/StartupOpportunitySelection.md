## Definition
[[StartupOpportunitySelection]] is the judgment of whether to found, join, fund, or leave a startup by distinguishing the common availability of startup activity from the much rarer opportunity to help build an exceptional company.

## Current Synthesis
[[AndrewChen]] argues that proximity to Silicon Valley corrects one romantic misconception: starting a company and attracting some investment are not inherently rare. A dense ecosystem produces more attempts across the quality distribution—more mediocre companies as well as more great ones. The scarce outcome is a company with the scale and significance of Amazon, Google, Facebook, or Uber.

That distinction changes career choice. Founding is not automatically more valuable than joining, and persistence is not automatically superior to switching. Chen frames his move to Uber as choosing a high-potential “rocketship” over continuing with a mediocre opportunity, while also acknowledging that such judgments are uncertain and generate painful missed chances in hindsight.

## Key Claims
- Startup formation and external funding are weaker quality signals than outsiders may assume.
- A larger ecosystem produces more attempts across the whole quality distribution, not uniformly better founders.
- Exceptional companies remain rare even where mediocre startups are common.
- Joining a strong company can offer a more distinctive experience than founding a weak one.
- Missed opportunities are inevitable because exceptional outcomes are difficult to recognize early.

## Evidence
- Base-rate correction: [[andrewchen-10-years-in-the-bay-area]] says starting and funding a company became visibly commonplace after several years in the Bay Area.
- Attempt volume: [[andrewchen-10-years-in-the-bay-area]] relays Bill Gossman's view that Silicon Valley has more people trying, yielding more mediocre and more great companies rather than categorically better entrepreneurs.
- Scarce outcome: [[andrewchen-10-years-in-the-bay-area]] contrasts plentiful startups with Amazon-, Google-, Facebook-, or Uber-scale companies that emerge only occasionally.
- Join-versus-found decision: [[andrewchen-10-years-in-the-bay-area]] gives joining Uber as Chen's choice to participate in a potentially exceptional company rather than continue with the mediocre.
- Recognition uncertainty: [[andrewchen-10-years-in-the-bay-area]] cites Chen dismissing early Facebook and acquaintances passing on Uber's seed round as missed-opportunity examples.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The essay is a retrospective from a participant in a highly selected ecosystem and uses later winners to define what was exceptional. “Rocketship” judgments can rationalize prestige chasing, expose workers to concentrated equity and culture risk, or undervalue durable smaller companies. The source supplies no prospective scoring method, and outcomes depend on role, team, timing, compensation, learning, ethics, personal constraints, and luck as well as company trajectory.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to separate startup participation from exceptional-company quality and to include joining as a first-class entrepreneurial career choice.
