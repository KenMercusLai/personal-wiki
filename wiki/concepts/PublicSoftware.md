## Definition
[[PublicSoftware]] is [[NadiaEghbal]]'s proposed umbrella for software made accessible over the internet for people to view, use, or collaborate around, encompassing legally open-source software while also naming broader public-development practices.

## Current Synthesis
The concept separates two questions that ordinary “open source” usage can blur. A license determines legally protected permissions and distribution conditions; public development describes visibility, participation, stewardship, and social organization. Eghbal's initial essay stresses the cultural category because GitHub-era work is often described as open source even when it lacks a qualifying license or is not software at all.

Her follow-up narrows the proposal. Public software should not weaken or replace the open-source definition: licensing makes reuse and modification dependable. The broader term instead creates space to discuss who maintains shared resources, how socially valuable work receives time and compensation, and how people outside insider software culture can participate in those decisions.

## Key Claims
- Legal openness and public visibility are related but distinct properties.
- Open-source software is one form of public software, not a synonym for every publicly visible repository or artifact.
- Public projects require roles, stewardship, time, and compensation even when their output is broadly accessible.
- A more intuitive umbrella may make sustainability and collaboration legible to people outside open-source insider culture.
- Broader cultural language should complement rather than dilute enforceable user rights.

## Evidence
- Legal distinction: [[i-hate-the-term-open-source-nadia-eghbal-medium]] cites the Open Source Initiative's criteria and contrasts them with unlicensed GitHub repositories described colloquially as open source.
- Public-development norm: [[i-hate-the-term-open-source-nadia-eghbal-medium]] identifies building and collaborating in public as the newer everyday meaning.
- Labor and stewardship: [[i-hate-the-term-open-source-nadia-eghbal-medium]] argues that public-resource output can coexist with compensated work and a sense of responsibility for process.
- Accessible framing: [[i-hate-the-term-open-source-nadia-eghbal-medium]] proposes a private/public distinction intended to be understandable without software-community background.
- Rights boundary: [[i-hate-the-term-open-source-nadia-eghbal-medium]] later calls licensing essential and warns against restricting access, use, or the legal definition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source proposes terminology but supplies no user research showing that “public software” is clearer, no adoption evidence, and no operational test for ambiguous cases such as source-available licenses, public repositories without licenses, hosted services, open data, or non-software artifacts. “Public” can describe visibility without granting modification or redistribution rights, so using the umbrella without explicit license information may reproduce the ambiguity it is meant to solve. The historical claim about GitHub licensing relies on a cited 2015 platform analysis and should not be treated as current prevalence.

## What Changed
- Created the concept as a cultural umbrella that explicitly preserves the narrower legal protections of open source.
