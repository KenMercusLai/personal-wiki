## Definition
[[TargetUserDiscipline]] is the practice of deciding which users and problems a product will serve, testing whether adjacent demand represents a viable market, and declining requests that would fragment the product without creating a scalable opportunity.

## Current Synthesis
[[MichaelSeibel]] separates openness to discovery from obedience to every request. An off-target user can be strategically valuable when repeated behavior reveals a larger group, the new need is more acute, the economics remain viable, and the opportunity improves growth. The same user becomes a product risk when the request is isolated, requires materially different capabilities or costs, or turns the roadmap into bespoke consulting. The practical rule is therefore not "ignore unexpected users" but investigate the segment before changing direction, then say no courteously when it does not fit. [[JustinTV]]'s gaming broadcasters are the positive case: they were small but persistent, compatible with the service's main salary-and-bandwidth costs, and commercially attractive to advertisers.

## Key Claims
- Unexpected demand is a hypothesis about a segment, not automatic evidence that the company should pivot.
- Segment evaluation should test prevalence, problem intensity, cost-to-serve, business-model compatibility, and growth potential.
- Isolated custom requests can fragment a technology product into consulting work.
- Saying no to off-target users can protect service quality for the intended market.
- Narrow focus can make [[ProductMarketFit]] more legible by concentrating effort on making one group very satisfied.
- Persistent behavior with compatible economics can justify changing the target segment.

## Evidence
- Segment test: [[users-you-dont-want]] asks whether the user represents a larger group, changes or breaks the economics, or offers a better growth opportunity.
- Scope mismatch: [[users-you-dont-want]] uses a babysitting marketplace to show how adjacent customers can require materially different training and service design.
- Consulting risk: [[users-you-dont-want]] warns that solving one-off problems for individual users is a consulting model rather than a scalable technology-product strategy.
- Focus and refusal: [[users-you-dont-want]] argues that courteous refusal lets a startup serve its target users better instead of half-solving many problems.
- Positive pivot case: [[users-you-dont-want]] says Justin.tv's gaming broadcasters formed a small but consistent group, did not materially alter major costs, and opened stronger advertising possibilities.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source provides a decision frame, not thresholds for determining when a group is large, consistent, or economically attractive enough to justify a pivot. Its Justin.tv example is retrospective and can make a successful segment shift appear easier to identify than it was: Seibel says the company took four to five years to appreciate the gaming group. Refusing adjacent users can also hide accessibility needs, emerging markets, or defects in the original segmentation, so teams need evidence rather than using "focus" as a blanket reason to dismiss inconvenient feedback. The babysitting analogy relies on simplified and dated assumptions about disability and should not be treated as evidence about care markets.

## What Changed
- Created a segment-level decision rule that distinguishes scalable adjacent demand from isolated roadmap hijacking.
- Added cost compatibility and monetization potential to the tests for whether persistent unexpected behavior merits a pivot.
