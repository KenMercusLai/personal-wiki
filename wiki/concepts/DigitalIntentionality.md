## Definition
[[DigitalIntentionality]] is the deliberate design of when, where, and for what purpose a person uses connected technology so access serves chosen activities and relationships instead of becoming an ambient default.

## Current Synthesis
The available source treats intentionality as an environmental-design problem as much as a willpower problem. A smartphone stripped to a task list, music, email, and Signal still enabled overdue reminders, newsletter scrolling, and immediate social sharing. The author's dumbphone experiment therefore moves the boundary outside individual apps: a less capable device makes internet use slower, more situated, and less rewarding while retaining calls, SMS, navigation, and a limited hotspot.

That boundary is neither complete nor costless. The hotspot keeps laptop access available, and contemporary parking, restaurant, and retail workflows may assume a QR scanner, browser, or app. The useful claim is not that older technology is inherently healthier, but that deliberate friction can reveal which conveniences are worth preserving and which habits were sustained mainly by constant availability.

## Key Claims
- Removing applications can reduce stimuli without changing the deeper habit of carrying immediate access everywhere.
- Physical separation, limited interfaces, and deliberate friction can support chosen boundaries when settings-based restraint remains porous.
- A temporary device substitution can function as a behavioral experiment for observing habits rather than as a permanent or universal prescription.
- Visible technology use affects [[RoleModeling]] because children learn norms of presence and connection from adult behavior.
- Intentional disconnection has infrastructural costs where ordinary services presume smartphone ownership and continuous connectivity.

## Evidence
Residual attention capture:
- [[im-switching-to-a-dumbphone-this-summer]] says a browser-disabled, minimal-app smartphone still enabled task reminders, newsletter scrolling, and reflexive Signal sharing.

Environmental boundary:
- [[im-switching-to-a-dumbphone-this-summer]] contrasts temporary, location-bound early internet access with an internet that now follows the user, and frames a three-month dumbphone trial as a reset.

Role modeling:
- [[im-switching-to-a-dumbphone-this-summer]] reports concern that a child sees adults scrolling, working through tasks, and normalizing constant connection.

Tradeoffs and retained access:
- [[im-switching-to-a-dumbphone-this-summer]] reports useful calls, SMS, navigation, and a 4G hotspot alongside problems with QR-based parking, app-mediated restaurant ordering, and IKEA warehouse lookup.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept currently rests on one first-person plan and first-impressions report, not a completed experiment or comparative behavioral study. The author supplies no baseline, screen-time measure, wellbeing outcome, follow-up, or evidence that friction changes habits after smartphone access returns. Calls, maps, hotspots, accessibility tools, emergency information, authentication, transport, payments, care, and work can make mobile access beneficial or necessary. Deliberate friction can protect attention but can also transfer work to other people, reduce independence, or exclude someone from services designed around smartphone ownership.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to distinguish purpose-shaped access from merely reducing notification volume or installed apps.
- Added physical and infrastructural friction as both a behavioral support and a source of exclusion or inconvenience.
- Added visible adult device use as a role-modeling dimension of digital boundaries.
