## Definition
[[SurvivorshipBias]] is the distortion created when visible successes dominate the evidence set, making the practices and attributes of winners appear more reliably causal than they are while failed, abandoned, or lucky paths receive less attention.

## Current Synthesis
In the source's business-case setting, favorable outcomes cast a halo over a whole organizational system. Revenue, user growth, or strong reviews can make the team's structure, roles, tools, culture, and decisions all look correct, even when some elements were irrelevant, harmful, offset by other strengths, or assisted by luck and serendipity. Most accessible stories are also told by successful companies and people, so the sample itself overrepresents winners and corporate heroics.

Failure narratives can produce a mirror-image error: once the outcome is bad, every visible feature appears obviously wrong even though failed organizations may contain valuable practices. Better learning compares mechanisms and context, looks for missing cases and alternatives, and avoids treating outcome knowledge as proof that every component caused the result.

## Key Claims
- Winner-centered evidence overrepresents successful companies, people, and practices.
- A good outcome can make every visible system attribute look causally correct.
- Retrospective success stories tend to suppress luck, serendipity, failed alternatives, and internal disagreement.
- Failed outcomes can invert the halo, making useful components look obviously defective.
- Transfer requires examining mechanisms, interactions, context, and missing cases rather than copying winners' visible features.

## Evidence
- Selection problem: [[use-and-misuse-of-business-case-studies-learning-by-shipping]] says most available stories are told by companies or people that succeeded.
- Success halo: [[use-and-misuse-of-business-case-studies-learning-by-shipping]] argues that strong revenue, reviews, or user results make all identifiable team attributes appear right.
- Narrative cleanup: [[use-and-misuse-of-business-case-studies-learning-by-shipping]] says case stories can overemphasize corporate heroics and remove luck or serendipity.
- Failure inversion: [[use-and-misuse-of-business-case-studies-learning-by-shipping]] notes that failed teams are often treated as though their structure, people, tools, and decisions were all wrong, despite useful elements within the failure.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source identifies the bias through practitioner observation rather than a measured sample of business cases. A winning organization's practice can genuinely contribute to success, and a failed organization's practice can genuinely be defective; the bias concerns overgeneralization from outcome-selected evidence, not a rule that outcomes reveal nothing. Reducing it requires broader comparison and causal investigation that the source recommends in principle but does not operationalize.

## What Changed
- Created the concept from Sinofsky's warning that successful and failed business cases distort attribution in opposite directions.
