## Definition
[[IntangibleAssetEconomics]] is the economic behavior of assets such as software, data, brands, research, training, media, and other non-physical investments whose costs, reuse, spillovers, valuation, and policy needs differ from tangible goods.

## Current Synthesis
[[BillGates]] uses [[CapitalismWithoutCapital]] to argue that a growing share of the economy no longer behaves like the tangible goods assumed in introductory supply-and-demand examples. The article's core contrast is cost structure: a car, farm product, or property asset keeps requiring materials and labor as output grows, while a software program can be costly to create once and almost free to copy afterward. Gates treats this as a macroeconomic transition, not merely a software-industry quirk. When investment shifts toward software, data, branding, training, research, insurance, e-books, or movies, old tools for valuation, competition policy, taxation, GDP measurement, and regional economic development become less reliable.

## Key Claims
- Intangible assets often have high first-unit creation costs and very low reproduction costs.
- Traditional supply-and-demand and production-cost assumptions become less useful as intangible investment becomes a larger share of the economy.
- Intangible-heavy companies can be difficult for investors to value when they are used to tangible assets and conventional capital expenditure.
- Public measurement systems can undercount economic activity when they omit software-like, brand, research, training, or organizational investments.
- The policy questions are broad rather than settled: intellectual property, competition, taxation, stimulus, and regional development all need reconsideration.

## Evidence
- Cost structure: [[capitalism-without-capital-bill-gates]] contrasts Ford's repeated vehicle material and labor costs with Microsoft's high development cost for the first software unit and near-zero cost for later copies.
- Expanding scope: [[capitalism-without-capital-bill-gates]] lists software, data, insurance, e-books, and movies as examples of assets that do not fit the old model.
- Investor valuation: [[capitalism-without-capital-bill-gates]] says Gates had to explain Microsoft's asset logic to early investors who could not yet imagine its long-term returns.
- Measurement gap: [[capitalism-without-capital-bill-gates]] says U.S. GDP did not include software until 1999 and still excludes market research, branding, and training investment.
- Policy scope: [[capitalism-without-capital-bill-gates]] raises patent and trademark law, competition policy, taxation, and stimulus as open debates for intangible-heavy economies.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is Gates's review of Haskel and Westlake's book rather than a full empirical summary of the book's evidence. It praises the book's four-part framework for intangible investment behavior, but the markdown excerpt does not include the four reasons themselves. The concept also should not imply that all intangible businesses are automatically profitable, defensible, or socially beneficial; Gates explicitly frames the traits as different rather than inherently good or bad.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to capture Gates's macroeconomic extension of software's near-zero marginal-copy-cost structure into measurement and policy questions.
