## Definition
[[CaliforniaRollRule]] is [[NirEyal]]’s product-design heuristic that meaningful novelty becomes easier to adopt when it is introduced through ingredients, metaphors, controls, or routines users already recognize.

## Current Synthesis
The rule treats familiarity as a bridge rather than the product’s destination. A new product can preserve enough of an existing [[MentalModels|mental model]] for users to predict an initial action, experience the new value, and learn the unfamiliar system incrementally. The California roll supplies the metaphor: familiar ingredients lowered the perceived distance to sushi without making the result identical to established American food.

The source applies the same pattern to graphical desktops, card-like mobile-payment interfaces, and the Apple Watch Digital Crown. In each case, a recognizable surface is meant to reduce [[CognitiveOverheadInProductDesign|cognitive overhead]] or the “non-routine” cost in the [[FoggBehaviorModel]]. Once the behavior becomes learned, literal imitation can be reduced. The useful design question is therefore not merely “What looks familiar?” but “Which accurate, recognizable cue lets a user experience the genuinely new value without creating a misleading model?”

## Key Claims
- Familiar cues can lower the learning and interpretation cost of a novel product or behavior.
- A gateway should preserve the product’s new value rather than disguise it as an unchanged old product.
- Familiar mental models are most useful when they accurately predict the first important actions and outcomes.
- Familiar scaffolding can be removed or simplified after the target interaction becomes routine.
- Adoption depends on more than interface recognition, so familiarity should be tested alongside value, trust, distribution, timing, and product quality.

## Evidence
- Food analogy: [[people-dont-want-something-truly-new-they-want-the-familiar-done-differently]] says the California roll rearranged mostly familiar ingredients into an accessible introduction to sushi.
- Graphical-interface bridge: [[people-dont-want-something-truly-new-they-want-the-familiar-done-differently]] points to desktop folders, windows, disks, and trash cans as recognizable metaphors for personal computing.
- Payment behavior: [[people-dont-want-something-truly-new-they-want-the-familiar-done-differently]] shows Passbook and Wallet rendering digital payment options as physical-looking cards.
- Progressive removal: [[people-dont-want-something-truly-new-they-want-the-familiar-done-differently]] quotes [[JonyIve]] saying touch-screen familiarity reduced the need for physical-button imagery.
- Behavioral mechanism: [[people-dont-want-something-truly-new-they-want-the-familiar-done-differently]] cites [[BJFogg]]’s claim that non-routine behavior feels less simple and that training demands effort.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The single source is a 2015 practitioner essay built from analogy and selected Apple examples, not a comparative adoption study. It does not isolate familiarity from brand, price, distribution, product quality, social proof, timing, or complementary infrastructure. Familiar metaphors can also import false constraints, hide important system differences, exclude users who do not share the assumed convention, or make a genuinely better interaction harder to discover. A familiar surface is therefore a hypothesis about learnability, not proof of value or adoption.

## What Changed
- Created the concept as a qualified gateway heuristic rather than a general claim that users reject all novelty.
- Connected recognizable entry cues to mental models, cognitive overhead, and non-routine behavior cost.
