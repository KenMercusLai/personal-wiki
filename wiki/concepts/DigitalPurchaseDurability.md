## Definition
[[DigitalPurchaseDurability]] is the extent to which a purchased digital good remains accessible, compatible, useful, and transferable as devices, operating systems, stores, services, and consumption habits change.

## Current Synthesis
The source separates value already received from value that remains. Hundreds of CDs and DVDs could provide years of listening and viewing even after resale prices collapsed and playback hardware disappeared from the author's home. Digital goods do not remove this depreciation pattern. An app may be paid for and still depend on a platform owner maintaining distribution, compatibility, device assumptions, and supporting infrastructure.

Durability is therefore layered rather than binary. A good can remain technically executable yet lose practical value because its canvas, typography, interaction conventions, or surrounding ecosystem no longer fit current devices. Format migration can extend access, as CD ripping did for music, but a later distribution shift such as streaming can reduce the practical value of the migrated files. Purchase price proves neither perpetual access nor perpetual usefulness; it records an exchange whose value may be consumed over time.

## Key Claims
- Past use value can be real even when current resale or practical value approaches zero.
- Physical and digital purchases both depend on supporting playback or execution environments.
- Platform compatibility, not payment alone, determines whether an old app remains usable.
- A digital good can degrade in experiential value before it becomes technically inaccessible.
- Format migration can preserve access without guaranteeing that the migrated copy will remain practically valuable.

## Evidence
- Value already realized: [[worth-less-to-worthless-500ish]] says an estimated $7,500 CD and DVD collection delivered substantial enjoyment despite later becoming nearly worthless to the author.
- Physical dependency: [[worth-less-to-worthless-500ish]] notes that discs retain some market value where players remain, while the author's own home no longer had a CD or DVD player.
- Platform dependency: [[worth-less-to-worthless-500ish]] argues that purchased apps depend on iOS, Android, and their backward-compatibility decisions continuing to support them.
- Partial degradation: [[worth-less-to-worthless-500ish]] reports that some early apps crashed while most still ran, but with shrunken layouts, heavy typography, and dated skeuomorphic styling on an iPhone 6s Plus.
- Migration limit: [[worth-less-to-worthless-500ish]] says ripping CDs into MP3s extended their use, while streaming later reduced the practical value of those local copies.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a personal essay, not a longitudinal study of media resale prices, app survival, licensing, or platform compatibility. Its claim that the collection was “worthless” primarily means worthless to the author rather than literally valueless: other households still owned players, trade-in markets remained, and the goods had already produced consumption value. The essay also does not distinguish locally stored software from subscription services, server-dependent apps, DRM-protected media, or digital goods with export and emulation paths, all of which can have different durability.

## What Changed
- Created a layered durability model separating past use value, present practical value, technical compatibility, and resale value.
- Identified platform compatibility and interface fit as distinct ways a paid app can lose value.
- Added format migration as a temporary preservation strategy rather than a guarantee of lasting usefulness.
