## Definition
[[WorkplaceIncentiveDesign]] is the deliberate shaping of rewards, penalties, metrics, prices, and feedback loops so daily workplace behavior aligns with organizational goals.

## Current Synthesis
The sources show incentive design operating through both explicit prices and implicit social signals. At [[Disco]], meeting-room charges, desk and PC costs, work-assignment auctions, team billing, overtime penalties, help payments, Colosseum wagers, and internal crowdfunding push employees to ask whether an action is worth its cost. In vacation policy, removing a fixed allowance does not remove incentives: it can replace a visible entitlement with peer comparison, managerial discretion, and guilt about appearing insufficiently dedicated.

Both cases show why stated freedom is an incomplete test. A system may give employees choice while still steering behavior through uncertain bonuses, internal competition, unwritten ceilings, or workload conflicts. Incentive design therefore needs observed behavioral measures, attention to outliers, and safeguards against pressure and short-term optimization.

## Key Claims
- Incentives shape everyday behavior most strongly when they are visible at the moment of choice.
- Pricing shared resources can reduce casual overuse by making opportunity cost concrete.
- Penalties can redirect unwanted behavior such as overtime or excess inventory, but they also signal what management values.
- Rewards and bonuses can make contribution legible, yet uncertain payouts can intensify pressure.
- Incentive systems need horizon control because quarterly or easily measured targets can crowd out longer-term work.
- Removing a formal rule can create stronger informal incentives through ambiguity, comparison, and status.
- Policy evaluation should measure actual behavior and distributional outliers rather than infer success from stated intent.

## Evidence
- Meeting behavior: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] reports employees cut back on useless meetings after room charges.
- Assignment selection: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] says workers bid for tasks and unwanted tasks often prove unnecessary.
- Overtime penalty: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] reports overtime hours falling 9% after penalties were implemented in 2015.
- Helping market: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] says employees can pay colleagues to finish reports or provide coding and translation help.
- Bonus pressure: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] says quarterly bonuses can rival annual pay for top performers, but payouts are not guaranteed.
- Horizon risk: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] quotes Waseda professor Takashi Shimizu warning that senior management must avoid a short-term loop.
- Informal leave ceiling: [[unlimited-vacation-and-other-forms-of-guilt-based-management]] argues that unlimited vacation remains bounded but makes employees infer the boundary through managers and peers.
- Alternative signals: [[unlimited-vacation-and-other-forms-of-guilt-based-management]] contrasts ambiguous leave with minimums, quota relief, use bonuses, closures, and measurement of low-use outliers.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not prove that every workplace should price or formalize every behavior. Some valuable work is collective, slow, ambiguous, or relational and can be harmed by narrow measurement. Disco is a single high-intensity company case, while the vacation article is practitioner argument and acknowledges unsettled comparative evidence. Incentive effects also depend on trust, managerial integrity, labor-market options, legal entitlements, payout fairness, workload, and whether workers can challenge distorted metrics or unwritten norms.

## What Changed
- Expanded incentive design from explicit internal prices to informal guilt, comparison, and unwritten policy ceilings.
- Added actual usage and outlier analysis as tests of whether a workplace policy produces its intended behavior.
