## Definition
[[ProductDemandAlignment]] is the practice of connecting a product’s beneficial long-term outcome to an immediate result, experience, or identity that intended customers already want enough to act on.

## Current Synthesis
A need describes a problem or beneficial change; demand requires observable willingness to start, pay, choose, or continue. The source argues that builders should not assume the importance of health, environmental, relational, or business progress will supply motivation by itself. They should identify the short-term reward that makes the first step desirable while preserving the longer-term value that justifies continued use.

This produces a two-stage product test. Positioning and the first experience must make the desired progress emotionally and practically legible now; onboarding must then lead quickly to a product-specific success and continue delivering value. LA Weight Loss, Tesla, Dating Ring, and invoicing, proposal, and email software are illustrative hypotheses about this alignment, not proof that one reward structure works across markets.

## Key Claims
- Important needs do not automatically create purchase, activation, or retention behavior.
- Immediate rewards can make delayed benefits easier to choose and sustain.
- Product positioning should express an outcome customers desire, not merely the builder’s rational account of what is good for them.
- Onboarding should shorten the path to a meaningful product-specific success rather than celebrate account setup alone.
- Initial desirability and durable value are separate requirements; optimizing only the quick win can create shallow or manipulative adoption.

## Evidence
Need versus demand:
- [[people-need-my-product-but-do-they-want-it]] contrasts widely acknowledged health and environmental needs with the behavior required to act on them.
- [[people-need-my-product-but-do-they-want-it]] reports that Dating Ring’s rational no-photo premise produced weak signups until photos and preference control returned.

Immediate reward:
- [[people-need-my-product-but-do-they-want-it]] uses an appearance-focused weight-loss advertisement and Tesla’s performance, luxury, and status to illustrate near-term rewards attached to longer-term benefits.
- [[people-need-my-product-but-do-they-want-it]] attributes to [[JamesClear]] a timing model in which short-term pain weakens pursuit of delayed benefit unless a nearer reward is added.

Activation and continuing value:
- [[people-need-my-product-but-do-they-want-it]] uses [[RobWalling]]’s “Minimum Path to Awesome” to define onboarding milestones such as sending an invoice or gaining a first subscriber.
- [[people-need-my-product-but-do-they-want-it]] explicitly pairs a quick win with continuing value rather than treating activation as the whole product.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is grounded in one 2017 practitioner essay using selected examples rather than comparative research. It provides no causal measurement of positioning, activation, retention, willingness to pay, or long-term outcomes. What customers say they want may differ from revealed behavior, and a quick win can be entertaining, status-driven, deceptive, or disconnected from durable value. Some products also address mandatory, infrequent, safety-critical, regulated, or collective needs where trust, compliance, switching cost, or institutional purchasing matters more than immediate emotional reward. Demand alignment should therefore be tested through actual behavior and welfare-aware outcomes, not inferred from persuasive messaging alone.

## What Changed
- Created a two-stage synthesis separating immediate desirability and activation from continuing customer value.
- Preserved the ethical and evidentiary boundary between creating a useful near-term reward and exploiting emotion without durable benefit.
