## Definition
[[ArtAsObjectTension]] is the aesthetic claim that art works by joining a withdrawn real object with sensuous qualities, creating tension between visible appearance and hidden reality.

## Current Synthesis
The source follows [[GrahamHarman]] in treating art as a relation between real objects and sensual qualities. Ordinary experience can let a sensual object and its qualities fit together smoothly, but art disrupts that fit: it makes the viewer feel a hidden life of things through visible or sensuous form. The essay's example of [[VincentVanGogh]]'s painted shoes shows art revealing more than the everyday tool-character of shoes while still not giving total access to the real object.

## Key Claims
- Art differs from ordinary perception because it creates tension between what appears and what remains hidden.
- The relevant aesthetic relation is real object to sensual quality rather than a simple copy of familiar appearance.
- Artistic depiction can make a tool or object feel like it belongs to a different, deeper world.
- Art discloses hidden aspects of objects without fully revealing the thing itself.
- The viewer's experience is necessary to complete the aesthetic contact between sensuous presentation and withdrawn reality.

## Evidence
- RO-SQ tension: [[cong-hai-de-ge-er-de-chui-zi-dao-yin-yu-de-xi-ju-xing]] describes art as the tension and fusion of real object and sensual experience.
- Ordinary contrast: [[cong-hai-de-ge-er-de-chui-zi-dao-yin-yu-de-xi-ju-xing]] contrasts art with the smooth fit of sensual object and sensual quality in daily experience.
- Van Gogh example: [[cong-hai-de-ge-er-de-chui-zi-dao-yin-yu-de-xi-ju-xing]] says the painted shoes are not merely everyday tools but seem to disclose the life of shoes.
- Limit of disclosure: [[cong-hai-de-ge-er-de-chui-zi-dao-yin-yu-de-xi-ju-xing]] asks whether the revealed hidden object is truly the thing itself and answers through continued withdrawal.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This concept is source-specific and depends on Harman's object-oriented aesthetic vocabulary. The article does not compare this account with other theories of art such as expression, representation, formalism, institutional theory, or reader-response aesthetics.
