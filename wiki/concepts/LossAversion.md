## Definition
[[LossAversion]] is the tendency for anticipated or experienced losses to weigh more heavily in decisions than comparable gains.

## Current Synthesis
The token-incentive source invokes loss aversion to explain why an employee who buys a project's tokens might work harder than one who received tokens without paying. Norton's 10x essay applies the mechanism to both project selection and incumbent strategy: people may prefer a 99% chance of a modest gain to a 1% chance of a much larger gain, while Kodak managers protected highly profitable film from a digital successor invented inside the company.

Together the sources separate salience from judgment. Personal downside can strengthen motivation, and probable loss to a current business can dominate attention, but neither response establishes the choice that best serves workers or long-run company value. Loss exposure can produce stress, concentrated risk, defensive capital allocation, or short-term attempts to protect a price or profit stream. Expected value can reveal one distortion without settling decisions where survival, variance, timing, or irreversible harm matter.

## Key Claims
- Personally funded exposure can make failure feel more consequential than losing only unrealized upside.
- A stronger motive to avoid loss is not necessarily a motive to create durable product or organizational value.
- Incentive designs that deliberately use downside exposure must account for worker welfare, concentration, power, and conduct risk.
- Incumbent profit can become a psychologically and organizationally salient asset whose protection crowds out uncertain successor investment.
- Low-probability high-upside choices require risk-capacity analysis as well as arithmetic expected value.

## Evidence
- Employee token purchases: [[employee-incentives-in-a-tokenized-world-token-economy]] hypothesizes that later employees who buy tokens may be more motivated than early employees who receive them free because purchasers can lose their own money.
- Incentive qualification: [[employee-incentives-in-a-tokenized-world-token-economy]] also warns that liquid token pricing can encourage short-term marketing over sustainable product work.
- Project-choice illustration: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] compares a 99% chance of $1 million with a 1% chance of $1 billion and argues that most people underweight the latter's higher expected value.
- Incumbent-defense case: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] connects Kodak's reluctance to advance digital photography with fear of damaging its highly profitable film market.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources cite or illustrate the general idea but supply no experiment, employee comparison, effect estimate, or causal organizational analysis. The token essay's “twice as powerful” phrasing is not established within the article and should not be treated as a universal coefficient. Norton's arithmetic example omits ruin, variance, repetition, timing, liquidity, and the learning value of each project, while the Kodak anecdote compresses a much larger corporate history. Motivation strength, decision quality, useful effort, worker welfare, and long-run strategy are distinct outcomes; loss exposure can worsen some while increasing another.
