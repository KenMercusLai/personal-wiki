## Definition
[[LossAversion]] is the tendency for anticipated or experienced losses to weigh more heavily in decisions than comparable gains.

## Current Synthesis
The token-incentive source invokes loss aversion to explain why an employee who buys a project's tokens might work harder than one who received tokens without paying. That mechanism is plausible within the essay's argument because the purchaser has personal capital at risk, but it does not establish better judgment or company outcomes. The same exposure can produce stress, excessive risk concentration, defensive choices, or short-term attempts to protect the token price.

## Key Claims
- Personally funded exposure can make failure feel more consequential than losing only unrealized upside.
- A stronger motive to avoid loss is not necessarily a motive to create durable product or organizational value.
- Incentive designs that deliberately use downside exposure must account for worker welfare, concentration, power, and conduct risk.

## Evidence
- Employee token purchases: [[employee-incentives-in-a-tokenized-world-token-economy]] hypothesizes that later employees who buy tokens may be more motivated than early employees who receive them free because purchasers can lose their own money.
- Incentive qualification: [[employee-incentives-in-a-tokenized-world-token-economy]] also warns that liquid token pricing can encourage short-term marketing over sustainable product work.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source cites the general idea but supplies no experiment, employee comparison, effect estimate, or organizational outcome data for token holders. Its “twice as powerful” phrasing is not established within the article and should not be treated as a universal coefficient. Motivation strength, decision quality, useful effort, and employee welfare are distinct outcomes; loss exposure can worsen some while increasing another.

## What Changed
- Created the concept with an explicit boundary between stronger downside salience and evidence of better work or outcomes.
