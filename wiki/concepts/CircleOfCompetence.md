## Definition
[[CircleOfCompetence]] is the bounded set of domains in which a person has built, through experience or study, enough real understanding to evaluate situations and make decisions rather than guess.

## Current Synthesis
The Farnam Street essay presents the circle of competence as [[WarrenBuffett]]'s way of avoiding problems rather than a formula for maximizing returns. Buffett's 1996 shareholder letter supplies the rule the essay is built on: an investor does not have to be an expert on every company, or even many, only on the companies inside the circle, and "the size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital." The essay's reason for preferring boundary knowledge over breadth is that mistakes cluster where people stray outside what they actually understand, and that the discipline depends on honesty - including the willingness to say "I don't know" - because the boundary is only as real as the person's own assessment of it. The circle is not fixed, but it grows slowly: it can be widened over time and not by assertion. Buffett's Mrs. B example is used to show that narrowness can be an advantage, since her devotion to cash, furniture, and real estate produced the focus that let her succeed while refusing to own stocks she did not understand. [[CharlieMunger]] makes the wider claim, applying the same logic to how a person should spend a limited life: find your aptitudes, avoid games where others hold them, and play where you have an edge. Farnam Street files the model as one element of its latticework of mental models rather than as investing-specific advice.

## Key Claims
- The circle of competence is knowledge built through experience or study, not exposure to information; domains differ in how much specialty they require before a judgment is worth making.
- Knowing the boundary of the circle matters more than its size, and straying outside that boundary is the essay's stated origin of most mistakes.
- The circle can be widened, but only slowly and over time.
- Competence is domain-specific: a person can be highly capable in one area and deliberately abstain in another, as Mrs. B did with stocks.
- A narrow circle can be an advantage rather than a handicap because it produces focus.
- Munger generalizes the model from investing to life: play games where you have an edge rather than where other people have the aptitude.
- The discipline is as much about self-honesty - refusing to fool yourself about where the boundary sits - as about accumulated knowledge.

## Evidence
Definition and domain specificity:
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] defines the circle as useful knowledge built through experience or study, and contrasts areas most people understand with areas that require much more specialty to evaluate.
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] uses a restaurant's basic economics, learnable with some accounting and a little study, against microchip and biotech companies that most readers cannot judge at the same level.

Boundary over size:
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] quotes Buffett's 1996 shareholder letter that an investor only has to evaluate companies within the circle, and that knowing its boundaries is vital.
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] says mistakes are most often made when the discipline of staying inside the area is abandoned.

Gradual widening:
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] states that the circle can be widened, but only slowly and over time.

Deliberate abstention inside a narrow circle:
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] has Buffett describing Mrs. B, who understands cash, furniture, and real estate, buys end tables and odd-lot carpets on the spot, and "wouldn't buy 100 shares of General Motors if it was at 50 cents a share".
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] argues that her rigid devotion to that narrow area allowed the focus behind her success, so narrowness cost her nothing.

Application beyond investing:
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] quotes Munger's prescription to figure out your own aptitudes, find where you have an edge, and play within your circle of competence.
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] contrasts the near-hopeless goal of becoming the world's best tennis player with the attainable goal of becoming the best plumbing contractor in a small town, and describes the circle as partly innate and partly developed through work.

Honesty and expansion:
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] closes by telling readers to define the perimeter, operate inside it, expand over time, never fool themselves about where it stands today, and never be afraid to say "I don't know".

Framework home:
- [[understanding-your-circle-of-competence-farnam-street]] places the model in Farnam Street's latticework of mental models rather than treating it as a standalone investing rule.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The wiki holds this concept from a single 2013 Farnam Street essay that assembles Buffett's shareholder letter, a Buffett talk, and Munger's remarks; it reports no research, measurement, or outcome data. The boundary is located by the decision-maker's own self-assessment, which is exactly the judgment the wiki's other material treats as unreliable - a person who is wrong about where their competence ends cannot detect that error from within this frame. The essay does not say how to test competence, what to do when a domain shifts underneath an existing circle, how to weigh partial competence at the edge, or whether deliberately staying inside a circle beats delegating the judgment to a diversified or passive alternative. Its examples come from investing and from a furniture retailer's operating life, so the life-strategy extension rests on analogy rather than evidence, and the widely repeated "best plumbing contractor in Bemidji" figure of two-thirds of readers is rhetorical.
