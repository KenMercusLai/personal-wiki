## Definition
[[ValueHacking]] is a proposed product-growth practice that identifies distinct customer value functions, then tests new combinations of segment, use case, capability, and offering rather than optimizing one aggregate growth metric indefinitely.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats growth as a lagging signal of repeated customer judgments that a product is worth choosing. North Star metrics and activation milestones can summarize those judgments, but neither is the underlying value: a team can inflate a metric without improving the customer outcome, and an “a-ha” moment does not guarantee future choice or retention.

Value hacking begins when ordinary [[GrowthHacking]] approaches a local maximum. Instead of continuing to tune the same proposition for the whole market, a team clusters customers by behavior and perceived value, chooses a more coherent segment, forms a hypothesis about its use case, and measures a focused experiment against that segment. It can also revisit the offering when technology or culture changes the value proposition. The method therefore resembles segment-specific product discovery and adjacency strategy more than a replacement for disciplined growth work.

## Key Claims
- Customer value is the causal target; growth and North Star metrics are lagging or proxy measures.
- Activation milestones show that value was discovered once but do not establish durable preference or retention.
- Repeated optimization of one proposition can reach a local maximum.
- Different customer segments can have different value functions and should not always be evaluated through one aggregate metric.
- New use cases can combine existing assets with different offerings to open adjacent markets.
- Focused segment-level experiments should confirm or reject explicit value hypotheses.

## Evidence
- Metric-versus-value distinction: [[growth-hacking-vs-value-hacking-by]] describes an ecommerce company increasing GMV in artificial ways that did not improve matching between buyers and sellers.
- Retention boundary: [[growth-hacking-vs-value-hacking-by]] treats famous activation milestones as evidence that users found value, while warning that they still choose among alternatives repeatedly.
- Optimization limit: [[growth-hacking-vs-value-hacking-by]] uses local and global extrema to argue that tuning one value proposition can plateau before the broader opportunity is exhausted.
- Segment method: [[growth-hacking-vs-value-hacking-by]] recommends clustering customers by usage, identifying their use cases and value perceptions, and measuring experiments within those clusters.
- Adjacency examples: [[growth-hacking-vs-value-hacking-by]] points to McCafé, Steam, WeChat payments and social media, and Gojek's service expansion as changes in the value function being served.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The framework comes from one practitioner essay and is not supported by controlled comparisons, segment-level experiment results, retention data, or unit economics. “Value function” remains an informal metaphor rather than a defined measurement model, and a local plateau can reflect execution, channel saturation, competition, measurement error, or insufficient statistical power rather than exhaustion of the proposition. Adjacent expansion can dilute focus, increase operational complexity, and transfer poorly across markets. The company examples are selective retrospectives, and the Gojek example is an employer-linked promotional claim.
