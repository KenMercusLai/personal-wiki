## Definition
[[PsychologicalReactance]] is resistance that can arise when a person perceives an attempt to constrain autonomy, sometimes creating motivation to reject or oppose the intended behavior.

## Current Synthesis
The source applies reactance to familiar commercial nudges. In a hotel-booking scenario, repeated scarcity and popularity messages were often interpreted not as credible information but as attempts to create anxiety and force a quick decision. This suggests a feedback loop: exposure teaches audiences to recognize a tactic, inferred manipulative intent changes the signal's meaning, and a prompt intended to increase conversion can instead produce distrust, negative emotion, avoidance, or preference for a competitor.

The evidence supports a contextual warning rather than a universal rule. The study bundles scarcity and social-proof statements, asks about a hypothetical booking situation, and reports stated interpretations and intentions rather than completed purchases. Its broader claims about other nudges, sectors, prosocial uses, and long-run intervention resistance remain hypotheses.

## Key Claims
- Perceived coercive intent can reverse a persuasion attempt by making autonomy defense more salient than the offered action.
- Repetition changes interpretation: a once-informative cue can become a recognizable sales script.
- Trust and emotional response are material intervention outcomes even when short-term conversion is the immediate goal.
- Reactance depends on audience, context, perceived intent, and prior exposure rather than belonging permanently to a tactic.
- Commercial overuse may create spillovers for other practitioners, but the available source does not test cross-domain resistance.

## Evidence
- Pressure and trust: [[consumers-are-becoming-wise-to-your-nudge-behavioral-scientist]] reports that 65% read the tested hotel claims as sales pressure, 49% expected to distrust the company, and only 16% believed the claims.
- Negative response: [[consumers-are-becoming-wise-to-your-nudge-behavioral-scientist]] reports that 34% selected negative emotions including contempt and disgust and connects their comments to perceived anxiety induction.
- Oppositional tendency: [[consumers-are-becoming-wise-to-your-nudge-behavioral-scientist]] reports 40% agreement with wanting to do the opposite when forced and seven-in-ten agreement with wanting a competitor when a large company dominates a market.
- Dynamic interpretation: [[consumers-are-becoming-wise-to-your-nudge-behavioral-scientist]] argues that repeated exposure educates consumers about a tactic's likely veracity in a particular setting.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is an author summary rather than the full study report. It does not disclose the exact nine stimuli, experimental conditions, response scales, subgroup results, uncertainty intervals, or observed purchasing behavior. Scarcity and social proof are discussed together, so their separate effects cannot be inferred. The author explicitly warns against generalizing from this narrow commercial setting to all behavioral interventions.

## What Changed
- Created the concept from evidence that familiar hotel-booking prompts can be read as coercion and produce distrust rather than persuasion.
- Distinguished demonstrated context-specific skepticism from untested claims about universal nudge fatigue or cross-domain spillover.
