## Definition
[[FounderSacrificeNorm]] is the belief that willingness to surrender personal money, security, time, health, or relationships—and sometimes to request comparable sacrifice from a team—is evidence that a founder deserves to lead a startup.

## Current Synthesis
[[JasonCalacanis]] supplies a strong version of the norm. He presents startup leadership as a rare, brutal role and asks founders to test themselves against three crisis scenarios: selling everything to fund another month of payroll, assuming $20,000 of credit-card debt, and asking employees to accept 90 days of deferred salary. On this account, extraordinary sacrifice demonstrates seriousness even though it cannot secure the desired outcome.

The source also contains two constraints that complicate a simple hustle doctrine. Calacanis says effective founders solve hard problems with the least work, and he recommends employment at a mid-sized company as a responsible apprenticeship for someone not yet ready to found. Intensity is therefore not identical to long hours, while choosing not to found can be a capability decision rather than a failure of ambition.

As evidence, however, the essay establishes only that one experienced practitioner endorsed this norm in 2015. It offers no comparison showing that the proposed sacrifices predict sound judgment, product demand, company survival, or ethical leadership. Personal wealth changes who can pass a financial-sacrifice test; deferred pay transfers founder risk to workers; and unlimited commitment can worsen burnout, family harm, and [[SunkCostFallacy]]. The norm is best retained as a contested cultural claim, not a founder-quality rule.

## Key Claims
- Sacrifice norms convert willingness to absorb severe personal loss into a signal of founder identity or readiness.
- Founder commitment and startup success are distinct: even the source says extraordinary sacrifice creates only a chance of an extraordinary outcome.
- Efficient problem solving can matter more than raw hours, so extreme effort is not itself proof of capability.
- Joining a larger organization can be a rational apprenticeship when a person's current skill or support level does not fit founding.
- Financial sacrifice tests are confounded by wealth and can shift risk onto employees whose consent and resilience differ from the founder's.
- Extreme commitment may reflect escalation, coercive culture, or absent boundaries as readily as courage or persistence.

## Evidence
- Personal-risk threshold: [[jason-calacanis-you-dont-have-what-it-takes]] asks whether a founder would sell assets or assume $20,000 in credit-card debt to extend payroll.
- Team-risk threshold: [[jason-calacanis-you-dont-have-what-it-takes]] asks whether a founder would seek 90 days of deferred employee salary while attempting a rescue.
- Outcome uncertainty: [[jason-calacanis-you-dont-have-what-it-takes]] explicitly says extraordinary sacrifice is made for a chance, not a guarantee, of an extraordinary outcome.
- Efficiency distinction: [[jason-calacanis-you-dont-have-what-it-takes]] contrasts working hard with solving hard problems using the least work.
- Apprenticeship alternative: [[jason-calacanis-you-dont-have-what-it-takes]] recommends that an underprepared founder refine skills as one person in a mid-sized company rather than remain one of three in a startup.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The only direct evidence is a rhetorical first-person essay built around an anonymized founder conversation, selected famous founders, and unsupported “99%” and “80%” figures. It provides no definitions, denominator, sampling method, follow-up outcome, unsuccessful high-sacrifice comparison, or successful low-sacrifice comparison. The litmus test measures stated willingness rather than actual conduct or decision quality.

The norm also collapses importantly different actions. Spending one's own assets, taking expensive personal debt, and withholding or deferring employee compensation have different legal, ethical, household, and distributional consequences. Founders with wealth or family support can appear more committed while bearing less marginal risk; workers may lack the founder's control, equity, information, or upside. Sacrifice can sometimes preserve a viable company, but the source does not show when persistence is rational, when shutdown is safer, or what informed consent and recovery boundaries require.

## What Changed
- Created a contested concept separating founder commitment from sacrifice as a selection signal.
- Added wealth, worker-risk transfer, consent, and survivorship as core qualifications.
- Preserved efficient problem solving and apprenticeship as internal limits on a pure hustle interpretation.
