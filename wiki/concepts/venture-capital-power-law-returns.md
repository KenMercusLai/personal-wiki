Venture capital power-law returns mean that a fund's result is dominated by a very small number of companies rather than by the average company. The 500 Startups source says most VC funds perform poorly relative to capital returned or public-market alternatives, while successful funds often depend on one or more exceptional winners.

The source gives a rough portfolio distribution from 500 Startups' experience. It estimates that 50-80% of investments do not exit or return less than 1X invested capital, 15-25% create small 2-5X exits, 5-10% become companies worth more than $100 million, and only 1-2% reach unicorn scale with the potential for 50X or greater returns.

This distribution changes how fund skill should be interpreted. A concentrated portfolio with a winner can look like proof of superior judgment, but the article warns that the same strategy can also resemble lottery-like exposure when the underlying hit rate is low. The existence of power laws therefore supports both selectivity and diversification: investors still need exceptional companies, but they also need enough attempts for rare outcomes to appear.

For founders and employees, the same pattern explains why venture-backed startup narratives often overrepresent successes. The companies that define fund returns are highly visible after the fact, while the many investments that return little or nothing are structurally easier to forget.

## Implications

- Mean portfolio outcomes can be misleading when returns are concentrated in a small tail.
- Hindsight can make rare winners look easier to identify than they were before investment.
- Fund construction should be tested against downside cases where no unicorn appears.
- A portfolio needs enough breadth for the expected tail outcomes to have room to occur.
