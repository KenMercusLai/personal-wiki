## Definition
[[SubscriptionSurplusAlignment]] is the pricing pattern in which recurring payments tie producer revenue to continuing customer value, reducing both one-time-price mismatch and per-use friction.

## Current Synthesis
The sources describe two complementary alignment mechanisms. Thompson uses economic-surplus reasoning and two diagrams to compare a one-time packaged-software price with recurring payments: subscriptions lower the starting commitment and let producer revenue accumulate as user value grows. Wenger shifts from price discrimination to ongoing behavior: the provider must keep delivering enough aggregate value to earn renewal, while a subscriber can try another item, class, or interaction without a marginal charge. Together, the model aligns revenue more closely with a continuing relationship, but it does not prove that every subscription is affordable, well used, easy to cancel, or proportionate to value.

## Key Claims
- Packaged software captures value poorly when users differ sharply in willingness to pay and intensity of use.
- Heavy professional users can receive career-scale value after paying only a one-time license.
- Beginners or casual users may be blocked by upfront price before they discover whether the product is useful.
- Subscriptions convert a static price into an ongoing relationship whose revenue depends on renewal.
- A fixed recurring payment can encourage exploration because additional use carries no marginal price.
- Better alignment changes producer incentives because reducing churn requires continuing maintenance and customer value.

## Evidence
- Packaged-software diagram: [[adobes-subscription-model-why-platform-owners-should-care-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] shows flat producer revenue against rising consumer value, with consumer surplus expanding later in time.
- Subscription diagram: [[adobes-subscription-model-why-platform-owners-should-care-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] shows a stepwise payment path where producer surplus accumulates as consumer value grows.
- User examples: [[adobes-subscription-model-why-platform-owners-should-care-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] uses casual, professional, and student Photoshop users to illustrate different surplus outcomes.
- Maintenance incentive: [[adobes-subscription-model-why-platform-owners-should-care-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] says Adobe is incentivized to maintain the app to reduce churn.
- Renewal alignment: [[continuations-by-albert-wenger-from-advertising-to-subscriptions-and-the]] argues that a consumer-subscription company must provide enough continuing value for customers to remain subscribed.
- Use without marginal price: [[continuations-by-albert-wenger-from-advertising-to-subscriptions-and-the]] uses Skillshare to show how a subscriber can sample another video or class without another financial charge and judge value across the bundle.
- Cross-category applicability: [[continuations-by-albert-wenger-from-advertising-to-subscriptions-and-the]] cites media, music, health, education, finance, and publishing services, including hybrids with free advertising-supported and paid ad-free tiers.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are conceptual business-model arguments rather than measured revenue, utility, retention, or welfare comparisons. The alignment depends on pricing, real continuing value, informed choice, and easy exit; subscriptions can overcharge light or forgetful users, exclude customers who cannot sustain recurring payments, fragment household budgets, or frustrate people who prefer ownership. Hybrid services also retain advertising incentives for their free tiers.

## What Changed
- Broadened the concept from one-time software price capture to continuing provider-customer incentive alignment.
- Added zero marginal price for additional use and aggregate bundle value as customer-side mechanisms.
- Added cross-category and hybrid-model scope while preserving affordability, underuse, and evidence limits.
