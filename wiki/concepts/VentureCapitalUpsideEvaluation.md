## Definition
[[VentureCapitalUpsideEvaluation]] is the practice of judging an early-stage investment by whether the scale, probability, and assumptions of a successful outcome justify its known failure risks within a power-law portfolio.

## Current Synthesis
Early-stage companies are fragile enough that an analyst can almost always identify plausible reasons to decline. [[HunterWalk]] argues that this risk inventory is necessary but incomplete because venture funds depend on a few exceptional companies returning enough to offset many losses. The central question is therefore whether a credible success case could become venture-scale, not whether the company could fail.

The same frame improves post-mortems. Analysis should distinguish risks that were reasonably assessable at the time from changes in technology, markets, or regulation that emerged later; reconstruct the assumptions required for the large outcome; evaluate their original credibility and execution difficulty; and ask whether the investing firm had relevant experience with that risk profile. This does not make investment decisions immune from criticism: vague probabilities and inflated assumptions can manufacture a paper bull case, so upside claims still require evidence and calibration.

## Key Claims
- Venture decisions should compare downside probability with the magnitude and credibility of success, rather than treat the existence of risk as dispositive.
- Power-law portfolios make missed large outcomes economically important because a small number of winners can offset many total losses.
- A viable and valuable company is not necessarily venture-scale; the successful outcome must be large enough for the financing model.
- Retrospective analysis should separate risks visible at investment time from novel external changes that emerged later.
- A credible post-mortem reconstructs the bull case, its assumptions, its execution difficulty, and the investor's demonstrated fit with that type of risk.
- Upside reasoning needs an evidence bar because flexible probabilities and optimistic assumptions can rationalize almost any decision after the fact.

## Evidence
- Asymmetric returns: [[for-vcs-what-could-go-right-is-more-important-than-what-could-go-wrong-hunter-walk]] says early-stage investors accept frequent zeros because successful companies may return 20, 50, or more than 100 times capital.
- Decision standard: [[for-vcs-what-could-go-right-is-more-important-than-what-could-go-wrong-hunter-walk]] argues that investment analysis should ask how large the company could be if it works and how the team defines success.
- Post-mortem test: [[for-vcs-what-could-go-right-is-more-important-than-what-could-go-wrong-hunter-walk]] proposes testing predictability, venture-scale assumptions, execution difficulty, and investor experience with comparable risk.
- Learning mechanism: [[for-vcs-what-could-go-right-is-more-important-than-what-could-go-wrong-hunter-walk]] recommends comparing outcomes with the original investment memo and sharing reasoning on background with trusted reporters after a company ends.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The framework comes from one investor's short 2017 essay and supplies no portfolio data, prospective scorecard, comparison of accepted and rejected deals, or evidence that background conversations improve reporting. Power-law economics does not establish that any particular bull case was credible, and reconstructed assumptions are vulnerable to hindsight, survivorship, self-protection, and blame shifting. Upside evaluation should complement rather than replace diligence on ownership, price, follow-on capital, downside exposure, founder incentives, and whether venture financing fits the company's goals.

## What Changed
- Established upside scale and credibility as a distinct complement to risk identification in venture decisions.
- Added a time-aware post-mortem framework covering predictability, assumptions, execution difficulty, and investor risk-profile fit.
- Preserved a falsifiability boundary: optimistic arithmetic alone cannot vindicate a failed investment.
