## Definition
[[EmployeeOpportunityDesign]] is the organizational practice of giving employees real responsibility, useful work, and enough trust for their ability to turn into meaningful output.

## Current Synthesis
The sources argue that employee performance and felt usefulness are situation-dependent. When a large company appears to have many people doing little, the problem may not be mass fraud or weak talent; it may be that the company has structured work so people lack clear responsibility, tangible outcomes, or permission to contribute. The corporate-disillusionment essay adds the worker's view of this condition: jargon, meetings, documents, and coordination rituals can continue even when participants privately doubt their value. In this synthesis, a "10x person" needs a "10x situation," but perceived pointlessness remains a diagnostic signal rather than proof that indirect work has no value.

## Key Claims
- Underutilization can look like low talent even when the underlying problem is poor opportunity allocation.
- Headcount growth is not evidence that a company is creating valuable work for employees.
- Treating employees like children can suppress autonomy, fulfillment, and contribution.
- Exceptional output depends on matching capable people with unusually enabling situations.
- Smaller teams may produce better work when remaining employees have clearer responsibility and more meaningful ownership.
- A worker's inability to identify a concrete outcome can signal poor opportunity design, although necessary coordination and risk work may remain difficult to observe.

## Evidence
- Underutilized engineers: [[a-thing-i-wonder-do-companies-have-too-many-employees]] quotes a claim that only 400 of 4,000 engineers seemed to be doing meaningful work, then interprets the other 3,600 as potentially waiting for real work rather than cheating the company.
- Headcount bias: [[a-thing-i-wonder-do-companies-have-too-many-employees]] describes venture investors treating rapid hiring and hundreds of employees as startup-success signals.
- Small-headcount counterexample: [[a-thing-i-wonder-do-companies-have-too-many-employees]] cites [[WhatsApp]] selling for $19 billion with 55 employees.
- Opportunity multiplier: [[a-thing-i-wonder-do-companies-have-too-many-employees]] argues that talented people produce 1x results without opportunity and 10x results with 10x opportunity.
- Lived underutilization: [[the-still-wandering-the-death-of-the-corporate-job]] describes anonymous workers whose days are full of meetings, documents, and facilitation but who cannot name a tangible contribution.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are reflective essays built from anecdotes rather than systematic organizational measurement. They do not prove that most large companies are overstaffed, identify which employees are underutilized, or show how smaller teams should cover support, compliance, operations, coordination, relationship, and risk-management needs. A role can create real value without a tangible daily artifact, and private disillusionment can reflect poor communication, personal fit, or missing line of sight rather than objectively useless work. The sources are strongest as hypotheses about opportunity allocation and felt contribution.

## What Changed
- Added the worker-experience signal of being busy without being able to name a tangible contribution.
- Distinguished perceived pointlessness from evidence that indirect organizational work has no value.
