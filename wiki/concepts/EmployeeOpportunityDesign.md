## Definition
[[EmployeeOpportunityDesign]] is the organizational practice of giving employees real responsibility, useful work, and enough trust for their ability to turn into meaningful output.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that employee performance is situation-dependent. When a large company appears to have many people doing little, the problem may not be mass fraud or weak talent; it may be that the company has structured work so most people are waiting for an opportunity to contribute. In this view, a "10x person" emerges from a "10x situation": talent needs scope, responsibility, and permission before it can produce outsized results.

## Key Claims
- Underutilization can look like low talent even when the underlying problem is poor opportunity allocation.
- Headcount growth is not evidence that a company is creating valuable work for employees.
- Treating employees like children can suppress autonomy, fulfillment, and contribution.
- Exceptional output depends on matching capable people with unusually enabling situations.
- Smaller teams may produce better work when remaining employees have clearer responsibility and more meaningful ownership.

## Evidence
- Underutilized engineers: [[a-thing-i-wonder-do-companies-have-too-many-employees]] quotes a claim that only 400 of 4,000 engineers seemed to be doing meaningful work, then interprets the other 3,600 as potentially waiting for real work rather than cheating the company.
- Headcount bias: [[a-thing-i-wonder-do-companies-have-too-many-employees]] describes venture investors treating rapid hiring and hundreds of employees as startup-success signals.
- Small-headcount counterexample: [[a-thing-i-wonder-do-companies-have-too-many-employees]] cites [[WhatsApp]] selling for $19 billion with 55 employees.
- Opportunity multiplier: [[a-thing-i-wonder-do-companies-have-too-many-employees]] argues that talented people produce 1x results without opportunity and 10x results with 10x opportunity.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a short reflective essay built from anecdotes rather than systematic organizational measurement. It does not prove that most large companies are overstaffed, identify which employees are underutilized, or show how smaller teams should cover support, compliance, operations, and coordination needs. Its strongest use is as a hypothesis and critique of headcount bias.
