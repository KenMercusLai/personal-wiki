## Definition
[[Neuroplasticity]] is the nervous system's capacity to remodel structure and function through changes including neurogenesis, glial growth, synaptic formation and pruning, dendritic and axonal remodeling, angiogenesis, neurotransmission, and network connectivity.

## Current Synthesis
The review treats neuroplasticity as a capacity for adaptive learning that is relevant across heterogeneous mental-health conditions and may be modified by exercise. Aerobic and resistance activity can influence neurotrophins, cerebral metabolism, inflammation, sleep, neurotransmitter systems, and large-scale brain networks. The most consistent exercise-related volumetric findings described are in prefrontal, subcortical, and mesial temporal regions, especially the dentate gyrus, while connectivity findings involve executive-control, default-mode, and salience networks.

This is not a claim that one biomarker or brain region explains mental-health improvement. Inflammation may inhibit neurogenesis, baseline connectivity can predict behavioral change, and age, cognition, reward processing, sleep, and adherence can modify response. The review therefore presents plasticity as a dynamic intermediate capacity that may enable behavioral learning in [[ExerciseForMentalHealth]], not as a direct synonym for symptom remission.

## Key Claims
- Exercise can influence multiple substrates of plasticity, including neurotrophins, vascular function, metabolism, inflammation, sleep, and monoaminergic signaling.
- Adult neurogenesis discussed in the review centers on the dentate gyrus and the subventricular zone.
- Exercise-related structural changes are most consistently reported in prefrontal, subcortical, and mesial temporal regions.
- Exercise may improve functional efficiency and connectivity within or between executive-control, default-mode, and salience networks.
- Plasticity may increase capacity for cognitive, affective, and self-regulatory learning without guaranteeing clinical improvement.
- Baseline biology and changes during treatment may help explain response heterogeneity and guide adaptive interventions.

## Evidence
- Cellular scope: [[nihms-1685283]] describes neurogenesis, gliogenesis, dendritic remodeling, synaptic change, axonal augmentation, angiogenesis, and neurotransmitter potentiation.
- Facilitators and inhibitors: [[nihms-1685283]] connects exercise with BDNF, VEGF, IGF-1, metabolism, inflammation, sleep, and skeletal-muscle handling of kynurenine.
- Structural observations: [[nihms-1685283]] reports the most consistent volumetric exercise changes in prefrontal, subcortical, and mesial temporal structures, particularly the dentate gyrus.
- Network observations: [[nihms-1685283]] summarizes acute default-mode efficiency and longer-term executive-control/default-mode connectivity changes with corresponding executive-function improvement.
- Personalization: [[nihms-1685283]] proposes connectivity or executive-function change as intermediate response markers in adaptive trials.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The review integrates evidence across populations, methods, durations, imaging measures, and diagnoses, so the pathway is not demonstrated as one continuous causal sequence. Peripheral growth-factor measures may not map cleanly onto central nervous-system change, volumetric or connectivity changes do not automatically imply symptom relief, and the cited older-adult findings may not generalize to every age or disorder. Human adult neurogenesis and its measurement require methodological care beyond the source's summary. Inflammation, aging, sleep, illness, medication, baseline network disruption, and failure to maintain exercise may alter or block the proposed effects.

## What Changed
- Created a multi-level account of exercise-sensitive plasticity from cellular remodeling to large-scale network connectivity.
- Distinguished an intermediate capacity for adaptive learning from direct proof of symptom remission.
- Added biological and behavioral moderators that limit a single-mechanism explanation.
