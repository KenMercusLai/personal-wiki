## Definition
[[ExtendedStockOptionExerciseWindow]] is a startup equity policy that lets departing employees exercise vested stock options for a much longer period, such as 10 years, rather than forcing exercise soon after leaving.

## Current Synthesis
The sources frame the exercise window as a central fairness mechanism in startup equity. A short post-employment window can make vested options unusable for employees who cannot afford the exercise cost or tax risk before liquidity, while a 10-year window lets them preserve the possibility of value from already vested compensation. The policy debate turns on whether retained former-employee equity unfairly dilutes current and future employees; Quora's answer is that compensation should be set by market value and adjusted through option pools, not by clawing back previously vested shares through short deadlines.

Holman's termination account shows the decision pressure in practice: after believing an extension had been discussed, he says he was told shortly before his deadline that he needed either to finance a large exercise or sign a separation agreement. That anecdote strengthens the procedural case for early disclosure, written terms, and time for independent advice, but it does not establish the legal or tax treatment of every option grant.

## Key Claims
- Long exercise windows protect the practical value of already vested startup equity.
- Short exercise windows can function as a hidden penalty on employees who leave before liquidity.
- Former employees retaining vested options does not necessarily reduce future employee grants when compensation is market-based.
- A company can still choose harsher terms, but those terms should be disclosed clearly before hiring.
- Involuntary turnover strengthens the fairness case for longer exercise windows.
- Written, early, and unambiguous exercise terms matter because termination stress and approaching deadlines weaken employee decision-making.

## Evidence
- Practical value: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] says 90-day windows can require departing employees to produce a large sum of money to keep vested stock.
- Hidden penalty: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] argues there is no need to add a hidden penalty beyond the explicit vesting schedule.
- Market-based grants: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] says Quora grants based on current market compensation for the role and can expand the option pool when hiring is rational.
- Disclosure: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] says companies should be free to choose compensation schemes as long as employees are fully informed before joining.
- Involuntary turnover: [[10-year-exercise-periods-make-sense-thoughts-quora]] notes that people may leave because of family care, performance standards, or role changes rather than pure choice.
- Departure pressure: [[firing-people]] describes Holman's claimed 28-hour deadline to fund a large exercise or accept a separation agreement after an expected extension did not materialize.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The Quora source summarizes but does not ingest the opposing investor argument in full. Neither source models tax consequences, option-pool negotiation dynamics, company-stage differences, or legal variations in option agreements. Holman's statement that vested incentive stock options disappear after 90 days “by law” is an imprecise compression of tax qualification and contractual expiration; his experience is evidence of decision pressure, not current legal or tax guidance. The strongest claims remain normative and strategic rather than empirical.
