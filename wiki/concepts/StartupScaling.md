## Definition
[[StartupScaling]] is the process of changing a startup's organization, processes, leadership, and operating systems as users, customers, revenue, and employee count move through larger stages.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat scaling as an order-of-magnitude problem whose bottlenecks change as the organization grows. The CS183C source organizes scale from OS1 "family" through OS5 "nation," while the [[PatGrady]] interview adds an investor-side warning around roughly 150 employees: ambient knowledge fades, new employees may no longer share the founding context, and coordination increasingly depends on explicit systems and culture. Advice does not transfer cleanly across these stages; a 3-person company, a 15-person company, and a 150-person company need different processes, priorities, and leadership behavior.

## Key Claims
- Startup advice is stage-sensitive and can fail when carried into a different scale band.
- Processes should be expected to break at every order of magnitude.
- The first major scaling move is often from one product-building team to two broad groups: builders and supporters.
- Coordination stress rises sharply around the point where people no longer know everyone else.
- Scaling requires both product/customer traction and organization-building capacity.
- Revenue scaling and new growth avenues become major hurdles after product-market fit.
- Spending more money cannot substitute for a superior value proposition in competitive markets.

## Evidence
- Stage table: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] includes an OS1-to-OS5 scale table spanning family, tribe, village, city, and nation stages.
- Process breakage: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] cites [[EricSchmidt]] via [[MarissaMayer]] on processes breaking at 1, 10, 100, and 1,000 scale.
- Team split: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] cites [[JohnLilly]]'s distinction between the product/growth team and the support functions that defend it.
- Coordination threshold: [[16-lessons-on-scaling-from-eric-schmidt-reid-hoffman-marissa-mayer-brian-chesky-diane-greene-jeff-weiner-and-more]] uses Dunbar's number around 150 people as a point where informal coordination weakens.
- Coordination threshold: [[8-lessons-from-one-of-silicon-valleys-top-vcs-drift]] quotes Grady's warning that around 150 employees leaders stop knowing everyone by name and new hires may hold different assumptions about why the company exists.
- Systems and culture: [[8-lessons-from-one-of-silicon-valleys-top-vcs-drift]] says the answer to this scaling breakage is regular communication systems and intentional culture.
- Scale limit: [[8-lessons-from-one-of-silicon-valleys-top-vcs-drift]] argues that crowded SaaS markets require a superior value proposition because companies cannot brute-force scale with spending alone.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The stage model and 150-person threshold are heuristics rather than universal laws. Employee, user, customer, and revenue scale can diverge by business model, and some organizations may need process maturity earlier because of regulation, hardware, safety, or enterprise commitments. The Grady source is also investor advice rather than direct operational measurement.

## What Changed
- Added Grady's 150-person scaling warning and his claim that capital cannot compensate for a weak value proposition.
