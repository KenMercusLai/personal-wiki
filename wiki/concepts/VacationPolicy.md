## Definition
[[VacationPolicy]] is the set of entitlements, approval rules, incentives, minimums, and shared closures through which an organization determines how workers take time away from work.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats vacation policy as behavioral infrastructure rather than a statement of trust. A nominally unlimited plan still has an unstated boundary, so employees must infer what is acceptable from managers, peers, workload, and in-group norms. That ambiguity can make leave feel like a test of dedication and can shift the effective policy from a defined process to guilt-based negotiation.

Clearer alternatives make desired rest legible: a fixed allowance establishes an entitlement, a mandatory minimum establishes a floor, quota relief removes a performance conflict, bonuses reward full use, and planned closures coordinate time away. The governing principle is to measure actual leave taken, especially low-use outliers, rather than treating attractive policy language as evidence of success.

Even adds a small-company implementation example: it pairs paid vacation with a required minimum and frames time away as a performance-supporting benefit used outside work. This supports the legibility principle but does not show how much leave employees took or whether the minimum improved recovery.

## Key Claims
- Vacation policy should be evaluated by observed behavior and wellbeing outcomes, not stated generosity alone.
- "Unlimited" leave remains bounded in practice but often leaves the boundary informal and socially negotiated.
- Ambiguous approval norms can disadvantage people with less status or weaker access to the workplace in-group.
- Fixed allowances, minimum leave, workload relief, use incentives, and closures make permission to rest more explicit in different ways.
- Average usage and low-use outliers are practical signals of whether employees can actually take leave.

## Evidence
- Ambiguous entitlement: [[unlimited-vacation-and-other-forms-of-guilt-based-management]] argues that six months or fifteen years would plainly trigger intervention, showing that unlimited leave retains an unwritten ceiling.
- Social pressure: [[unlimited-vacation-and-other-forms-of-guilt-based-management]] says employees must compare requests, dedication, and peer behavior when no fixed pool defines what is theirs to use.
- Reported reversals: [[unlimited-vacation-and-other-forms-of-guilt-based-management]] cites [[Kickstarter]] and [[TribunePublishing]] as companies that discontinued unlimited-vacation policies.
- Explicit alternatives: [[unlimited-vacation-and-other-forms-of-guilt-based-management]] cites [[HubSpot]]'s minimum and quota relief, [[RANDCorporation]]'s use bonus, and planned closures.
- Measurement: [[unlimited-vacation-and-other-forms-of-guilt-based-management]] recommends tracking average usage and investigating employees taking unusually little leave.
- Minimum-leave example: [[anti-perks-even]] reports that Even offered paid vacation with a minimum number of days off as part of its effort to support life outside work.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are practitioner and company-authored essays, and their company examples are reported rather than independently compared. The 2016 essay explicitly says the broader evidence on whether unlimited policies increase or decrease leave was unsettled, while Even gives no leave-usage or wellbeing results. Policy labels alone cannot establish outcomes: approval behavior, staffing, workload coverage, manager example, labor law, compensation, and employee bargaining power can change how any formal model works.

## What Changed
- Established vacation policy as behavioral infrastructure whose real effect may differ from its stated intent.
- Distinguished an explicit entitlement or minimum from an informal, socially negotiated ceiling.
- Added actual leave usage and low-use outliers as evaluation signals.
- Added Even's required minimum as a source-scoped implementation example without treating it as outcome evidence.
