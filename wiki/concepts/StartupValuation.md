## Definition
[[StartupValuation]] is a negotiated estimate of what a startup is worth, which sets the price investors pay for ownership and which early-stage companies support with expectations about the future rather than with current financial results.

## Current Synthesis
TechCrunch treats valuation as the number that makes a funding round legible, while insisting that it is a judgment rather than a measurement. For an early startup, and especially a pre-revenue one, the estimate is a bet on how large the company could become, informed by the estimated size of its market. Later rounds add evidence: revenue, growth trajectory, and the likelihood of a successful outcome through IPO or acquisition.

The practical warning is that a higher valuation is not the same as a better outcome. Raising at a high price raises the bar the company must clear. If a startup raises at a $500 million valuation and is later sold for $200 million, late investors lose money and employees who joined at the higher valuation see their equity worth less than expected. [[dont-get-trampled-the-puzzle-for-unicorn-employees]] sharpens this by separating headline price from economic terms: a company can preserve unicorn status through preferences that protect new investors while reducing common-share proceeds. Valuation therefore sets an expectation, while the capitalization and payout terms determine who receives value at a particular exit.

Wilson's USV review adds historical market context and makes valuation explicitly stage-, time-, geography-, and sample-dependent. In the firm's led or co-led deals, average seed pre-money valuations moved from roughly $5–10 million in the 2012 timeframe to $10–15 million in 2017, while Series A moved from roughly $10–15 million to $22–27 million. That supports the existence of valuation inflation in this portfolio, but not a universal benchmark or a return conclusion: the sample combines North America and Europe, the Bay Area was costlier than Europe, and the later investment cohorts had not matured.

The 645 Ventures essay adds the investor's opposite error: treating a seemingly high entry valuation as an independent veto can become a blind spot when the team, product, market, maximum plausible outcome, and probability of scale are not evaluated together. The combined judgment is not that price is irrelevant. A high valuation still raises the exit required for returns and may reduce ownership, but a disciplined decision must compare that cost with the distribution of possible outcomes rather than optimize entry price in isolation.

## Key Claims
- Valuation is the estimate of company worth that determines the price of the shares sold in a round.
- Early-stage and pre-revenue valuations are forward-looking bets, built from estimated market size rather than current financial performance.
- Later-stage valuations incorporate revenue, growth trajectory, and the probability of an IPO or acquisition.
- Valuation benchmarks vary by financing stage, period, geography, and sample, and higher entry valuations do not establish realized returns until cohorts mature.
- A high valuation raises the outcome required for returns, so entry price must be judged with achievable scale, success probability, ownership, and dilution.
- A headline valuation can rise while special terms shift downside toward earlier common shareholders, so valuation must be read with preferences, debt, and payout seniority.
- An exit below the last valuation can leave late investors or employees underwater even when the sale price is large in absolute terms, but contractual protections may distribute that downside unevenly.

## Evidence
- Definition and price-setting role: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says valuation is how much the company is worth and that setting it is complicated, especially for early-stage startups.
- Pre-revenue market-size bet: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says many startups raise before revenue and that an estimated market size for the product or service is taken into account.
- Later-stage inputs: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says revenue, growth trajectory, and the likelihood of a successful outcome are measured later on.
- Historical stage comparison: [[valuation-inflation-avc]] reports seed pre-money ranges rising from $5–10 million to $10–15 million and Series A ranges rising from $10–15 million to $22–27 million between the 2012 and 2017 timeframes in USV-led or co-led rounds.
- Geographic and sample boundary: [[valuation-inflation-avc]] combines USV investments in North America and Europe, identifies the Bay Area as most expensive and Europe as least expensive, and does not claim a Series B trend from its thin sample.
- High-valuation risk: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] describes a startup raising at a $500 million valuation and selling for $200 million, which means investors who bought at the higher valuation lost money.
- Employee impact: [[wtf-is-a-funding-round-techcrunch]] says a high valuation can also be bad news for employees with equity compensation who joined when the valuation was higher.
- Terms beneath the headline: [[dont-get-trampled-the-puzzle-for-unicorn-employees]] argues that late-stage companies can maintain a large announced valuation by giving investors protective terms whose cost appears in common-share payout rather than in the headline price.
- Persistence of preferences: [[dont-get-trampled-the-puzzle-for-unicorn-employees]] notes that quoted share value can change while liquidation preferences remain part of the exit waterfall.
- Return-timing limit: [[valuation-inflation-avc]] says contemporary VC returns were still largely driven by 2005–2010 investments, leaving the effects of higher-priced later cohorts unknown.
- Valuation trap: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] relays Peter Fenton's argument that team, product, and market quality drive the outcome independently of whether the entry price initially feels high.
- Upside framing: [[venture-capital-blind-spots-645-ventures-medium]] recommends asking how large the company could become if things go right before deciding whether valuation and ownership requirements should be relaxed.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
TechCrunch supplies one illustrative raise-and-sale example, while Belsky explains preference risk without a worked cap table or outcome sample. A high valuation with protective terms is not automatically irrational: it may extend runway, avoid a disruptive down round, or fund growth that ultimately benefits every class. Wilson’s ranges come from one firm’s 55 led or co-led investments, combine geographies, and are not market-wide medians. The 645 Ventures argument selects exceptional winners retrospectively and does not show when relaxing price discipline improves returns. None of the sources establishes a universal causal relationship between entry valuation, financing terms, and realized performance.
