## Definition
[[DirectSponsorship]] is a paid relationship in which an advertiser buys placement from a known publisher or creator, deliberately choosing the audience and media context rather than purchasing an anonymous impression pool.

## Current Synthesis
The Daring Fireball case shows direct sponsorship operating as both measurable response and contextual distribution. Saddington's first placement generated enough reported first-week proceeds to recover its cost, but its broader value came through App Store rank, review requests, press testimonials, and possible contribution to later Apple recognition. That halo also created a capacity problem: insufficient review codes limited follow-on coverage. The second campaign was weaker, so the practical synthesis is conditional—audience-product fit, message, product quality, timing, preparedness, and nonlinear platform feedback shape the return, while before-and-after revenue alone cannot isolate causality.

## Key Claims
- A known publication lets an advertiser choose audience, context, and reputational association together.
- Campaign economics should include direct response and explicitly qualified secondary effects rather than collapsing every later outcome into attributable sales.
- Strong audience-product fit can make an expensive concentrated placement rational when the break-even volume is plausible.
- Secondary attention can compound through platform rankings, reviews, testimonials, and editorial coverage.
- Operational readiness is part of sponsorship performance because follow-on demand can be wasted when review, support, or fulfillment capacity is insufficient.
- Results can vary materially across repeated placements even for the same publication and product.

## Evidence
- Audience fit and cost: [[does-sponsoring-daring-fireball-actually-work-john-saddington]] describes a US$9,000 placement for a Mac blogging app in an Apple-focused publication and an estimated 300-sale break-even point.
- Immediate response: [[does-sponsoring-daring-fireball-actually-work-john-saddington]] reports nearly US$16,000 in first-week net proceeds versus just over US$2,100 in the preceding week.
- Distribution halo: [[does-sponsoring-daring-fireball-actually-work-john-saddington]] reports elevated Mac App Store rankings, review demand, press testimonials, and later Apple recognition.
- Capacity constraint: [[does-sponsoring-daring-fireball-actually-work-john-saddington]] says more than 30 blogs were wait-listed because review codes were unavailable.
- Repeatability boundary: [[does-sponsoring-daring-fireball-actually-work-john-saddington]] says the second campaign produced smaller results.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This is one advertiser's uncontrolled retrospective. The article does not define net proceeds consistently, provide customer-level attribution, estimate organic baseline uncertainty, or separate sponsorship from ranking feedback, seasonal demand, press, Hacker News, and Apple's award. A concentrated sponsorship also puts substantial budget into one placement and may fail when audience relevance, product readiness, pricing, creative, or timing differs.

## What Changed
- Created the concept from the Daring Fireball and Desk campaign case.
- Distinguished immediate response from a broader but causally uncertain distribution halo.
- Added operational capacity and repeat-placement variance as effectiveness constraints.
