## Definition
[[WorkplacePerkDesign]] is the selection, delivery, and deliberate exclusion of employee benefits and workplace amenities according to their effects on autonomy, wellbeing, inclusion, and the work itself.

## Current Synthesis
The Even source distinguishes portable, employee-controlled support from office-bound consumption. Minimum paid leave, flexible schedules, health coverage, compensation, equity, and flexible stipends can reduce friction outside work while leaving people latitude over how they rest or spend. Daily catering, entertainment rooms, alcohol, mandatory social events, and routine pet presence may look generous while encouraging immobility, distraction, longer office presence, coerced participation, or exclusion.

The useful principle is not a universal blacklist. Perks are part of [[WorkplaceIncentiveDesign]]: their timing, location, conditions, and social meaning shape behavior. A team should state the outcome it wants, examine who benefits or bears costs, measure actual use and effects, and revisit the design as the workforce and work model change.

## Key Claims
- A benefit's practical effect matters more than its visibility or conventional status as a perk.
- Employee-controlled resources can preserve autonomy better than a fixed recreational experience chosen by the employer.
- Office amenities can create hidden incentives around presence, movement, distraction, alcohol, social participation, and caregiving.
- Deliberate omission is a valid design choice when an amenity conflicts with the work or imposes concentrated costs on some employees.
- Perk design should be derived from team values and needs, then tested against actual behavior rather than copied from startup culture.

## Evidence
- Portable support: [[anti-perks-even]] lists minimum paid vacation, flexible schedules, medical plans, equity, salaries, and flexible stipends as benefits aimed at life outside work.
- Movement and health: [[anti-perks-even]] argues that habitual catered lunch can remove a useful walk from an otherwise sedentary day.
- Attention and inclusion: [[anti-perks-even]] says entertainment rooms, alcohol, mandatory fun, and pets can distract, burden abstainers, pressure off-hours participation, or impose a preference on the whole team.
- Workspace fit: [[anti-perks-even]] reports adding private, shared, and flexible spaces after asking employees about work styles, while omitting a dedicated recreation area.
- Alternative delivery: [[anti-perks-even]] describes using a flexible wellness stipend for dog-sitting rather than making the office routinely pet-friendly.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sole source is a self-authored account from a 17-person company and gives no comparative productivity, wellbeing, retention, inclusion, or utilization data. Some shared meals, social events, pets, food, recreation, or alcohol-free gathering spaces may improve access, belonging, convenience, or collaboration when designed voluntarily and inclusively. Employee-controlled cash or stipends can also be inadequate, taxable, unevenly accessible, or unable to solve workload and staffing constraints. The framework should therefore guide local evaluation, not justify blanket removal of benefits.

## What Changed
- Created a values- and outcomes-led framework for distinguishing employee-enabling benefits from potentially counterproductive anti-perks.
