## Definition
[[ExerciseAdherence]] is the initiation and sustained performance of an exercise program at a frequency, duration, and intensity sufficient to preserve its intended benefit.

## Current Synthesis
In the review, exercise adherence is not merely a compliance metric after treatment design; it is part of the treatment mechanism. Mental-health benefits are sustained most reliably among people who continue exercising after active intervention, with the cited maintenance evidence clustering around 150 minutes per week. Continued activity supplies repeated biological stimulation and repeated practice in planning, monitoring, tolerating arousal, solving problems, and building [[SelfEfficacy]].

Adherence is also an outcome shaped by the condition being treated. Blunted reward, executive dysfunction, cognitive inflexibility, affect-regulation difficulty, symptom severity, and low support can all make engagement harder. Tailoring may therefore include gradual activity increases, motivational preparation, clearer reward cues, structured classes, exercising with another person, self-management training, distraction, or biometric feedback. The benchmark is a practical signal from the reviewed studies, not a universal threshold or individualized prescription.

## Key Claims
- Continued exercise after supervised treatment is central to preserving mental-health gains.
- Maintenance appears more consistently related to remission than assigned differences in initial intensity or duration.
- Adherence repeatedly exposes participants to both neurobiological stimuli and behavioral skill practice.
- Depression- and anxiety-related differences in reward, executive control, cognition, and affect tolerance can impede participation.
- External structure and individualized titration may compensate for specific adherence barriers.
- A population benchmark such as 150 minutes per week does not replace clinical judgment, safety screening, or adaptation to capacity.

## Evidence
- Maintenance-outcome link: [[nihms-1685283]] says benefits after active treatment are sustained among people who continue to exercise and identifies maintenance as the strongest remission correlate reviewed.
- Practical threshold: [[nihms-1685283]] reports benefit near the American Heart Association benchmark of 150 minutes per week in the cited maintenance evidence.
- Skill loop: [[nihms-1685283]] links participation with goal setting, activation, planning, feedback, self-monitoring, problem solving, arousal tolerance, and self-efficacy.
- Barrier matching: [[nihms-1685283]] proposes gradual titration and reward cues for blunted reward, external regulation for executive dysfunction, and distraction or biometric monitoring for affect-regulation difficulty.
- Adaptive design: [[nihms-1685283]] proposes re-randomizing support based on changes in connectivity or executive function rather than waiting only for final symptoms.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The review does not establish one adherence intervention, one optimal dose, or one threshold for every diagnosis and participant. Association between maintained activity and remission can reflect selection: people who improve, have fewer symptoms, or possess more support may find continued exercise easier. Self-report, supervision, access, injury, disability, comorbidity, medication, cost, time, environment, and social conditions can affect both measured adherence and outcome. More exercise is not automatically better, and a missed target should not be interpreted as personal failure.

## What Changed
- Created an account of sustained participation as both a condition and mechanism of treatment benefit.
- Added symptom-linked barriers and matched forms of external or motivational support.
- Qualified the 150-minute benchmark against selection effects, safety, capacity, and context.
