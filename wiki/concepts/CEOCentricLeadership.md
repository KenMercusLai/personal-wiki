## Definition
[[CEOCentricLeadership]] is the organizational and public narrative that treats one chief executive as the dominant source of a company's identity, decisions, success, and failure while making the wider management system less visible.

## Current Synthesis
Banerjee describes CEO centrality as a reinforcing loop among companies, investors, and media. Public coverage focuses on a recognizable individual, while companies amplify that focus because acknowledging a distributed management team may be mistaken for weak leadership. The result is a gap between representation and operation: critical decisions already depend on functional executives and teams, but annual reports, market statements, interviews, and public appearances continue to present the CEO as the company.

The source argues that this concentration creates two related risks. Internally, expectations that the CEO be all-seeing, always visible, and personally accountable for uncontrollable outcomes can increase workload and stress. Externally, investors may treat a departure or death as a larger company-level shock when organizational value appears inseparable from one person. Its remedy is not to erase CEO accountability but to make collective capability legible: show the executive team, distribute investor communication and public engagements, and recognize team contributions throughout the company.

## Key Claims
- CEO-centric narratives are co-produced by media attention and company communication rather than by executive behavior alone.
- A solitary-leader image can hide the distributed management work behind strategy and daily execution.
- Concentrated visibility can turn legitimate CEO accountability into unrealistic responsibility for every outcome, including external market conditions.
- If investors perceive organizational capability as residing in one person, CEO departure or death can become a key-person and valuation risk.
- Making functional leaders and teams more visible may distribute recognition, workload, expertise, and confidence in organizational continuity.

## Evidence
- Reinforcing narrative: [[its-time-to-end-the-cult-of-the-ceo]] says media focus and companies' fear of appearing weak jointly sustain the all-powerful-CEO image.
- Hidden management system: [[its-time-to-end-the-cult-of-the-ceo]] argues that executive teams already hold power and make critical daily decisions despite limited visibility in reports, statements, and coverage.
- Executive burden: [[its-time-to-end-the-cult-of-the-ceo]] attributes excessive workload, stress, insomnia, and blame for uncontrollable performance partly to concentrated expectations.
- Investor exposure: [[its-time-to-end-the-cult-of-the-ceo]] reports larger valuation movement around CEO death and losses around selected departure examples.
- Communication remedy: [[its-time-to-end-the-cult-of-the-ceo]] proposes team photographs, distributed interviews and public appearances, and functional-leader investor updates.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a short practitioner argument, not a demonstrated causal study. Its research figures cannot be assessed from the supplied document because study titles, methods, samples, time windows, and links are absent. Valuation movement after a death or resignation may reflect rational information about strategy, relationships, succession readiness, or operating capability as well as excessive CEO identification. The article also does not distinguish constructive CEO accountability and clear decision authority from celebrity-centered attribution, or test whether greater executive visibility reduces stress or market volatility. Distributed communication needs coherent responsibility and governance so that it does not create conflicting messages or merely turn several executives into public personalities.
