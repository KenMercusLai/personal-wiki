## Definition
[[HighIntensityCircuitTraining]] (HICT) is a circuit-training method that combines resistance and aerobic demands by sequencing large-muscle exercises at high effort with deliberately incomplete recovery.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents HICT as a practical response to limited time, travel, and lack of equipment, not as a universal replacement for conventional training. Its sample uses body weight, 12 stations, 30 seconds per exercise, and 10-second transitions; exercise order alternates total-body, lower-body, upper-body, and core demands so one region can partly recover while another works. The “seven-minute” circuit is one pass through this structure. For most participants, the authors recommend repeating it two or three times rather than treating seven minutes as a complete universal dose. The method may efficiently improve aerobic and muscular health, but safe execution depends on screening, technique, exercise modification, and matching the program to goals.

## Key Claims
- HICT combines aerobic and resistance stimuli by using large-muscle exercises, high effort, and short rest intervals.
- Sequencing should alternate muscle groups and higher- with lower-demand stations to sustain intensity while preserving form.
- Body weight and common environmental features can remove facility and equipment barriers.
- Thirty-second work bouts and transitions of no more than 15 seconds provide a practical template, not an immutable prescription.
- A single seven-minute circuit is usually a repeatable unit; the source recommends multiple rounds for participants unable to sustain supramaximal intensity.
- HICT favors general health and time efficiency over maximal strength, power, or sport-specific adaptation.

## Evidence
- Program structure: [[high-intensity-circuit-training-using-body-weight]] describes a 12-station circuit with 30 seconds of work, 10-second transitions, and a roughly seven-minute duration per round.
- Exercise balance: [[high-intensity-circuit-training-using-body-weight]] alternates total-body, lower-body, upper-body, and core movements and includes inspected demonstrations of all 12 exercises.
- Accessibility: [[high-intensity-circuit-training-using-body-weight]] uses body weight plus a wall and chair so the routine can be performed in homes, offices, or hotel rooms.
- Outcome rationale: [[high-intensity-circuit-training-using-body-weight]] summarizes earlier studies reporting gains in VO2 max, insulin sensitivity, body composition, and muscular fitness from limited-rest high-intensity protocols.
- Dose qualification: [[high-intensity-circuit-training-using-body-weight]] distinguishes very short supramaximal research protocols from its recommendation to repeat the circuit and approach 20 minutes for most people.
- Safety boundary: [[high-intensity-circuit-training-using-body-weight]] advises screening, medical clearance, correct form, scalable exercises, and special caution or substitutions for several higher-risk groups.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a practitioner review and program proposal, not a controlled trial of its exact 12-exercise routine. Its benefit claims aggregate protocols with different movements, durations, populations, and intensities, so they do not establish that every participant will obtain equal or superior results from one seven-minute circuit. The routine also requires substantial discomfort and may be inappropriate without modification for detrained, injured, elderly, obese, hypertensive, cardiac, or otherwise comorbid participants. Body-weight loading may be insufficient for maximal strength and power goals, while chair-based exercises introduce stability and environmental risks not resolved by the photographs.

## What Changed
- Created a qualified programming model that separates the seven-minute circuit unit from the likely total session dose.
- Added exercise-order, work-rest, accessibility, safety, and goal-specificity constraints.
