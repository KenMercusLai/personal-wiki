## Definition
[[ComputationalThinking]] is a problem-solving frame built around decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, and algorithm design.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat computational thinking as a valuable but incomplete foundation for AI-era education. It helps learners break complex systems into parts, identify reusable structures, and design procedures, but it can look too clean when moved into the real world. RORIRI therefore extends it with statistical model thinking for uncertainty and academic writing for problem definition and evidence.

The CS-education reflection adds an assessment boundary. When an AI system can produce a passing implementation from a prompt, the final program no longer reveals whether a student decomposed the problem, understood the architecture, or can judge correctness. Computational thinking must therefore be made observable through explanation, planning, live dialogue, verification, or other process evidence rather than inferred from artifact completion alone.

## Key Claims
- Computational thinking names a durable capability for understanding powerful tools rather than merely learning one current tool.
- Its core operation is abstraction from concrete cases into reusable structure.
- Narrow CT assumes clean inputs and defined tasks more readily than messy real-world inquiry allows.
- AI-era education should preserve CT's execution layer while adding epistemic and problem-definition layers.
- AI-generated artifacts make decomposition, architectural reasoning, and verification harder to infer, so assessment must expose the learner's process.

## Evidence
- Durable capability: [[jiao-yu-de-xia-yi-bu-qi-er-luo-li-li-de-shu-ju-zhong-xin]] argues that fast tool turnover makes learning one specific tool less important than understanding the boundaries of powerful tools.
- Core operations: [[jiao-yu-de-xia-yi-bu-qi-er-luo-li-li-de-shu-ju-zhong-xin]] defines CT through decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, and algorithm design.
- Real-world gap: [[jiao-yu-de-xia-yi-bu-qi-er-luo-li-li-de-shu-ju-zhong-xin]] says CT underhandles noisy data, lossy descriptions, task origins, boundaries, and evidence standards.
- Extended frame: [[jiao-yu-de-xia-yi-bu-qi-er-luo-li-li-de-shu-ju-zhong-xin]] combines abstraction/programming, statistical model thinking, and academic writing into a wider CT.
- Assessment visibility: [[ai-shi-dai-de-ben-ke-cs-jiao-yu-sui-xiang-jie-ge-de-jiao-xue-yun-wei-bian-cheng-diao-ban-zi-xiao-bi-ji]] argues that a working AI-generated assignment can conceal whether the student framed, decomposed, understood, or verified the solution.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are formative essays rather than curriculum trials. RORIRI explicitly says the three abilities are an adaptable heuristic rather than a natural or final list. The CS reflection reports cross-course teaching-assistant experiments but supplies no protocol or outcome data, and oral or process-based assessment may introduce workload and presentation bias.

## What Changed
- Created this concept to capture RORIRI's expanded CT frame for AI-era education.
- Added the distinction between producing an artifact and demonstrating the reasoning used to produce and verify it.
