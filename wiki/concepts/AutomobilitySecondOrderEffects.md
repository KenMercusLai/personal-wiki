## Definition
[[AutomobilitySecondOrderEffects]] are the downstream social, economic, spatial, labor, energy, and surveillance consequences that follow when electric and autonomous vehicles change the cost, behavior, and infrastructure of car-based transportation.

## Current Synthesis
Evans' core claim is that electric and autonomous vehicles should be analyzed like infrastructure transitions rather than car-feature upgrades. Electric vehicles remove much of the internal-combustion system, shifting maintenance, suppliers, gas stations, convenience retail, tobacco sales, fuel taxes, subsidies, grids, and household storage. Autonomous driving, if it works, changes not only accidents but vehicle design, congestion, parking, on-demand mobility, buses, logistics work, land values, commuting geography, urban nightlife, and public surveillance. The important pattern is second-order coupling: each efficiency gain can free capacity, but it can also induce new demand, move costs elsewhere, or blur old category boundaries.

## Key Claims
- Vehicle electrification changes adjacent systems because removing the combustion engine reduces mechanical maintenance and gasoline-related retail, tax, and subsidy structures.
- Charging a fully electric fleet shifts pressure onto electricity generation, distribution, load timing, and possibly household storage.
- Autonomous driving can reduce accidents and change vehicle design, but its road-capacity gains are limited by induced demand and changed trip behavior.
- Parking is a major urban lever because autonomous and on-demand vehicles can reduce, relocate, or transform parking demand while also creating empty repositioning trips.
- Cheap autonomous on-demand service can blur taxis, ride-hailing, shuttles, buses, and car ownership into a more fluid transport market.
- Labor effects are uneven because truck-driver demographics, local delivery work, ride-hailing, bus operations, truck stops, and ecommerce logistics change on different timelines.
- Autonomous vehicle sensor networks create surveillance and policing consequences beyond the transport function.

## Evidence
- Maintenance and suppliers: [[cars-and-second-order-consequences-benedict-evans]] says EVs reduce moving parts by roughly an order of magnitude and can eliminate much engine-related maintenance spending.
- Fuel retail and taxes: [[cars-and-second-order-consequences-benedict-evans]] links EV adoption to gas-station decline, convenience-store impulse purchases, tobacco distribution, gasoline-tax replacement, and fuel-subsidy politics.
- Grid and storage: [[cars-and-second-order-consequences-benedict-evans]] says a fully electric fleet could raise electricity demand by roughly 10-20%, with effects depending heavily on off-peak charging, local distribution, and home batteries.
- Safety and design: [[cars-and-second-order-consequences-benedict-evans]] links autonomy to reduced deaths, injuries, crash costs, insurance, emergency services, cycling safety, airbags, crumple zones, and vehicle weight.
- Capacity and demand: [[cars-and-second-order-consequences-benedict-evans]] argues that computer driving could reduce traffic waves, enable platooning, and increase road capacity, while induced demand can pull congestion back upward.
- Parking and land use: [[cars-and-second-order-consequences-benedict-evans]] treats parking as road capacity, construction cost, housing cost, and urban land reserve, citing Los Angeles parking land share and Oakland apartment cost effects.
- Transit and on-demand: [[cars-and-second-order-consequences-benedict-evans]] argues that cheap autonomous rides could substitute for some public transport while also making buses cheaper, faster, or differently routed.
- Labor and logistics: [[cars-and-second-order-consequences-benedict-evans]] distinguishes taxi and on-demand drivers from long-haul truckers, whose aging workforce, turnover, and retirement timeline complicate direct displacement claims.
- Surveillance: [[cars-and-second-order-consequences-benedict-evans]] warns that autonomous cars' 360-degree computer-vision sensors could become moving public-data collection systems for policing or facial recognition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Evans frames the essay as a map of where consequences might occur, not a prediction of what will occur. Several claims depend on full Level 5 autonomy, fleet turnover over decades, charging speed, policy choices, load factors, density, induced demand, consumer behavior, and local urban form. Some effects can point in opposite directions at once: road capacity can rise while demand rises, parking search traffic can disappear while empty pickup trips appear, and on-demand vehicles can weaken fixed transit in some cases while extending shared mobility in others.
