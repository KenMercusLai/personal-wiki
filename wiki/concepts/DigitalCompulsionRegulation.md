## Definition
[[DigitalCompulsionRegulation]] is the use of enforceable product rules to reduce engineered compulsive use and give people practical control over digital triggers, session boundaries, feedback, and cutoffs.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats compulsion as an interaction among a person's motives, product mechanics, and a business model that profits from repeated attention. On that account, regulation need not prohibit useful services or dictate how long everyone may use them. It can expand effective choice by requiring controls that let users decide delivery cadence, see their own usage, restore stopping points, and set binding limits. More interventionist rules could constrain endless feeds or require services with detailed behavioral data to identify and warn users showing unusually problematic patterns.

This case is conceptual rather than demonstrated policy evaluation. “Internet addiction” is diagnostically contested, compulsive behavior is hard to define across products and people, and firms can redesign around feature-specific bans. Usage detection also creates privacy, classification, paternalism, accessibility, and enforcement risks. The defensible core is therefore not that every engaging feature should be banned, but that the burden of control should not fall solely on users when designers possess superior behavioral data and profit from weakened stopping cues.

## Key Claims
- Regulation can expand user choice when it makes intention-setting and stopping controls real rather than merely available in theory.
- Distraction dashboards could let users control notification timing, feed refresh frequency, content quantity, and other repeated triggers.
- Removing infinite scroll, autoplay, or similar endless-consumption mechanics can restore explicit decision points between sessions or content units.
- Services with detailed usage telemetry could be required to provide warnings, cutoffs, or self-monitoring when patterns resemble problematic use.
- Individual blocking tools and voluntary certification are incomplete when access, effort, and business incentives are uneven.
- Feature-specific rules face evasion and require evidence-based thresholds, privacy safeguards, accessibility exceptions, and outcome evaluation.

## Evidence
Agency-expanding controls:
- [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] proposes dashboards for delivery cadence, feed updates, and page quantity, plus usage displays and user-selected lockouts.

Stopping cues:
- [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] argues that infinite scroll, swipe streams, and autoplay continually reset triggers and remove the pause at which a person could reconsider.

Risk detection:
- [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] notes that platforms already observe checking and play frequency and could warn or interrupt users whose patterns appear psychologically problematic.

Structural rationale:
- [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] connects weak self-regulation to an [[EngagementIncentiveConflict]] in which longer attention produces more advertising revenue.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The single source is a 2015 popular policy essay, not a clinical review, legal analysis, product experiment, or evaluation of enacted rules. It does not establish a diagnostic threshold, intervention effect, regulator, jurisdiction, compliance mechanism, cost, or remedy for errors. Infinite scroll, autoplay, frequent notifications, and long sessions can serve accessibility, communication, learning, safety, or deliberate entertainment, so duration alone is not harm. Behavioral monitoring may itself deepen surveillance, and compulsory warnings can misclassify users or become ignorable. Firms may replace prohibited mechanics with new ones, while voluntary tools can reproduce socioeconomic and technical-access gaps.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to distinguish agency-expanding product rules from blanket prohibition or user-only self-control advice.
- Identified dashboards, restored stopping points, risk warnings, usage feedback, and user-set cutoffs as separate intervention classes.
- Added evasion, privacy, classification, accessibility, and outcome-evidence requirements as unresolved design constraints.
