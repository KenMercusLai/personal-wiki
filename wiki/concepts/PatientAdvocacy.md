## Definition
[[PatientAdvocacy]] is action taken by a patient or supporter to obtain information, navigate institutions, challenge barriers, coordinate help, and press for timely, appropriate care.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents advocacy as a practical response to interacting failures rather than a single act of complaint. [[AnneWojcicki]] publicly escalated a payment dispute, drew in legal and insurance help, pushed for records, asked the family to photograph available clinical information, and searched her network for relevant clinicians. That combination reportedly helped an uninsured, critically ill patient obtain temporary coverage and transfer for advanced care.

The same success exposes the model's equity problem. A prominent advocate had social reach, biotechnology familiarity, institutional confidence, and access to specialists that most families do not. Public pressure can unlock attention in an urgent case, but a healthcare system that depends on exceptional persistence or fame makes navigation capacity part of access to care.

## Key Claims
- Effective advocacy may combine escalation, information gathering, coverage navigation, specialist search, and coordination across institutions.
- In urgent cases, improvised access to clinical information can help navigation but is not a substitute for reliable record transfer.
- Legal aid and insurance counselors can convert general eligibility rules into usable coverage.
- Social-media pressure can accelerate attention while creating privacy, accuracy, and unequal-influence concerns.
- Advocacy capacity is unevenly distributed, so individual success can reveal rather than resolve a systemic access failure.

## Evidence
Multi-channel intervention:
- [[lisa-krieger-23andme-founder-anne-wojcicki-berates-stanford-and-valley-med]] links Wojcicki's tweets, network search, phone-photo workaround, Bay Area Legal Aid, and Covered California counselors to the reported resolution.

Access outcome:
- [[lisa-krieger-23andme-founder-anne-wojcicki-berates-stanford-and-valley-med]] reports that Hospital Presumptive Eligibility supplied temporary insurance and that Stanford then accepted the patient.

Equity limit:
- [[lisa-krieger-23andme-founder-anne-wojcicki-berates-stanford-and-valley-med]] quotes advocates acknowledging that not everyone has a famous, well-connected supporter and that social-media shaming should not be necessary.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This is one journalist-reported case with an unnamed patient, undisclosed illness, no timeline adequate for causal attribution, and no case-specific hospital responses. It does not show which intervention was necessary, whether ordinary advocacy would have worked, or whether the eventual treatment improved the patient's outcome. Public escalation can also disclose sensitive information, oversimplify clinical or financial decisions, and allocate attention according to prominence rather than need.

## What Changed
- Created the concept from a case combining public escalation, coverage assistance, record gathering, and specialist navigation.
- Made unequal access to advocacy power part of the current judgment rather than treating publicity as a repeatable solution.
