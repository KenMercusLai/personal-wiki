## Definition
[[MedicalRecordPersistence]] is the durability and downstream reach created when clinical notes, summaries, screenshots, or copied material become part of a patient's medical chart.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that patient-targeted online search becomes especially consequential at the documentation stage. A Facebook post, YouTube video, or social-media screenshot may disappear from the original platform, but charted references can persist indefinitely and travel into insurance review, legal proceedings, custody disputes, criminal cases, or criminal data markets. The privacy problem is therefore not only that clinicians can see online information; it is that clinical systems can freeze, recontextualize, and redistribute material that patients did not expect to become formal medical evidence.

## Key Claims
- Charting online findings turns informal or ephemeral posts into formal medical-record content.
- Medical records are durable and may outlive the original online material.
- Third-party access expands the risk of clinical documentation beyond the treating relationship.
- Insurance, legal, custody, criminal, and data-theft contexts can make charted online content materially consequential.
- Documentation choices should be part of the ethics of [[PatientTargetedOnlineSearch]], not an afterthought.

## Evidence
- New record type: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] quotes [[PaulAppelbaum]] saying that putting online findings into charts creates medical-record information that did not exist before.
- Durability: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] says online sources may be hard to find, ephemeral, or disappear, while medical records persist.
- Insurance access: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] notes that insurance companies regularly review medical records.
- Legal reach: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] says charts can appear in accidental injury, child custody, and criminal cases.
- Theft risk: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] cites [[JohnLuo]] warning that hackers seek medical-record information because it can be sold.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source emphasizes risk but does not define best-practice documentation rules. Some charting may be necessary for safety, continuity of care, and accountability, especially in high-risk emergency decisions. The concept therefore concerns persistence and downstream exposure, not a blanket claim that clinicians should never document online findings.
