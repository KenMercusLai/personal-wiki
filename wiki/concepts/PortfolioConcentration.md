## Definition
[[PortfolioConcentration]] is the choice to hold fewer investments so each position can receive more attention, judgment, influence, or follow-through.

## Current Synthesis
Wilson frames concentration as the natural partner of active investing. If an investor intends to help shape outcomes, the scarce resource is not only capital but time, energy, and intellect. McClure adds the venture-capital counterpressure: in early-stage startup investing, rare outlier winners dominate returns, so concentration can become a statistical hazard if a small portfolio misses every unicorn. The synthesis is therefore conditional rather than universal: concentration fits hands-on active involvement, but high-attrition power-law domains may need enough independent bets to make outlier capture plausible.

## Key Claims
- Concentration is a practical consequence of active investing's attention demands.
- Diversification is more compatible with passive investing than with hands-on investing.
- Attempting to scale an active posture across too many investments can create a mismatch between promise and actual support.
- In VC, concentration can raise the risk of missing all rare outlier winners even if a concentrated portfolio would look brilliant when it does contain one.
- The active/concentrated and passive/diversified choice is a strategic boundary, but early-stage VC may need an operating model that separates governance rights from direct board-seat capacity.

## Evidence
- Attention demand: [[active-vs-passive-investing-avc]] says active investors must invest time, energy, and intellect as well as capital.
- Capacity limit: [[active-vs-passive-investing-avc]] says active investing is hard to scale and limits how many investments one can make.
- Diversification pairing: [[active-vs-passive-investing-avc]] says investors should choose active and concentrated or passive and diversified.
- Outlier miss risk: [[99-vc-problems-but-a-batch-aint-one-why-portfolio-size-matters-for-returns]] argues that if unicorns occur only 1-2% of the time, portfolios below 50-100 companies risk finding none.
- Board-seat qualification: [[99-vc-problems-but-a-batch-aint-one-why-portfolio-size-matters-for-returns]] suggests traditional VC board-seat expectations may keep portfolios undersized even when voting rights and control could be secured differently.
- Model neutrality: [[active-vs-passive-investing-avc]] says both active and passive models work, while [[99-vc-problems-but-a-batch-aint-one-why-portfolio-size-matters-for-returns]] says concentrated VC can work if it contains one or more big winners.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Wilson does not define an optimal number of investments or distinguish individual investors, firms, and funds with larger teams. McClure's counterargument is also a simplified VC model: it does not include fees, reserves, deployment timing, recycling, or the operational cost of supporting hundreds of startups. The disagreement is best read as a tradeoff between attention depth and outlier exposure, not as proof that either concentration or diversification always wins.

## What Changed
- Qualified the active-investing concentration rule with McClure's early-stage VC argument that rare unicorn outcomes require larger portfolios.
