## Definition
[[CableBundleEconomics]] describes how television distributors combine many channels into broadly purchased packages, pay networks per carried household, and thereby spread specialized programming costs across viewers and non-viewers.

## Current Synthesis
The 2016 ESPN case presents bundling as both a financing mechanism and a source of transition risk. Broad carriage reportedly let [[ESPN]] collect about $7 per month from every subscribing household, including households that did not watch sports. This cross-subsidy supported expensive exclusive rights and made the network more valuable inside the bundle, but it also meant that cord-cutting removed high-margin revenue household by household while many content commitments remained.

The source argues that an unbundled service would face adverse selection: devoted sports viewers would need to pay a much higher standalone price because non-viewers would stop contributing. Promoting that alternative could also weaken the legacy product, invite distributors to remove ESPN from basic tiers, and create a period in which the shrinking incumbent business still funded most costs while the new service had fewer subscribers. The result is a business-model transition problem, not simply a change in delivery technology.

## Key Claims
- Bundles can finance expensive niche or specialized content by charging every household that receives a channel, not only active viewers.
- A high per-household affiliate fee makes a network valuable but also magnifies the revenue effect of subscriber losses.
- Fixed or multi-year content commitments can turn gradual bundle erosion into a widening revenue-cost mismatch.
- Unbundling removes non-user cross-subsidy and can force a much higher price on the smaller group of direct users.
- A direct-to-consumer offer can cannibalize the incumbent bundle before the replacement reaches comparable scale.

## Evidence
- Cross-subsidy: [[espn-loses-621000-subscribers-worst-month-in-company-history-outkick-the-coverage]] says cable and satellite households paid ESPN roughly $7 per month whether or not they watched it.
- Attrition sensitivity: [[espn-loses-621000-subscribers-worst-month-in-company-history-outkick-the-coverage]] compares ESPN's annualized revenue exposure from 621,000 lost homes with the much smaller effect at the lower-fee FS1.
- Cost rigidity: [[espn-loses-621000-subscribers-worst-month-in-company-history-outkick-the-coverage]] argues that ESPN's multi-year rights commitments were harder to reduce than ordinary channel programming costs.
- Standalone pricing: [[espn-loses-621000-subscribers-worst-month-in-company-history-outkick-the-coverage]] estimates that a direct ESPN service might need to cost $20-$40 per month to replace bundle economics.
- Cannibalization: [[espn-loses-621000-subscribers-worst-month-in-company-history-outkick-the-coverage]] says promoting direct access could encourage distributors to challenge ESPN's basic-tier fee and placement.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This synthesis rests on one polemical 2016 article and should not be read as a current description of cable penetration, ESPN pricing, carriage contracts, or direct-to-consumer strategy. The source provides no household demand curve, churn model, distributor contract text, price-elasticity evidence, or comparison with bundles that lower search and transaction costs for consumers. Its $20-$40 standalone-price range is speculative, and its assumption that non-viewers receive no value from broad access ignores option value and household-level shared viewing. Later market outcomes are outside the bounded evidence here.

## What Changed
- Established cross-subsidy between non-viewers and viewers as the bundle's core sports-financing mechanism.
- Added high affiliate fees as both a strength and a source of attrition sensitivity.
- Added incumbent cannibalization and distributor response as constraints on unbundling.
