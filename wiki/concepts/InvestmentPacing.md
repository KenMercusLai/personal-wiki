## Definition
[[InvestmentPacing]] is the deliberate rate at which an investor makes new commitments so the resulting concurrent portfolio remains compatible with available attention, governance capacity, support resources, and the intended investing model.

## Current Synthesis
Wilson's early-stage model connects flow and stock. One or two new investments per partner per year may sound inactive, but a seven-year average holding period implies about seven to fourteen concurrent companies before allowing for uneven exits, follow-ons, or workload differences. Because he treats early-stage venture capital as a service business, the binding resource is not only deployable capital: it is partner bandwidth, supported by a wider team, over a seven-to-ten-year relationship.

Pacing therefore becomes both a capacity rule and a selection rule. Wilson says USV rejects most opportunities, uses an evolving thesis and collaborative rigor to choose a small number, and reserves enough attention for post-investment help. This is a coherent description of a concentrated active model, not evidence that one-to-two deals is universally optimal or that the model produces superior returns.

## Key Claims
- Annual commitment rate and average relationship duration jointly determine a partner's approximate concurrent portfolio load.
- Capital availability alone does not determine capacity when the investing model promises sustained partner involvement.
- Team support can extend partner capacity but does not make senior judgment and relationship bandwidth unlimited.
- A deliberately low pace requires selective rejection and high conviction because each commitment occupies capacity for years.
- Thesis formation, collaborative decision-making, and post-investment service operate as one system: focus guides entry while pacing preserves the ability to serve selected companies.
- A pacing target is model-specific and should not be treated as a universal deal-count benchmark or proof of return quality.

## Evidence
- Flow-to-load estimate: [[investment-pace-avc]] says one to two annual deals across a seven-year average hold imply roughly seven to fourteen concurrent portfolio companies.
- Capacity judgment: [[investment-pace-avc]] describes the low end of that range as manageable and the high end as unmanageable in Wilson's experience.
- Service premise: [[investment-pace-avc]] frames early-stage venture capital as long-duration service requiring partner bandwidth and a supporting team.
- Selection mechanism: [[investment-pace-avc]] links a low annual pace to rejecting most opportunities, an evolving thesis, rigorous collaborative decisions, and substantial post-investment value-add.
- Firm example: [[investment-pace-avc]] reports USV making eight to ten new investments per year, or about one to two per partner.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is one investor's firm-specific retrospective rather than a workload study or cross-fund performance comparison. Its simple multiplication assumes a stable annual pace and seven-year average hold while omitting workload variance by stage and company, follow-on rounds, board-seat allocation, early exits, write-offs, partner turnover, reserves, platform-team leverage, and shared coverage. The claim that this model has produced the best returns over time is unsupported in the article, and the reported billion-dollar-exit cadence lacks deal-level and fund-level evidence. A lower deal count can preserve attention, but it can also increase selection risk and concentration; a higher-volume model may make a different service promise.

## What Changed
- Created the concept linking annual deal flow, holding period, concurrent portfolio load, and service capacity.
- Distinguished a coherent firm operating model from an evidence-backed universal optimum.
- Preserved workload, governance, team-leverage, concentration, and performance-verification limits.
