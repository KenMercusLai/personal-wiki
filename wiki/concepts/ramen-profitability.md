Ramen profitability is a survival threshold, not proof that a startup has become an attractive job. In Steve Ridout's account, Readlang had low expenses and enough profit in its third year to keep the one-person business alive, but that profit was still below what a much smaller minimum-wage workload would have paid.

The concept is useful because it separates three different judgments that are easy to blur together: whether a product has users, whether revenue exceeds direct costs, and whether the founder is being compensated for time and career risk. Readlang passed the first two tests but still left Ridout carrying high opportunity cost compared with contract work or a software job.

Ramen profitability can justify continuing when it comes with evidence that the curve is improving. In this source, the case for continuing Readlang came from user growth, revenue growth, user praise, and the belief that more work on the same product could keep compounding those gains.

## Signals

- Revenue covers direct costs and keeps the product operating.
- Founder compensation remains below market salary or even below ordinary wage alternatives.
- The product has real usage or user value, not only theoretical upside.
- Continued focus depends on a credible growth path, not on survival revenue alone.
