## Definition
[[WisdomOfCrowds]] is the idea that aggregating many independent judgments can outperform individual judgment when diverse information is pooled and individual errors cancel out.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat crowd wisdom as real but conditional. Crowd averages can fail when respondents share the same information, overcount common knowledge, hide sensitive beliefs, or confuse confidence with competence. [[Metaknowledge]] improves the signal by asking people not only what they believe, but what they think others will believe. The [[HackerNews]] case adds that a public specialized crowd can violate both diversity and independence: members have overlapping technical preferences, see ranked prior comments, and may adapt their response to community reputation incentives. Such a crowd can still diagnose product details and business risks, but its approval is not equivalent to observed market demand.

## Key Claims
- Crowd wisdom depends on independent and diverse information, not merely on many respondents.
- Common information can be overcounted when many people repeat the same evidence.
- Confidence is a weak substitute for expertise because accuracy and confidence can diverge.
- Metaknowledge can help identify respondents with better-quality information.
- Strong consensus remains a useful proxy for truth when the majority is large.
- Public expert forums may be useful critics while remaining weak market forecasters.

## Evidence
- Independence condition: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] uses a failed Skittles-estimation example to show that nominally independent guesses can still miss badly.
- Common-information failure: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] argues that repeated common knowledge can drown out diverse information.
- Confidence problem: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] says accurate people often express confidence, but confident people are not necessarily accurate.
- Expertise signal: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] describes state-capital quiz results where better-informed students also predicted others' errors more accurately.
- Consensus qualification: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] says large majorities were always right in the quiz examples, while corrections mattered below about 70 percent agreement.
- Specialized-crowd dependence: [[why-you-need-to-stop-obsessing-over-comments-on-hacker-news-venngage]] argues that visible ranked comments and karma incentives can let early opinions influence later ones.
- Domain preference: [[why-you-need-to-stop-obsessing-over-comments-on-hacker-news-venngage]] reports warmer reactions to developer-oriented products than to broader consumer services.
- Mixed diagnostic record: [[why-you-need-to-stop-obsessing-over-comments-on-hacker-news-venngage]] contrasts Hacker News skepticism about later-successful Airbnb and Meteor with concerns about Homejoy's pricing and labor model that resembled its later problems.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources argue against naive aggregation, not against crowd judgment. Metaknowledge matters most when consensus is partial; when a large majority agrees, consensus itself may remain the best available proxy. The Hacker News article is a weak test of predictive accuracy because it selects mostly successful companies, has no matched general-crowd control, analyzes limited comment samples, assumes the top comment represents the crowd, and contains chart-versus-prose category conflicts. It is stronger as a mechanism-rich case about correlated judgment than as proof that expert and general crowds predict equally well.

## What Changed
- Added public visibility, ranking, reputation incentives, and domain homogeneity as mechanisms that can erode crowd independence.
- Distinguished technical criticism from market forecasting and preserved the case study's sampling and visualization defects.
