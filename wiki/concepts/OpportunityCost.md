## Definition
[[OpportunityCost]] is the value of the alternatives, habits, time uses, and identities given up when a choice allocates scarce time, attention, or resources to one direction.

## Current Synthesis
The sources frame opportunity cost as both life-direction tradeoff and commitment accounting. The Mission.org essay says ambition requires paying with attention, time, comfort, and attractive alternatives: goals identify what matters, while [[SelfDiscipline]] repeatedly chooses it over distraction and diffuse identity. Clear adds an asymmetry between refusal and acceptance. A no removes one option, but a yes assigns future time and thereby excludes every use that could occupy the same block; in his shorthand, no retains time credit while yes creates time debt. Together, the sources favor filtering before optimization, while recognizing that the capacity to refuse is unequal and that exploration sometimes warrants a lower threshold for acceptance.

## Key Claims
- Serious goals require choosing a few directions and letting other plausible identities or projects go.
- Time carries especially heavy opportunity cost because spent seconds cannot be recovered.
- Distraction and entertainment defaults are costly even when they feel minor, because they repeatedly displace learning, creating, and valued relationships.
- The cost of ambition includes saying no to good options, not only rejecting obviously bad habits.
- Every accepted commitment spends future optionality by excluding all competing uses of the same time.
- Acceptance thresholds should rise as experience and the value of time increase, but remain lower when exploration is the purpose.

## Evidence
- Choice cost: [[anything-is-possible-if-you-pay-the-price-mission-org-medium]] argues that trying to be everywhere or move in many directions prevents meaningful progress.
- Priority cost: [[anything-is-possible-if-you-pay-the-price-mission-org-medium]] says people must forego trivial and merely good options for the essential few.
- Time cost: [[anything-is-possible-if-you-pay-the-price-mission-org-medium]] treats time as the scarce, irreversible resource that makes distraction expensive.
- Daily default cost: [[anything-is-possible-if-you-pay-the-price-mission-org-medium]] uses spare minutes, alarm clocks, creative blocks, and phone use with loved ones as examples of small choices that reveal what someone is really paying for.
- Commitment asymmetry: [[blog-james-clear-the-ultimate-productivity-hack-is-saying-no]] describes no as rejecting one option and yes as rejecting every alternative use of the committed time.
- Threshold adjustment: [[blog-james-clear-the-ultimate-productivity-hack-is-saying-no]] recommends first rejecting distractions, then rejecting formerly good opportunities as better uses of time become available, while preserving early exploration.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources use motivational rhetoric and do not measure tradeoffs empirically. They can understate constraints such as money, caregiving, illness, discrimination, unsafe environments, or employment hierarchy, where options and refusals are not equally available. Clear explicitly acknowledges that power, money, and authority make refusal easier. The “do it today” and “hell yeah or no” tests are therefore heuristics, not universal decision rules; low-excitement maintenance, care, coordination, and exploratory work can still be necessary or valuable. Chosen aims require real tradeoffs, but tradeoffs alone do not guarantee an outcome.
