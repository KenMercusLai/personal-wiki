## Definition
[[OpportunityCost]] is the value of the alternatives, habits, time uses, and identities given up when someone chooses one direction strongly enough to pursue it.

## Current Synthesis
The Mission.org essay frames opportunity cost in personal-development terms: ambition is not mainly a declaration of desire but a willingness to pay with attention, time, comfort, and attractive alternatives. The "price" of a chosen life is giving up low-value default behavior, including distraction, entertainment, bad habits, unfocused priorities, and attempts to move in too many directions at once. This makes opportunity cost a bridge between [[GoalSetting]] and [[SelfDiscipline]]: goals clarify what should matter, while discipline repeatedly chooses that aim over competing uses of time.

## Key Claims
- Serious goals require choosing a few directions and letting other plausible identities or projects go.
- Time carries especially heavy opportunity cost because spent seconds cannot be recovered.
- Distraction and entertainment defaults are costly even when they feel minor, because they repeatedly displace learning, creating, and valued relationships.
- The cost of ambition includes saying no to good options, not only rejecting obviously bad habits.

## Evidence
- Choice cost: [[anything-is-possible-if-you-pay-the-price-mission-org-medium]] argues that trying to be everywhere or move in many directions prevents meaningful progress.
- Priority cost: [[anything-is-possible-if-you-pay-the-price-mission-org-medium]] says people must forego trivial and merely good options for the essential few.
- Time cost: [[anything-is-possible-if-you-pay-the-price-mission-org-medium]] treats time as the scarce, irreversible resource that makes distraction expensive.
- Daily default cost: [[anything-is-possible-if-you-pay-the-price-mission-org-medium]] uses spare minutes, alarm clocks, creative blocks, and phone use with loved ones as examples of small choices that reveal what someone is really paying for.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source uses motivational rhetoric and does not measure tradeoffs empirically. It can understate constraints such as money, caregiving, illness, discrimination, or unsafe environments, where the relevant opportunity costs are not freely chosen. The useful synthesis is therefore narrower: chosen aims require real tradeoffs, but tradeoffs alone do not guarantee any desired outcome.
