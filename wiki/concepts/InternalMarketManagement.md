## Definition
[[InternalMarketManagement]] is an organization design approach that uses internal prices, budgets, auctions, penalties, and settlement mechanisms to make teams or employees trade resources and services as if they were operating in a market.

## Current Synthesis
The Disco source shows the most extreme version currently in the wiki: a manufacturer turns meeting rooms, desks, computers, team services, task assignments, late work, coworker help, improvement proposals, and even internal publicity labor into priced transactions settled through a virtual currency. The management bet is that explicit prices reveal opportunity cost, reduce managerial policing, expose unnecessary work, and give employees more autonomy over what they do and whom they work with.

The same case also makes the limits visible. An internal market can sharpen focus and accountability, but it can also convert collaboration into continuous performance pressure, push quarterly profit thinking, distract research workers, and fail when local work culture does not accept the rules. Internal markets therefore sit between autonomy and coercion: they replace some managerial orders with price signals, but the organization still designs the currency, budgets, penalties, payouts, and contest rules.

## Key Claims
- Internal prices can make hidden opportunity costs visible inside an organization.
- Auctions and internal billing can reveal tasks or meetings that nobody values enough to fund or perform.
- Virtual currency can connect daily work choices to bonuses and profit contribution.
- Market-like autonomy can reduce some managerial coordination work by letting teams and employees move toward valued work.
- Internal markets can also intensify pressure, encourage short-termism, and strain research or culture-specific work.

## Evidence
- Resource pricing: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] says Disco charges Will for meeting rooms, desks, PCs, umbrellas, team services, and colleagues' time.
- Task auctions: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] says team members bid for assignments and unbid jobs often prove unnecessary.
- Supply-chain billing: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] describes sales, factory, engineering, HR, and IT work being paid through internal Will flows after a sale.
- Waste reduction: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] reports fewer useless meetings and lower overtime after penalties.
- Autonomy mechanism: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] says workers can abandon managers who misuse the system and move to other teams.
- Risk evidence: [[charging-employees-for-conference-rooms-helps-disco-boost-profit-bloomberg]] reports engineer departures, uncertain bonuses, continuous performance pressure, a Waseda professor's short-termism warning, and incomplete adoption in U.S. and China sites.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is one company case reported by Bloomberg, not a broad proof that internal markets generalize. Disco's strong results may depend on manufacturing context, Japanese organizational norms, semiconductor demand, management discipline, payout size, or other unreported factors. Pricing everything may also undervalue exploration, mentoring, trust repair, slack, compliance, and research work whose value appears only over longer horizons.
