## Definition
[[GeographicTechCareerTradeoffs]] are the interacting financial, professional, household, and lifestyle effects that determine whether moving to a technology hub improves a person's actual options rather than merely their nominal salary.

## Current Synthesis
[[MarkLane]]'s 2016 Bay Area-Seattle comparison separates three calculations that are often collapsed. For a renter with a strong technology-company offer, the Bay Area cash-pay premium could approximately cover the reported rent premium. For a prospective homeowner or household needing more space, the much larger home-price gap could dominate. For a worker able to access unusually strong companies, the longer-run value might instead come through progression, liquid public-company stock, a broader employer market, or a better chance of selecting a successful startup.

The decision therefore depends on marginal outcomes: after-tax and after-housing cash, achievable saving, housing tenure, commute, household needs, equity liquidity and variance, role quality, employer trajectory, and future mobility. Ecosystem density is an option, not a guaranteed payout. A person who cannot or does not want to rent frugally, delay ownership, tolerate startup risk, or organize life around the regional labor market may rationally choose another city even when headline compensation is higher.

## Key Claims
- Nominal salary should be converted into after-tax, after-housing, household-specific cash rather than compared directly across cities.
- Renting and buying produce materially different location decisions when home prices diverge more sharply than rents.
- Career progression, employer density, job mobility, and total compensation can matter more over time than first-year base salary.
- Public-company equity, illiquid startup options, and private-company valuations require different risk and liquidity treatment.
- A dense technology ecosystem creates more opportunities to search and switch, but does not guarantee access to successful companies or strong roles.
- Career stage, family needs, commute, space, lifestyle, and risk tolerance can outweigh a financial or professional premium.

## Evidence
- Cash and rent: [[does-it-make-sense-for-programmers-to-move-to-the-bay-area-triplebyte-blog]] estimates a $15,000-$33,000 Bay Area salary premium and a $17,000-$18,000 annual rent premium relative to Seattle, before a cited California tax cost.
- Ownership: [[does-it-make-sense-for-programmers-to-move-to-the-bay-area-triplebyte-blog]] reports 2016 median home values above $807,000 in the two Bay Area metros versus about $395,000 in Seattle.
- Career progression: [[does-it-make-sense-for-programmers-to-move-to-the-bay-area-triplebyte-blog]] cites higher destination offers for workers leaving San Francisco and much larger senior total compensation at Google and Facebook than at Amazon and Microsoft.
- Equity distinctions: [[does-it-make-sense-for-programmers-to-move-to-the-bay-area-triplebyte-blog]] separates liquid cash and stock bonuses from high-variance, illiquid startup options and warns that private valuations may not be realizable.
- Opportunity density: [[does-it-make-sense-for-programmers-to-move-to-the-bay-area-triplebyte-blog]] argues that more employers and startups make selective search easier while acknowledging that many companies fail.
- Personal boundary: [[does-it-make-sense-for-programmers-to-move-to-the-bay-area-triplebyte-blog]] treats early homeownership, family space, and place preference as legitimate reasons not to move.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a historical, employer-published comparison rather than a reproducible causal study. It mixes occupational averages, listings, self-reports, internal placements, metro medians, selected companies, and crowdsourced compensation; those populations need not be comparable. Its author acknowledges California income tax, equity illiquidity, startup failure, and valuation distortion but does not model a complete budget, benefits, commute, childcare, health, school, moving cost, partner income, or selection effects. Triplebyte benefited from Bay Area placements. The three source charts were unavailable for inspection, and all amounts describe 2015-2016 conditions rather than current markets.

## What Changed
- Created a multi-factor location-choice model that separates renting, ownership, progression, equity, ecosystem access, and personal constraints.
