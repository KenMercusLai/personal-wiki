## Definition
[[AcceleratorEcosystemAdoption]] is the process by which a GPU, NPU, or other xPU becomes practically teachable, deployable, and familiar through accessible hardware, usable software, documentation, training pathways, and sustained institutional participation.

## Current Synthesis
Campus adoption is not a hardware-placement campaign. Schools need affordable equipment, dependable operation around deadlines, offline or locally controllable teaching environments, and systems that instructors and assistants can run without resident vendor support. Vendors therefore have to align their ecosystem investment with educational constraints rather than optimize for short-lived visibility.

Adoption can deepen in stages. Invisible use behind an application establishes basic substitutability; semester-long framework use creates first-hand performance and usability experience; operator and compiler courses expose programming-model differences; hardware courses and research develop scarce architecture expertise. New compute-intensive courses and well-run competitions offer lower-path-dependence entry points, but deeper curriculum change requires instructor knowledge, open materials, research links, and repeated investment.

## Key Claims
- Practical access, reliability, openness, and self-service operation are prerequisites for broad ecosystem learning.
- Course pathways differ in both adoption difficulty and the depth of durable platform knowledge they create.
- New compute-intensive courses can be a beachhead because demand is high while incumbent-specific curriculum is less entrenched.
- Deep operator, compiler, and architecture education depends on experienced instructors and research-to-curriculum continuity.
- Competitions can recruit and educate participants only when operations, fairness, infrastructure, and continuity earn trust.
- Long-term substitutability is a more credible first goal than promotional claims of immediate technical superiority.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The current evidence is one teacher-practitioner's qualitative analysis, not a cross-campus adoption study. The four-level taxonomy does not measure learning or purchasing effects, local infrastructure is not inherently more reliable than cloud infrastructure, and favorable memories of one competition do not establish causal recruiting or ecosystem outcomes. Nvidia's accumulated advantage may also differ by discipline, institution, geography, workload, and future export or procurement conditions.

## What Changed
- Established a staged model connecting shallow awareness, sustained use, low-level programming, and hardware expertise.
- Made school budgeting, reliability, local control, teaching governance, and self-service support explicit adoption constraints.
- Added competitions as a lower-barrier but operations-intensive complement to curriculum integration.
