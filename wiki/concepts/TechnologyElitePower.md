## Definition
[[TechnologyElitePower]] is the combined economic, platform, investment, legal, social, and agenda-setting influence held by a small group of technology founders, executives, and investors.

## Current Synthesis
The sources' concern is not wealth alone. Technology leaders may control companies that mediate communication, collect data, allocate capital, shape imagined futures, and influence the media institutions meant to scrutinize them. Private relationships and mechanisms such as litigation funding can exercise public power without the visibility or accountability applied to elected institutions. Tiku presents Thiel's financing as one example within a broader elite-media system; Holiday's account adds a proposed operating mechanism involving a dedicated company, investigators, lawyers, a multi-year timeline, and a large budget.

## Key Claims
- Company control, personal wealth, investment networks, and platform ownership can reinforce one another.
- Platforms that monitor and distribute information give their leaders power over both users and the media covering them.
- Private legal or financial action can alter the press environment without passing through public political accountability.
- Detailed planning can make concentrated private power operational by connecting money to personnel, institutions, timelines, and legal tactics.
- Celebrity and visionary status can convert speculative statements into broad agenda-setting power.
- Concentrated technology power warrants scrutiny proportional to its effects, not merely to its formal institutional category.

## Evidence
- Combined influence: [[ground-control-to-silicon-valley]] places founders, platform directors, venture investors, and reporters in a dense shared network.
- Platform-media tension: [[ground-control-to-silicon-valley]] notes media dependence on Facebook and distrust around platform bias and distribution control.
- Private coercive capacity: [[ground-control-to-silicon-valley]] uses Thiel's secret financing of litigation against Gawker as an example of wealth shaping media survival.
- Operational mechanism: [[heres-the-technique-that-ambitious-people-use-to-get-what-they-want-ryanholiday]] reports a proposal for a Thiel-funded shell company, investigators, lawyers, a three-to-five-year horizon, and a $10 million budget aimed at finding claims against Gawker.
- Agenda setting: [[ground-control-to-silicon-valley]] shows Musk and Bezos receiving sustained attention for distant space futures while immediate governance conflicts remained unresolved.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are reported essays rather than a systematic map of ownership, lobbying, litigation, or platform effects. Tiku groups people with different roles and interests under a broad elite-power critique and does not quantify their influence relative to governments, legacy media, finance, or civil society. Holiday recounts the Thiel proposal to illustrate preparation, not to provide a complete evidentiary history of Gawker's litigation or closure. Wealth, technical ambition, conference participation, and one detailed campaign do not by themselves establish coordinated control across the technology sector.

## What Changed
- Created the concept as a multi-channel power model while preserving the distinction between concentration and coordinated conspiracy.
- Added Holiday's account of how funding, staffing, organizational structure, time, and legal tactics made one private campaign operational.
