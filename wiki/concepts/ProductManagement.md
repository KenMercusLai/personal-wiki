## Definition
[[ProductManagement]] is the cross-functional product role responsible for turning customer needs, technical possibilities, business constraints, organizational resistance, and scarce development capacity into a product outcome.

## Current Synthesis
The SVPG source defines product management by what it is not: not backlog administration, not CEO escalation, not project management, not design ownership, and not stakeholder compromise. The stronger version of the role resembles CEO-like accountability without formal authority: the product manager must understand enough of the business, technology, customer environment, marketing, sales, finance, legal, partnerships, service, and user experience to help the team discover a solution that works for both customers and the company.

Across the cases, the role's distinctive contribution is integration. [[MartinaLauchengco]] helped turn [[MicrosoftWordForMac]] away from internal code-convergence pressure toward Mac-specific performance and experience. [[KateArnold]] helped make [[Netflix]]'s subscription model economically workable through the queue, ratings, and recommendation engine. [[JaneManning]] helped [[GoogleAdWords]] survive sales and engineering objections by finding a design that separated ad surfaces and rewarded relevance. [[AlexPressland]], [[CamilleHearst]], and [[LeaHickman]] each show the same pattern in distribution, media integration, and business-model transformation.

The pre-greenlight version of the same responsibility comes before development begins. The PM has to sell the idea internally, translate it into stakeholder language, understand the company's KPIs, create enough visual form for decision makers to understand the proposal, and prioritize ideas by expected impact and build difficulty. In that frame, PM work is not only solution integration after a project exists; it is also earning the right for the organization to spend real development money.

Caroli's PBB article adds a useful boundary around backlog work. Product management may organize and prioritize product work, but [[UserStories]] should not become a solitary Product Owner writing task. [[ProductBacklogBuilding]] makes backlog creation a collaborative process where personas, features, PBIs, acceptance criteria, UI artifacts, and enablers are visible to the team.

## Key Claims
- Product management is distinct from project management, design, sales, and stakeholder committee work.
- Product managers are accountable for business outcomes, not just feature definition or delivery tracking.
- Strong product work integrates customer value, technical feasibility, business viability, legal constraints, marketing, sales, finance, operations, and user experience.
- Winning product solutions often emerge through design and engineering collaboration rather than direct user requests or sales demands.
- Product managers need smart, creative, persistent leadership because their work often means overcoming internal objections without formal authority.
- Before a product is greenlit, product managers need internal persuasion, KPI fluency, rough visual communication, and prioritization discipline.
- Backlog authorship should remain collaborative; user stories are stronger when the team shares persona, feature, PBI, and acceptance-criteria context.

## Evidence
- Role boundary: [[behind-every-great-product-silicon-valley-product-group]] rejects PM models based on CEO escalation, stakeholder fights, backlog administration, or roadmap administration.
- Business-outcome accountability: [[behind-every-great-product-silicon-valley-product-group]] says the PM must ensure business outcomes and understand interrelated constraints across the company.
- Cross-functional integration: [[behind-every-great-product-silicon-valley-product-group]] uses Netflix's subscription redesign, AdWords' sales-and-engineering compromise, and iTunes' American Idol integration to show product work spanning finance, marketing, fulfillment, legal, engineering, and customer experience.
- Collaboration source of solutions: [[behind-every-great-product-silicon-valley-product-group]] argues the winning solutions came from product, design, and engineering collaboration rather than from users or sales specifying the answer.
- Leadership without authority: [[behind-every-great-product-silicon-valley-product-group]] presents the PM as CEO-like in scope but different because no one reports to the PM.
- Pre-greenlight advocacy: [[being-a-product-manager-how-to-get-your-products-built]] says PMs must sell ideas to stakeholders, know company KPIs, use visuals, and prioritize ideas before development resources are committed.
- Collaborative backlog work: [[blog-paulo-caroli-martinfowler-com-product-backlog-building-canvas]] argues that anyone on the team can write user stories when the canvas gives them shared persona, feature, PBI, and benefit context.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are product-management advocacy essays built from selected cases and practitioner advice, so they emphasize PM agency while depending on teams, founders, engineers, executives, and organizational context. They do not provide failure cases, quantitative comparisons, a hiring rubric, or a calibrated way to estimate idea impact before build.

The PBB source also qualifies PM-centered backlog administration: if story writing is centralized too tightly, the team can lose the conversation and shared understanding that user stories are meant to preserve.

## What Changed
- Created Product Management as an outcome-accountable, cross-functional leadership role rather than a delivery-administration role.
- Added the pre-greenlight PM responsibilities of selling ideas, aligning with KPIs, using rough visuals, and prioritizing by impact and difficulty.
- Added collaborative backlog authorship as a boundary on Product Owner-only story writing.
