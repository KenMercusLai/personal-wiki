## Definition
[[RepeatableLearningFromHistory]] is the practice of studying past success or failure by separating transferable skills and broad behavioral patterns from one-time circumstances, timing, institutions, markets, and path-dependent conditions.

## Current Synthesis
Housel's essay reframes luck as a learning problem: instead of arguing whether a successful person was "lucky," ask which parts of the outcome could not be repeated. That distinction lets skill and historical contingency coexist. Bezos can be unusually visionary and Amazon can still owe part of its scale to Internet growth, market timing, weak incumbents, politics, and regulation that a new founder cannot recreate on demand.

The practical lesson is to learn at the right level of abstraction. Buffett's patience, Rockefeller's distribution focus, Musk's risk-taking and branding, and Bezos's management and long-term thinking are more repeatable than the exact market, legal, competitive, or technology conditions surrounding their original wins. Zweig's crash example gives the failure mode: after the dot-com bust, some investors learned only "avoid Internet stocks" rather than the broader lesson against speculating in overvalued assets, leaving them exposed to the housing bubble.

## Key Claims
- Luck debates become more useful when translated into repeatability questions.
- Exceptional skill can coexist with non-repeatable historical circumstances.
- Broad behavioral or strategic lessons transfer better than exact tactics copied from another era.
- Narrow postmortems can preserve the deeper mistake while changing only the surface asset, market, or label.
- History is useful for judgment, but the future rarely repeats the detailed conditions that produced a past outcome.
- Path-dependent systems such as evolution and markets can produce real patterns without making those paths reproducible.

## Evidence
- Luck reframing: [[collaborative-fund-lucky-vs-repeatable]] argues that asking what is not repeatable avoids the status and ego problems around calling someone lucky.
- Skill plus context: [[collaborative-fund-lucky-vs-repeatable]] says Bezos was highly skilled, while Amazon also benefited from Internet growth, market conditions, competitors, politics, and regulation that cannot be replicated exactly.
- Emulation boundary: [[collaborative-fund-lucky-vs-repeatable]] says learners should emulate repeatable skills rather than non-repeatable success context.
- Evolution analogy: [[collaborative-fund-lucky-vs-repeatable]] uses [[DollosLaw]] to show that outcomes can arise from specific forces without those forces returning.
- Business history: [[collaborative-fund-lucky-vs-repeatable]] says business and investing history should teach broad lessons without treating the past as a direct future map.
- Narrow-lesson risk: [[collaborative-fund-lucky-vs-repeatable]] quotes [[JasonZweig]] on investors who avoided Internet stocks after the dot-com crash but then repeated speculative behavior in housing.
- Transferable examples: [[collaborative-fund-lucky-vs-repeatable]] separates repeatable traits such as Buffett's patience, Rockefeller's distribution focus, Musk's risk-taking and branding, and Bezos's management and long-term thinking from their less-repeatable historical contexts.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is an essayistic judgment framework rather than a formal causal method. It does not provide a rigorous test for determining which parts of an outcome are repeatable, and some supposedly repeatable traits may still depend on capital, status, timing, team, regulation, or social permission. The framework is strongest as a guardrail against naive copying, not as proof that broad lessons are always transferable.
