## Definition
[[ClinicalDigitalFootprints]] are online traces such as profiles, posts, photos, videos, and social-network context that clinicians may interpret as evidence about a patient's condition, behavior, risk, or support system.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats online material as a new kind of collateral history. A profile photo, suicide letters, videos, visible relationships, or evidence of substance use can carry clinical meaning, especially in acute psychiatric care. But digital footprints are fragile evidence: clinicians may not know whether a post is current, staged, ironic, private in intent, or representative of the patient's present mental state. Clinical use therefore requires both interpretive humility and explicit handling of how the information was found, discussed, and documented.

## Key Claims
- Online traces can reveal clinically relevant mental state, risk, behavior, and support information.
- Social-media evidence is time-sensitive and may be inaccurate or misleading.
- Video can provide richer mental-status clues than static text or photos.
- Digital footprints blur public/private expectations because accessibility does not equal consent for clinical use.
- Clinical interpretation of digital traces can alter power dynamics between patient and practitioner.
- Digital footprints become more sensitive when linked to durable medical records.

## Evidence
- Risk signal: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] describes a Facebook profile picture and apparent suicide letters that led clinicians to involve police and hospitalize a patient.
- Emergency context: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] says social media can show substance use, self-harm, or family supports in acute care.
- Video richness: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] says videos can reveal mood, speech rate, and organization of thought.
- Accuracy limit: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] warns that clinicians may not know whether online information is accurate or timely.
- Power shift: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] quotes [[PaulAppelbaum]] saying online lives are visible in both directions, equalizing knowledge while creating new professional demands.
- Record linkage: [[cancer-on-nautilus-getting-googled-by-your-doctor-is-the-new-normal]] says charting online findings creates medical-record information that did not previously exist.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not prove that online footprints improve diagnosis or outcomes. It focuses on mental healthcare and cancer-care support examples, so the concept should not be generalized to all clinical specialties without additional sources. Online footprints may also reflect performance, old material, privacy-setting accidents, or platform context that clinicians misunderstand.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to distinguish patient online traces from generic behavioral data.
