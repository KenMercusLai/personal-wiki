## Definition
[[JournalingPractice]] is the habit of recording daily experience in a structured enough form that reflection becomes easier to start, sustain, and reuse.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames journaling as a high-benefit habit that often fails at the moment of the blank page. Its answer is the T.L.C. method: write a few sentences each day about something to thank, something learned, and something connected. The method matters less as a comprehensive diary system than as a friction-reducing prompt that makes daily attention more deliberate.

This also gives journaling a light knowledge function. The "learn" and "connect" prompts ask the journaler to notice small lessons, analogies, patterns, conversations, and future collaboration openings, so the journal becomes a small bridge between personal reflection, [[ActiveLearning]], and [[PersonalKnowledgeManagement]].

## Key Claims
- Journaling habits are easier to start when the prompt is memorable, small, and repeatable.
- The T.L.C. method structures daily reflection around thanking, learning, and connecting.
- Specific daily events make reflection more grounded than generic statements.
- A prompt can shape attention before writing because the journaler begins looking for material during the day.
- Journaling can support learning and relationship awareness when it captures small lessons, analogies, conversations, and follow-up possibilities.

## Evidence
- Low-friction habit design: [[a-better-easier-way-to-journal-personal-growth-medium]] says the author built the T.L.C. method because blank-page resistance and laziness made journaling hard to sustain.
- Three-part structure: [[a-better-easier-way-to-journal-personal-growth-medium]] defines T.L.C. as Thank, Learn, and Connect, with only a few sentences under each heading.
- Grounded reflection: [[a-better-easier-way-to-journal-personal-growth-medium]] tells readers to thank specific daily events rather than repeat generic gratitude phrases.
- Attention shaping: [[a-better-easier-way-to-journal-personal-growth-medium]] argues that needing to write about three things makes people look for those three things during the day.
- Learning and connection: [[a-better-easier-way-to-journal-personal-growth-medium]] treats daily learning, analogies, conversations, and future collaboration topics as journal material.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a short personal-growth essay rather than empirical research on journaling outcomes. It claims that journaling benefits mental and physical health but relies on external general support rather than presenting evidence inside the article. The T.L.C. method may suit beginners or blocked journalers better than people who need open-ended diary writing, therapeutic processing, or detailed project logs.
