## Definition
[[HypeAndCraftCultures]] is [[ArtKleiner]]'s distinction between a commercial culture oriented toward financing, deals, scale, visibility, and urgent promises and a production culture oriented toward reflection, professional judgment, elegance, and pride in delivery.

## Current Synthesis
The frame explains startup conflict as a clash between interdependent forms of work rather than a simple failure of commitment. Commercial actors can win capital and customers yet overpromise scope or treat practitioners as interchangeable resources; craft actors can protect quality yet resist deadlines, compromise, or strategic urgency. Each side becomes destructive when it dismisses the other's knowledge.

The proposed balance is not to eliminate either culture. Commercial leaders should provide clear boundaries, time, and professional autonomy, while craft practitioners should understand market strategy and communicate constraints in terms decision-makers can use. Persistent teams and leaders who can translate between both cultures provide the operating bridge.

## Key Claims
- Commercial ambition and production craft are distinct but mutually necessary forms of startup work.
- Hype-driven commitments can create impossible scope, deadline, budget, and quality combinations.
- Craft motivation depends heavily on autonomy, reflection, elegance, and professional pride rather than compensation alone.
- Treating craft workers as commodities can turn pay and stock options into substitutes for commitment.
- Translation, boundaries, and shared accountability can make the tension productive rather than debilitating.

## Evidence
- Commercial incentives: [[corporate-culture-in-internet-time]] connects capital raising, prominent deals, market-share urgency, and infrastructure costs to expansive promises.
- Craft incentives: [[corporate-culture-in-internet-time]] describes programmers, designers, and information architects as seeking deep understanding and elegant delivery.
- Conflict mechanism: [[corporate-culture-in-internet-time]] recounts practitioners unable to challenge commitments that exceed time or budget.
- Reciprocal learning: [[corporate-culture-in-internet-time]] argues that each culture must recognize its dependence on the other.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This is a practitioner typology, not a validated classification. People can move between commercial and craft modes, and functions such as product management, design, operations, support, and client service do not fit neatly into a binary. The source also treats early dot-com conditions as especially intense; later organizations, labor markets, tooling, and delivery practices may change how the tension appears.

- Created the concept from Kleiner's diagnosis of early Internet-company culture clashes.
