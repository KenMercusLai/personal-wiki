## Definition
[[SharedInformationBias]] is the distortion that occurs when information known by many people receives too much aggregate weight while less common but relevant information is undercounted.

## Current Synthesis
The source uses shared-information bias to explain why independent-looking crowd judgments can still fail. If most respondents rely on the same visible sources, averaging their answers counts that shared evidence many times and makes minority information seem less important than it is. Metaknowledge-based survey designs can detect the bias by comparing the crowd's average response with what respondents expect the crowd's response to be.

## Key Claims
- Shared information can dominate group judgment even when people submit answers independently.
- The bias makes crowd averages overrepresent common knowledge and underrepresent minority evidence.
- Asking people to predict the group's answer can reveal when the crowd is anchored on common information.
- Pivot-style corrections can move an aggregate estimate away from the shared-information pull.
- Forecasting communities can show this bias when widely circulated narratives overpower more diverse information.

## Evidence
- Independent-looking failure: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] says crowd members may make independent judgments while still working from the same information.
- Overcounting mechanism: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] argues that common knowledge gets counted once per respondent and can drown out diverse knowledge.
- Prediction gap: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] describes Jack Soll and Asa Palley's method of comparing the crowd's response with its predicted average response.
- Pivot correction: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] uses a New York Times versus Economist growth-forecast example to explain correcting away from a shared forecast.
- Forecasting example: [[a-mathematical-bs-detector-can-boost-the-wisdom-of-crowds-aeon-essays]] says Metaculus polls on Bernie Sanders and Brexit showed mismatches consistent with shared-information bias.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The essay gives illustrative and experimental examples rather than a universal rule. Shared-information bias is most plausible when respondents draw from overlapping sources; it is less central when a strong consensus reflects genuinely broad and accurate knowledge.
