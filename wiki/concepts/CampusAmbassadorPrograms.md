## Definition
[[CampusAmbassadorPrograms]] recruit students to represent and distribute a product within their local university networks, combining participant acquisition, motivation, enablement, and performance management.

## Current Synthesis
The Morning Brew case suggests that ambassador programs face two separate selection problems: recruiting plausible candidates and discovering who will remain active. A small, carefully screened cohort still lost half its members, while automated recruitment expanded reach but left participants with too little individual support to produce many strong outcomes. The reported hybrid treated early activity as a second-stage signal, promoting engaged ambassadors into a private group with coaching, peer examples, scripts, and flyers.

This design joins scalable discovery to concentrated enablement. A broad cohort creates campus coverage and a pool from which committed participants can emerge; a tiered program then spends scarce support time where observed effort makes it more promising. Advancement, access, practical competence, product affinity, and résumé value may motivate participation without making cash the dominant reason to join. The case nevertheless cannot establish that money generally reduces motivation or that an 80/20 contribution pattern is universal.

## Key Claims
- Pre-recruitment selectivity does not reliably predict sustained ambassador participation.
- Automated recruitment can expand geographic reach while weakening coaching and average participant performance.
- Observed activity can serve as a promotion signal for allocating scarce high-touch support.
- Private peer groups, scripts, flyers, and coaching can reduce the execution burden on committed ambassadors.
- Status, access, product affinity, and career signaling may be more useful than cash in some volunteer-like advocacy programs.
- Program success should distinguish campus coverage, active ambassadors, subscriber acquisition, retention, and cost rather than treating enrollment as the outcome.

## Evidence
- Selectivity and attrition: [[happy-xiao-ru-he-kuo-san]] reports that six of 12 carefully selected students left within weeks, despite one remaining participant producing 1,000 registrations in two weeks.
- Scale without enablement: [[happy-xiao-ru-he-kuo-san]] says automated recruitment brought in more than 100 ambassadors and reached more campuses but produced few high-performing participants.
- Tiered hybrid: [[happy-xiao-ru-he-kuo-san]] describes recruiting about 50 students automatically, promoting active participants, and giving the advanced group private peer access, coaching, scripts, and flyers.
- Reported outcome and motivation: [[happy-xiao-ru-he-kuo-san]] says the hybrid reached more than 100 campuses, produced more than ten ambassadors with over 500 subscribers, and found cash weaker than product affection and résumé value.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is a single secondary founder-success story without a control group, consistent cohort definitions, total registrations, subscriber retention, acquisition cost, time cost, or campus-level outcome distribution. The first cohort's attrition does not prove that college students are generally unreliable, and the later result does not isolate tiering from cohort size, accumulated learning, product growth, seasonality, or brand recognition. The reported 80/20 pattern and adverse effect of money are heuristics in this account, not universal laws; compensation may also be ethically appropriate when ambassador work creates material value.
