## Definition
[[GrowthHacking]] is a startup-oriented marketing and product practice that prioritizes rapid user, revenue, traffic, or marketplace growth through tactics tightly coupled to product behavior, distribution channels, incentives, trust, and attention.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats growth hacking as a broad pattern library rather than a single method. The strongest examples turn ordinary use into acquisition: a shared file invites a new [[Dropbox]] user, a [[Hotmail]] email carries a signup prompt, a [[TripAdvisor]] badge sends hotel-site visitors back to the review platform, and a [[Spotify]] embed routes listeners toward the service. Other examples rely on reducing friction through free trials or freemium access, manufacturing attention through stunts or memorable advertising, converting expertise into inbound traffic, and solving trust barriers in marketplaces. The concept is useful when it names a specific growth mechanism, but weak when it becomes a retrospective label for any successful marketing.

## Key Claims
- Growth hacking is most useful when it describes the mechanism connecting product behavior to acquisition.
- Built-in sharing and public visibility can turn existing users into distribution.
- Incentives can accelerate growth when referral, affiliate, or reward economics fit customer value.
- Free, trial, or freemium entry can reduce adoption risk before monetization.
- Content, SEO, tools, and community participation can compound attention over time.
- Trust design can be a growth lever when the market's core barrier is perceived risk.
- Retrospective case lists can overstate causality when they do not separate tactic, product-market fit, timing, brand, and budget.

## Evidence
- Product-integrated distribution: [[51-examples-of-growth-hacking-strategies-techniques-from-the-worlds-most-innovative-businesses]] cites [[Dropbox]], [[Hotmail]], [[LinkedIn]], [[TripAdvisor]], [[Spotify]], and [[Airbnb]] as examples where normal use or public artifacts pull more people into the product.
- Incentive design: [[51-examples-of-growth-hacking-strategies-techniques-from-the-worlds-most-innovative-businesses]] presents [[PayPal]] cash referrals, [[Groupon]] friend sharing, [[WPEngine]] affiliate commissions, and [[Firebox]] affiliate networks as paid or reward-based growth loops.
- Friction reduction: [[51-examples-of-growth-hacking-strategies-techniques-from-the-worlds-most-innovative-businesses]] uses [[Shopify]], [[Spotify]], HideMyAss, and [[PokemonGo]] to show free trials, free tiers, or free downloads creating larger adoption surfaces.
- Content and attention: [[51-examples-of-growth-hacking-strategies-techniques-from-the-worlds-most-innovative-businesses]] links [[Buffer]], [[HubSpot]], [[Moz]], [[Buzzfeed]], InVision, Paddy Power, Moonpig, and [[GoPro]] to content, stunts, memorable brand devices, and user-generated publicity.
- Trust as growth infrastructure: [[51-examples-of-growth-hacking-strategies-techniques-from-the-worlds-most-innovative-businesses]] describes [[EBay]] ratings and escrow, [[Zappos]] returns, [[TripAdvisor]] reviews, and [[Kickstarter]] community legitimacy as ways to unlock participation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a practitioner list, not a controlled analysis. Some examples are conventional advertising, PR, affiliate marketing, paid search, or product design relabeled as growth hacking. Several metrics are snapshots from older periods, and many outcomes likely depended on timing, product-market fit, category novelty, media budgets, founder charisma, or network effects as much as the named tactic.
