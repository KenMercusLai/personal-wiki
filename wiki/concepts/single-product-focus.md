Single-product focus is the strategy of continuing to improve one product with evidence of traction instead of repeatedly starting unrelated products in search of diversification. In the OnlineOrNot source, the contrast is with the indie-maker "small bets" idea: launching many products may look diversified, but each new product repeats the hardest work of finding users, positioning the product, and reaching the first meaningful revenue.

Steve Ridout's Readlang update adds a lower-revenue version of the same pattern. After three years on one language-learning web app, signups and revenue had grown each year, user praise suggested real utility, and the product was profitable enough to keep alive. The hard part was that this focus still paid less than ordinary employment would have, so the decision to continue depended on whether product learning and traction could keep compounding.

The operating reason is compounding learning. The article says that OnlineOrNot improvements can build on one another: fixing one adoption problem creates a small gain, then improving that fix can create more gains. A product that stays alive long enough also accumulates customer memory and delayed feedback, such as rejected prospects returning later, early free users converting, and long-tail bugs becoming visible only after months of use.

The concept does not reject risk management. Instead, it shifts the risk controls away from product proliferation and toward avoiding platform dependence, choosing work the maker remains interested in, and building where existing product knowledge can keep compounding.

## Signals

- A product already has enough traction for operating improvements to matter.
- The next revenue tier depends more on better acquisition, onboarding, pricing, or feature depth than on inventing a new market.
- Starting another unrelated product would repeat the earliest and least informed stage of customer discovery.
- The main risks are platform dependence, loss of interest, or insufficient patience rather than lack of portfolio-style diversification.
- Current revenue may still be unattractive, but usage, customer value, or growth rate provide a concrete reason to keep investing in the same product.
