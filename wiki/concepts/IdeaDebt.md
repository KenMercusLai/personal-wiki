## Definition
[[IdeaDebt]] is the accumulating psychological burden created when a person invests fantasy, identity, planning, and expectation in a project without making proportionate progress on the artifact itself.

## Current Synthesis
[[JessicaAbel]] presents idea debt as a creative avoidance loop. A person repeatedly imagines the finished work, its reception, and the identity or rewards it will confer; planning, research, lore, and public discussion then make the unwritten project feel larger and more important. Because reality cannot immediately equal that ideal, the gap between current ability and imagined achievement raises the cost of beginning and strengthens [[Perfectionism]].

The proposed exit has two legitimate branches. A creator can act before expectation compounds, accept imperfect present ability, finish bounded work, and use [[ProlificPractice]] to develop. Or the creator can decide that an old idea belongs to an earlier self and abandon it, preventing [[SunkCostFallacy]] from consuming current attention. The useful decision is therefore not “finish every idea”; it is to convert a live idea into concrete work or consciously release it.

## Key Claims
- Imagined outcomes and creator identity can become liabilities when they displace concrete making.
- Repeated planning or world-building can increase expectations faster than it increases the ability to deliver.
- Perfectionism turns the ordinary gap between taste and current skill into a reason not to begin.
- Repeated completion can convert the skill gap into learning rather than self-judgment.
- Conscious abandonment is a valid response when an old project no longer reflects current priorities.
- Unreleased projects consume scarce attention even when no visible work is occurring.

## Evidence
- Fantasy without artifact: [[imagining-your-future-projects-is-holding-you-back]] uses the invented *Forest Lords* series to contrast elaborate lore and anticipated acclaim with the absence of a book.
- Compounding expectations: [[imagining-your-future-projects-is-holding-you-back]] relays Kazu Kibuishi's snowboarding analogy, in which waiting at the jump increases fear and the demand to perform impressively.
- Skill development: [[imagining-your-future-projects-is-holding-you-back]] uses Ira Glass's account of taste outpacing early ability to argue for deadlines and a large volume of finished work.
- Abandonment path: [[imagining-your-future-projects-is-holding-you-back]] reports that explicitly dropping stale projects restored the author's attention for work aligned with her present interests.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept comes from a reflective practitioner essay rather than controlled evidence, and the source does not measure attention recovery, completion rates, or craft improvement. Planning, research, incubation, and world-building can be necessary parts of complex work rather than avoidance. Volume does not guarantee useful learning without feedback and reflection, while high-risk or costly work may warrant substantial preparation. A project that is difficult, slow, or temporarily dormant is not necessarily stale; the relevant test is whether present reasons justify future effort, not whether progress is immediately visible.

## What Changed
- Created a synthesis separating idea accumulation from proportionate artifact-making.
- Distinguished two exits from idea debt: imperfect action and deliberate abandonment.
