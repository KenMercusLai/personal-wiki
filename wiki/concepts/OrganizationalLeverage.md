## Definition
[[OrganizationalLeverage]] is the redesign of work so a fixed amount of effort produces greater impact, often through software, automation, reusable systems, or capability growth.

## Current Synthesis
The eShares source distinguishes leverage from efficiency by holding effort fixed and seeking more impact rather than holding output fixed and reducing effort. It embeds that test in a learn-execute cycle: learn a role, apply the knowledge, automate or otherwise outgrow the repeatable work, and begin another learning phase. Calling every employee a "software person" makes this a company-wide operating principle rather than an engineering-only technique.

The distinction is useful but not absolute. Automation, reuse, and better systems frequently create leverage by making a process more efficient, and some fixed-output work—care, controls, maintenance, compliance, or relationship building—cannot safely be judged by a ten-times-impact slogan. Leverage therefore works best as a search question, not a universal performance metric.

## Key Claims
- Leverage asks how to increase impact from a fixed amount of effort.
- Software and automation can make non-engineering work reusable and scalable.
- Roles can evolve through repeated learning, execution, automation, and renewed learning.
- A leverage orientation should not erase maintenance, care, quality, control, or other work whose value resists simple multiplication.

## Evidence
- Impact test: [[eshares-101-owners-manual-blog-by-carta-medium]] reports eShares asking employees how they could multiply their impact for the same effort.
- Software translation: [[eshares-101-owners-manual-blog-by-carta-medium]] says all employees should translate real-world problems into software problems, regardless of job title.
- Role evolution: [[eshares-101-owners-manual-blog-by-carta-medium]] describes learning, executing, then automating a job or growing beyond it before restarting the cycle.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is one founder's prescriptive culture document and does not measure output quality, employee workload, automation failures, displacement, or who captures the gains. Its dismissal of efficiency is rhetorical and overlooks their overlap. Ten-times framing can also reward visible scalable output while undervaluing essential low-volume work and can create pressure to automate before a process is understood.

- Created the concept from eShares' company-wide leverage and learn-execute model.
