## Definition
[[EngineeringCareerArchitecture]] is the structured system a software organization uses to describe engineering levels, expectations, promotion signals, evaluation practices, and the kinds of impact that count at each stage.

## Current Synthesis
The NerdWallet source frames engineering career architecture as a tool for fairness and growth, but also as a source of distortion when it becomes too checklist-like. The original rubric worked better for a 25-person engineering team, where managers could preserve shared nuance informally. At about 100 engineers, that implicit context weakened: managers applied expectations inconsistently, and engineers could rationally treat the architecture as a set of tasks to complete for promotion.

The revised architecture keeps levels because they support compensation, professional-growth signaling, and external legibility, but it changes the shape of the evidence. Rather than enumerating narrow tasks, NerdWallet describes each level through high-level role distinctions, skill or behavior statements, rationale, broad expectations, and anonymized observed examples. The fairness move is especially important: Staff-level reach should extend beyond the immediate team, but the relevant partners may be product managers, designers, user researchers, or other stakeholders, not only the broader engineering organization.

Terrible Software supplies a complementary behavioral test: observe what an engineer does when the assignment is underspecified. Asking about the real problem, user, assumptions, downside, priorities, and removable scope can turn vague work into an executable plan and make project risk visible before implementation. That signal fits a craft-oriented architecture better than a technology or tenure checklist, but the essay's claim that it is the single foundation of seniority should not replace multidimensional, context-specific evidence.

## Key Claims
- Career architecture should describe craft expectations and values rather than only promotion tasks.
- Levels are useful for compensation and career signaling, but they remain blunt abstractions over varied engineering work.
- High-level role descriptions help justify level distinctions without pretending that every engineer at a level behaves identically.
- Examples from real engineers can make subjective expectations more concrete without overprescribing one path to impact.
- Promotion criteria should recognize multiple impact surfaces so product and platform engineers are not judged by implicitly different standards.
- Performance on ambiguous work can reveal problem framing, prioritization, assumption testing, and project-risk judgment that technology checklists miss.
- Architecture changes need validation, manager calibration, and explicit rollout because subjectivity raises the burden on evaluators.

## Evidence
- Checklist risk: [[building-engineering-careers-at-nerdwallet-nerdwallet]] says engineers approached the old rubric as tasks to execute, which was rational but counter to a learning and growth mindset.
- Craft frame: [[building-engineering-careers-at-nerdwallet-nerdwallet]] says NerdWallet moved toward subjective statements of values and expectations with examples at every level.
- Level utility: [[building-engineering-careers-at-nerdwallet-nerdwallet]] says leveling helps with compensation and with engineers demonstrating growth when they move on.
- Role distinctions: [[building-engineering-careers-at-nerdwallet-nerdwallet]] defines levels from technical foundation and production ownership through multi-team coordination, new product and technology thought, and company technical vision.
- Example-based calibration: [[building-engineering-careers-at-nerdwallet-nerdwallet]] says NerdWallet identified engineers who embodied each level, discussed their behaviors, and used wide-ranging examples to avoid overprescription.
- Product/platform fairness: [[building-engineering-careers-at-nerdwallet-nerdwallet]] says Staff-level product engineers may create reach through product managers, designers, and user researchers, while platform engineers may more often influence other engineering teams.
- Ambiguous-work signal: [[terrible-software-what-actually-makes-you-senior]] proposes evaluating whether an engineer clarifies the problem, user, assumptions, downside, priorities, and scope before coding.
- Hiring mismatch: [[terrible-software-what-actually-makes-you-senior]] argues that technology, tenure, and algorithm screens do not directly test conversion of incomplete requirements into shippable plans.
- Change validation: [[building-engineering-careers-at-nerdwallet-nerdwallet]] says the team compared old and new architectures, beta tested the new one with managers during mid-year reviews, incorporated feedback, and explained the rationale in multiple forums.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are practitioner accounts and do not prove that the proposed models improve promotion equity, compensation fairness, retention, hiring validity, delivery outcomes, or manager calibration. More subjective criteria can preserve nuance, but they can also increase inconsistency unless managers share examples, discuss rationale, and validate decisions carefully. NerdWallet acknowledges that discrete requirements are useful in large certification contexts such as aviation or professional credentials, so its critique is aimed at smaller, fast-changing company career systems rather than all standardized evaluation. Terrible Software's single-core-skill thesis is best treated as a corrective: ambiguity reduction is valuable evidence, but senior performance also depends on technical depth, communication, execution, mentoring, ownership, and the organization's actual scope expectations.

## What Changed
- Added behavior on ambiguous work as evidence of problem framing, prioritization, and risk judgment.
- Qualified the single-core-skill thesis against the need for multidimensional, organization-specific leveling evidence.
