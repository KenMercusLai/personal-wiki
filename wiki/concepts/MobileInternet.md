## Definition
[[MobileInternet]] is the view that smartphone access is not a lesser subset of the internet, but the internet's primary mass-market form.

## Current Synthesis
The sources argue that the distinction between "mobile internet" and "desktop internet" had become misleading. Smartphones are often used while seated near other computers, and their cameras, sensors, location, notifications, app stores, and always-present identity can make them richer internet platforms than PCs. By 2017 Evans extended that claim from platform capability to participation: billions of people used or would use a phone as their first individually owned computer, performing mainstream PC activities and forms of creation without mastering older PC abstractions. In that framing, the PC can look like the limited internet client rather than the default.

## Key Claims
- "Mobile internet" should not be treated as a reduced version of desktop internet.
- Smartphone use is not defined by walking-around behavior; phones are frequently used in stationary contexts.
- Smartphones add capabilities that desktop browsers did not make central.
- The desktop web's browser-mouse-keyboard model is only one historical internet runtime.
- Smartphone distribution and usable interfaces bring first-time computer access to people beyond the shared-PC population.

## Evidence
- Category critique: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] compares "mobile internet" versus "desktop internet" to an obsolete distinction like color TV versus black-and-white TV.
- Use-context critique: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] says smartphones are often used while sitting down, not only while mobile.
- Capability claim: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] argues smartphones can be more sophisticated internet platforms than PCs.
- Runtime history: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] says the old internet was a web browser plus mouse and keyboard for most users.
- First-computer scale: [[creation-and-consumption-benedict-evans]] argues that billions of people would use a smartphone as their first computer of any kind.
- Participation: [[creation-and-consumption-benedict-evans]] says phones make writing, photography, video, sharing, communication, and app capabilities usable without legacy PC workflows.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources push against desktop-default assumptions but use historical and approximate adoption figures. They do not fully quantify which tasks remain better on PCs, which disability and accessibility needs complicate phone-first design, how connectivity and charging costs constrain access, or how later tablets, foldables, web apps, and cloud desktops changed the boundary.

## What Changed
- Added the smartphone as a first computer and usable-creation platform for people beyond the shared-PC population.
