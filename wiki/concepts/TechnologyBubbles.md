## Definition
[[TechnologyBubbles]] are speculative phases around emerging technologies in which abundant capital can accelerate experiments and infrastructure while prices, narratives, metrics, leverage, and fraud become increasingly detached from durable use.

## Current Synthesis
[[AlbertWenger]] treats a bubble as neither proof that a technology is worthless nor evidence that every project deserves funding. His dot-com-to-cryptocurrency comparison separates a potentially productive system effect from dangerous participant behavior: capital can build shared infrastructure and fund many experiments, but the boom's internal logic can suppress outside comparison, reward [[VanityMetrics]], and make leverage or scams look normal. [[FredWilson]] adds the household balance-sheet consequence: a family can be correct about a technological wave, accumulate substantial wealth inside it, and still lose nearly everything when exposure remains concentrated.

The practical objective is durable participation rather than perfect timing. Participants should look for actual end-user adoption, avoid explicit and contract-embedded borrowing, preserve liquidity, realize and reallocate some gains during the ascent, diversify beyond the favored sector, and keep enough distance to reassess claims against the wider world. Recognizing a bubble does not reveal its end, while waiting to sell after the break can fail through denial, continuing losses, illiquidity, or bankruptcy.

## Key Claims
- Technology bubbles can finance useful experiments and infrastructure even when much of the accompanying valuation is speculative.
- A bubble's social and comparative environment can replace outside judgment with self-reinforcing internal logic.
- End-user adoption is stronger progress evidence than pageviews or cumulative registrations, though adoption still needs clearer outcome measures.
- Leverage converts uncertain timing and violent price movement into possible ruin, including when exposure is embedded in contracts rather than called borrowing.
- Liquidity, partial profit-taking, and cross-asset diversification can preserve household capital and post-crash participation when concentrated holdings fail.
- Productive experimentation and outright fraud can expand together, so long-term technological conviction requires skepticism rather than indiscriminate participation.
- Recognizing a bubble does not make its timing predictable or justify complete disengagement from an important underlying technology.

## Evidence
- Productive-capital claim: [[continuations-by-albert-wenger-some-lessons-i-learned-from-the-dotcom-bubble-for]] says technology waves commonly include financial bubbles that finance experimentation and physical infrastructure.
- Judgment and measurement: [[continuations-by-albert-wenger-some-lessons-i-learned-from-the-dotcom-bubble-for]] describes a self-contained bubble logic and contrasts dot-com pageviews or cumulative users with actual end-user adoption.
- Survivability rules: [[continuations-by-albert-wenger-some-lessons-i-learned-from-the-dotcom-bubble-for]] warns against borrowing, short or leveraged contracts, and recommends dry powder, segregated funds, and taking some gains during the rise.
- Participation boundary: [[continuations-by-albert-wenger-some-lessons-i-learned-from-the-dotcom-bubble-for]] combines scam awareness with the view that uncertain timing should not prevent resilient long-term involvement in decentralized systems.
- Household consequence: [[diversification-aka-how-to-survive-a-crash-avc]] says Wilson's family lost roughly 90% of its net worth after concentrating Internet-boom wealth in venture capital and Internet stocks, while earlier real-estate purchases preserved the remainder.
- Timing and allocation: [[diversification-aka-how-to-survive-a-crash-avc]] argues that investors should realize gains and diversify before a crash because the instinct to wait for recovery and holdings that go to zero can defeat reactive selling.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept rests on two short 2017 investor essays drawing lessons from the dot-com era for an anticipated cryptocurrency bubble. They do not quantify which bubbles produced net social value, how much capital reached useful infrastructure, how adoption should be measured, or whether the proposed guardrails improved outcomes. Wilson's account is recollection rather than audited portfolio evidence, and his allocation figures are personal judgments. Both essays predate later cryptocurrency cycles, failures, regulation, and infrastructure outcomes; their predictions therefore remain historically situated rather than current market analysis. A technology's importance does not imply that any particular token, project, valuation, custody arrangement, or investment is sound, and diversification does not eliminate market-wide loss.

## What Changed
- Added Wilson's household-loss case as evidence that sector conviction and wealth creation do not ensure crash survivability.
- Added cross-asset diversification before the break as a complement to liquidity, leverage avoidance, and partial profit-taking.
