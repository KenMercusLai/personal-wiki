## Definition
[[DesignSkillAxes]] are the product-design skill dimensions that the source uses to assess growth: product thinking, interaction design, visual design, intentionality, drive, and self-awareness.

## Current Synthesis
The sources present design axes as the building blocks beneath a [[ProductDesignCareerLadder]]. They combine hard skills, such as designing interfaces, prototypes, systems, layout, type, color, and motion, with softer skills, such as communication, collaboration, leadership, feedback, community contribution, and self-awareness. The important practical move is not to treat all nineteen subskills as a checklist. Designers have uneven profiles, and a career plan should focus on a small number of high-impact weaknesses rather than trying to become exceptional at everything at once.

Taylor makes the business-understanding component of product thinking more operational. It is not only knowledge about a market or a rubric score: it includes learning from sales, fulfillment, company projections, economics, financing, business models, management, competition, and executive priorities. That context helps a designer decide where craft creates material value and where more polish is excessive. The sources therefore support a balanced profile in which business fluency guides craft rather than displacing it.

## Key Claims
- Product-design skills include both hard skills and soft skills.
- The six axes are product thinking, interaction design, visual design, intentionality, drive, and self-awareness.
- Product thinking combines user understanding, business understanding, and strategy, with business understanding developed through cross-functional operating context.
- Interaction and visual design cover patterns, systems, prototyping, layout, typography, iconography, color, and motion.
- Intentionality, drive, and self-awareness capture process, communication, collaboration, leadership, feedback, community, strengths, weaknesses, and growth.
- Skill profiles are uneven, so designers should choose focused growth goals rather than treating the axes as an exhaustive checklist.

## Evidence
- Axis definition: [[a-guide-to-becoming-a-senior-product-designer]] names six design axes and defines them as the shared skills across product-design levels.
- Hard and soft skills: [[a-guide-to-becoming-a-senior-product-designer]] distinguishes design techniques and tools from people skills, work ethic, emotional intelligence, and self-awareness.
- Product and interaction skills: [[a-guide-to-becoming-a-senior-product-designer]] breaks product thinking into user, business, and strategy, and interaction design into patterns, systems, and prototyping.
- Visual and interpersonal skills: [[a-guide-to-becoming-a-senior-product-designer]] breaks visual design into layout, typography, color, and motion while framing intentionality and drive around process, communication, collaboration, leadership, feedback, and community.
- Focused improvement: [[a-guide-to-becoming-a-senior-product-designer]] warns that designers cannot be strong in every skill and should focus on selected skills when building a plan.
- Business-understanding practice: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] recommends learning from sales, fulfillment, projections, economics, financing, management, competitive analysis, and executive problems.
- Craft-priority judgment: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] says business context helps designers distinguish where craft matters from where further polish is excessive, while explicitly retaining craft quality.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The career source calls the axes imperfect. Some design specialties, such as sound design or emerging-product skills for VR, wearables, IoT, or other new platforms, may not fit neatly. The framework is useful for planning but should evolve as the design industry and local company expectations change. Taylor’s business-literacy argument is a short practitioner essay without comparative evidence; company priorities can also conflict with user welfare, so business understanding should support informed challenge rather than automatic executive alignment.
