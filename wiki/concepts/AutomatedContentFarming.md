## Definition
[[AutomatedContentFarming]] is the practice of programmatically harvesting someone else's published material - usually news articles and their images - and re-publishing it at industrial volume through an automated pipeline that converts it into a more monetizable format on a distribution platform.

## Current Synthesis
The source describes the pattern in one concrete form: a script takes recent articles from BBC, ABC, Reuters, and similar outlets, has a synthetic voice read them over floating low-resolution images and a fixed intro, and uploads the result every few minutes to a [[YouTube]] channel of hundreds of such videos, with more channels running the same recipe. The defining characteristics are the removal of human judgment between source and publication, the discarding of attribution, permission, and payment, and the reliance on volume rather than quality: no single video has to succeed when production cost is near zero. The economics come from [[WebAdEconomics]] - the source's estimate of roughly $1 to $20 per 1,000 views, about $7 on average, implies meaningful revenue from a channel viewed more than 225,000 times in a month - so the operator captures advertising value generated by work someone else paid to produce. The source also names two structural amplifiers: mixed search results can favor video over the text that carried the original reporting, and platform enforcement is the weak point, since the abuse is industrial in scale and the platform is accused of doing almost nothing about it. Its forecast is that falling AI costs raise the ceiling on the pattern rather than removing it.

## Key Claims
- The defining feature is the removal of human judgment between source and publication, which lets one operator run many channels at once.
- Harvested content is re-published without attribution, permission, or payment, so value created by original reporting accrues to the operator and the platform instead of the producer.
- The business works on volume and near-zero marginal cost rather than on any individual successful item.
- Revenue is captured through the platform's own advertising system, which shares money with the channel that uploaded the stolen material.
- Platform presentation can amplify the practice, because video results are treated as a way to make search results look more diverse.
- Enforcement lags the abuse because the operation is automated, distributed across many channels, and cheap to restart.
- Cheaper and more capable AI is expected to increase the scale and quality of automated farming rather than reduce it.

## Evidence
- Automated pipeline: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] describes a channel whose videos share a fixed intro, a robotic voice-over, and floating low-quality images, with new ones generated from recent news every few minutes.
- Theft without credit: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] says the articles and images are taken from BBC, ABC, Reuters, and other outlets, and that the writers and photographers receive no mention.
- Volume economics: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] notes that hundreds of videos are published week after week and that most individual videos have few views, while the channel's monthly total still exceeds 225,000.
- Revenue capture: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] places ad earnings between $1 and $20 per 1,000 views with about $7 as the cited average, and estimates roughly $3,000 for the featured channel over about two months.
- Platform amplifier: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] argues that video, image, and other non-link results receive preferred treatment because they make search results look more diverse.
- Enforcement gap: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] says Google does almost nothing to stop the practice, that several further channels follow the same concept, and that the problem is framed as an unaddressed new form of fully automated plagiarism.
- AI escalation: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] predicts the practice will get out of hand as AI becomes smarter, faster, and more efficient.
- Ethical boundary: [[unethical-growth-hacks-youtube-news-bot-epidemic]] separates this practice from growth hacking it admires, calling it plain theft.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence is a single first-person account on one platform, with revenue derived from a CPM range the source itself calls debatable, so the dollar figures illustrate an incentive rather than measure it. The source covers one moment in a changing enforcement story: it names channels but follows none of them, so it cannot show whether the described inaction persisted, whether the videos stayed monetized, or whether copyright systems later caught up. It also treats format and automation as the abusive elements, while automated and synthetic publishing can be used for original work, licensed material, or disclosed machine-generated news, and attribution failures range from deliberate theft to sloppy aggregation. The general mechanism is therefore better supported than the specific scale of harm or the specific accusation against any one platform.

## What Changed
- Created the concept for programmatic harvesting and re-publishing of third-party content as a platform-monetized pipeline.
