## Definition
[[SubsidizedUnitEconomics]] is a startup or marketplace condition where apparent product-market traction depends on customers paying materially less than the full cost of delivering the product or service, with investors, suppliers, workers, or future price increases expected to absorb the gap.

## Current Synthesis
The sources use 2016-era on-demand services, and [[Uber]] in particular, to warn that growth can reflect subsidy-mediated pricing rather than sustainable transaction economics. [[GuyShachar]] supplies the broad early-stage diagnostic: convenience, fundraising, and volume do not repair a business when most incremental orders lose money, and founder optimism can conceal the gap behind assumptions about retention, virality, or future scale. [[HubertHoran]] supplies a later, more quantitative Uber case: private financial disclosures showed enormous losses through 2016, riders paying below cost, and the clearest margin improvement coming from reduced driver compensation rather than demonstrated operating efficiency.

The combined judgment is conditional rather than anti-subsidy. Marketplace convenience, trust, liquidity, and growth can be real while each transaction still requires investor funding, supplier underpayment, or later price increases. Temporary subsidy can support experimentation or market creation, but a credible strategy must identify the mechanism—pricing, acquisition, retention, utilization, or delivery cost—by which contribution economics will improve.

## Key Claims
- Fast growth can hide negative unit economics when prices are funded by investor capital rather than full customer willingness to pay.
- Transaction volume does not by itself repair a negative per-order contribution and can enlarge aggregate losses when no cost or revenue mechanism changes.
- Subsidized prices can weaken incumbents and create a perception of inevitability without proving sustainable efficiency.
- Margin improvement should be attributed carefully; transferring more cost to workers or suppliers is different from lowering the total cost to serve.
- Physical-service marketplaces may not inherit software-style scale economies when labor, vehicles, fuel, maintenance, insurance, or local regulation remain major costs.
- A credible dominance thesis needs evidence that the company can raise prices, lower costs, or improve quality without destroying demand or supplier participation.

## Evidence
- Volume warning: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] argues that 2016-era “Uber for X” companies could not assume large order volumes would automatically cure losses on most orders.
- Forecast optimism: [[dear-unit-economics-i-hate-you-by]] links weak unit-economics judgment to assumed indefinite retention, easy virality, and continued investor tolerance.
- Loss scale: [[can-uber-ever-deliver-part-one-understanding-ubers-bleak-operating-economics-naked-capitalism]] reports Exhibit 2's roughly $2.0 billion GAAP loss on $1.4 billion revenue for the year ending September 2015.
- Customer subsidy: [[can-uber-ever-deliver-part-one-understanding-ubers-bleak-operating-economics-naked-capitalism]] argues Uber passengers paid only about 41% of actual trip cost.
- Worker-side margin transfer: [[can-uber-ever-deliver-part-one-understanding-ubers-bleak-operating-economics-naked-capitalism]] says the 2016 EBITAR-margin improvement follows driver share falling from about 83% to 77%, with a counterfactual 83% driver share producing a worse margin.
- Physical cost structure: [[can-uber-ever-deliver-part-one-understanding-ubers-bleak-operating-economics-naked-capitalism]] argues drivers, vehicles, and fuel dominate urban car-service costs, so ride-hailing does not scale like digital distribution.
- Driver economics: [[can-uber-ever-deliver-part-one-understanding-ubers-bleak-operating-economics-naked-capitalism]] includes comments from drivers discussing depreciation, deadhead miles, surge dependence, and guarantees as practical limits on take-home economics.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Shachar's essay is a broad practitioner argument without company-level CAC, LTV, contribution-margin, retention, or cohort evidence; it does not distinguish temporary market-building subsidy from a structurally loss-making model. Horan's critical financial analysis relies on leaked or privately circulated figures rather than audited public-company reporting and predates Uber's later IPO, segment reporting, post-2016 pricing, delivery expansion, and cost restructuring. The sources therefore illuminate the 2012-2016 on-demand funding thesis and a reusable diagnostic pattern, not the complete later economics of Uber or every named service. Subsidies can be rational during experimentation or market creation when they reveal demand, but they need an explicit, testable path to sustainable contribution.

## What Changed
- Added the volume fallacy: scaling more loss-making orders does not improve economics unless an underlying revenue or cost driver changes.
- Added founder optimism about retention and virality as a source of hidden subsidy dependence.
