## Definition
[[UserBehaviorDrivenProductDiscovery]] is product discovery that starts from what users are actually doing with a product, especially when that behavior differs from the founder's original framing.

## Current Synthesis
The sources show discovery shifting from the founder's intended use case to behavior users were already demonstrating. [[DavidLieb]] says calling the top 100 [[Bump]] users revealed photo sharing, redirecting the team toward [[Flock]], Photoroll, and eventually [[GooglePhotos]]-related work. [[ClassPass]] adds a marketplace version: repeated Passport accounts looked fraudulent at first but revealed that variety, not one-time studio sampling, was the deeper value. [[JustinTV]] adds a slower segment-level case: gaming broadcasters remained a small but consistent group for years before the team recognized their strategic importance. [[MichaelSeibel]] also supplies the limiting rule: unexpected behavior merits investigation, but only a recurring group with workable economics and growth potential should redirect the company. The pattern therefore complements customer-led development without making all behavior equally authoritative.

## Key Claims
- Heavy users can reveal a product's real use case more clearly than the initial pitch.
- Direct calls can uncover behavior that analytics or founder intuition may not explain.
- Product pivots are stronger when they respond to observed use rather than abstract opportunity.
- A failed follow-on product can still preserve insight for later products.
- Workarounds and policy violations can contain product insight when they reveal motivation rather than mere misuse.
- Repeated behavior is more decision-relevant when it forms a coherent segment with compatible economics.

## Evidence
- Top-user calls: [[bump-10-years-of-lessons-learned]] says Lieb and Jake called Bump's top 100 users to learn why they used it.
- Behavior discovery: [[bump-10-years-of-lessons-learned]] says those calls revealed that users were sharing photos.
- Pivot attempt: [[bump-10-years-of-lessons-learned]] says the team showed Paul Graham the first version of [[Flock]], a semi-automated photo-sharing product.
- Later reuse: [[bump-10-years-of-lessons-learned]] says Photoroll was never released, but many ideas later returned in [[GooglePhotos]].
- Marketplace workaround: [[classpass-founder-on-how-marketplace-startups-can-achieve-product-market-fit-first-round-review]] says Passport users created new email addresses to keep accessing class variety.
- Subscription pivot: [[classpass-founder-on-how-marketplace-startups-can-achieve-product-market-fit-first-round-review]] says that behavior pushed [[ClassPass]] toward a subscription model where variety could work for customers and studios.
- Persistent adjacent behavior: [[users-you-dont-want]] says Justin.tv's gaming broadcasters were a small but consistent group whose importance took four to five years to recognize.
- Economic fit: [[users-you-dont-want]] says serving gaming streams did not materially change salary or bandwidth costs and created attractive advertising options.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Top users may not represent the broader market, and Bump's source itself shows that discovering real behavior did not guarantee immediate growth for Flock. Workarounds can also harm suppliers, trust, compliance, or economics if treated only as demand signals. Seibel's Justin.tv account is retrospective and offers no quantitative pivot threshold. Behavior-driven discovery still needs a product that gives first users a reason to start, a reason to repeat or spread it, and a model that works for the company and other marketplace participants.

## What Changed
- Added Justin.tv as a persistent-segment case where unexpected behavior became strategic only after cost and monetization fit were considered.
