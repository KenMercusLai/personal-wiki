## Definition
[[EngineeringManagerRoleDesign]] is the allocation of an engineering manager's technical work, people responsibilities, product ownership, decision rights, evaluation duties, and management span around a team's organizational topology.

## Current Synthesis
The source rejects a single answer to whether an engineering manager should code by separating three overlapping designs. A functional tech-lead manager leads engineers in one stack, retains deep technical authority, and supplies those engineers to feature work owned elsewhere. A cross-functional product-team manager owns engineering for one product area across several stacks, so coding gives way to architecture, coordination, staffing, and alignment with product, design, QA, and peer engineering managers. A people manager spans stacks and product areas while tech leads make more daily technical and cross-functional decisions.

The useful distinction is not the title but the coupled tradeoff among expertise, ownership, span, and accountability. Technical depth can strengthen recruiting, mentoring, and direct evaluation but also encourages micromanagement and dependence on one manager. Broader or people-focused roles can support larger or more integrated teams, but require distributed technical feedback, external mentorship, explicit seniority coverage, and clear responsibility boundaries. Role design should therefore specify what the manager owns, what technical leads own, where product ownership sits, and whose evidence informs performance evaluation.

## Key Claims
- Coding expectations should follow technical scope and team topology rather than the engineering-manager title alone.
- Functional tech-lead managers trade broad product ownership for deep stack expertise, direct mentoring, and continued technical contribution.
- Product-team managers trade stack depth for integrated ownership and coordination across technologies within one product area.
- People managers can support a larger span by delegating technical and cross-functional decisions, but ambiguous accountability becomes a central risk.
- Performance evaluation becomes more dependent on peer and cross-team evidence as a manager's technical breadth and organizational span increase.
- Separating people and technical leadership reduces combined-role dependency only when decision rights, feedback paths, and outcome accountability remain explicit.

## Evidence
- Shared baseline and variable focus: [[flavors-of-engineering-management-thinking-inside-a-large-box]] assigns recruiting, career development, and organizational context to all three forms while distinguishing their daily work.
- Functional depth: [[flavors-of-engineering-management-thinking-inside-a-large-box]] describes a roughly three-to-eight-person, single-stack team whose manager may code up to about half time and participate in major technical decisions.
- Product-area breadth: [[flavors-of-engineering-management-thinking-inside-a-large-box]] describes a roughly four-to-eight-person team spanning web, API, mobile, and other stacks under one engineering owner.
- People-management span: [[flavors-of-engineering-management-thinking-inside-a-large-box]] describes a roughly ten-to-fifteen-person span across stacks and product areas, supported by feature-team tech leads.
- Organization charts: [[flavors-of-engineering-management-thinking-inside-a-large-box]] visually distinguishes direct reporting from dotted cross-functional collaboration and shows where product managers, design, QA, and tech leads sit.
- Failure modes: [[flavors-of-engineering-management-thinking-inside-a-large-box]] identifies micromanagement, single-person dependency, weak specialist evaluation, experience imbalance, and unclear accountability.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The taxonomy is a 2018 practitioner model, not comparative organizational research. Team-size ranges and coding percentages are illustrative rather than validated thresholds, and real roles can combine the three forms or vary by management level. The author explicitly lacks firsthand experience with the people-manager model, making its benefits and risks more speculative than the other two. The article also does not measure hiring, delivery, retention, quality, or career outcomes, and it leaves unresolved how regulation, remote work, platform complexity, staff-plus engineering roles, or organization scale change the designs.

## What Changed
- Created a three-part model connecting manager behavior to reporting structure, product ownership, technical breadth, and delegated leadership.
- Made evaluation evidence and accountability explicit consequences of widening a manager's scope.
- Preserved the author's limited firsthand evidence for the people-manager form.
