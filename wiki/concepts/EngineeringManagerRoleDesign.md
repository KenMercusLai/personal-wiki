## Definition
[[EngineeringManagerRoleDesign]] is the allocation of an engineering manager's technical contribution, domain authority, people responsibilities, product ownership, decision rights, evaluation duties, and management span around a team's topology and execution model.

## Current Synthesis
There is no universal answer to whether an engineering manager should code. A functional tech-lead manager leads engineers in one stack and may retain substantial implementation and technical authority. A cross-functional product-team manager owns engineering across several stacks, shifting effort toward architecture, staffing, coordination, and product alignment. A people manager spans products or stacks while technical leads make more daily technical decisions.

Across those variants, the manager's purpose is to multiply group performance through context, trust, roadblock removal, conflict resolution, advocacy, staffing, and system improvement. Agent-assisted execution changes the allocation rather than erasing that purpose. Nakazawa argues that as implementation becomes cheaper and individual contributors gain leverage, managers need enough domain and technical capability to make changes, evaluate generated work, and lead technical direction. That claim is compatible with technically narrow manager roles but tensions a categorical separation between engineering and management.

The durable design rule is to make the coupling explicit. A role should state what the manager owns, what technical leads own, how current domain evidence reaches evaluation, how much direct implementation is expected, and whether the manager's technical activity enables the team or competes with it. Technical depth can improve judgment and mentorship while encouraging micromanagement or key-person dependency; delegated technical leadership can widen span while weakening accountability if feedback and decision rights are vague.

## Key Claims
- Coding expectations should follow team topology, technical breadth, risk, and delegated leadership rather than title alone.
- Functional tech-lead managers trade broad product scope for stack depth, direct technical judgment, and continued contribution.
- Product-team managers trade stack depth for integrated ownership and coordination across technologies in one product area.
- People managers can support larger spans through technical leads, but need explicit evidence paths and outcome accountability.
- Every variant should be evaluated as a multiplier of group performance rather than by the manager's personal output.
- Agent leverage can increase the value of current domain expertise and technical judgment even when the manager writes little production code.
- Technical participation helps only when it clarifies direction, evaluates risk, or removes constraints without crowding out people leadership or team ownership.

## Evidence
- Role taxonomy: [[flavors-of-engineering-management-thinking-inside-a-large-box]] distinguishes functional tech-lead, cross-functional product-team, and people-manager designs by scope, ownership, and span.
- Functional and product tradeoff: [[flavors-of-engineering-management-thinking-inside-a-large-box]] contrasts deep single-stack authority with broader product-area coordination across web, API, mobile, and other specialties.
- Delegation and evaluation: [[flavors-of-engineering-management-thinking-inside-a-large-box]] identifies specialist feedback, tech-lead decision rights, experience balance, and ambiguous accountability as central risks.
- Multiplier purpose: [[its-not-a-promotion-its-a-career-change-fractional-by-lindsay-holmwood]] assigns managers listening, trust, roadblock removal, conflict resolution, advocacy, strategic translation, and systemic improvement.
- Success criterion: [[its-not-a-promotion-its-a-career-change-fractional-by-lindsay-holmwood]] requires shifting satisfaction from individual engineering performance toward group performance.
- Agent-era technicality: [[modern-engineering-values]] argues that cheaper implementation and greater individual leverage require managers to retain domain expertise, technical leadership, and the ability to change and evaluate their systems.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
All three sources are practitioner arguments rather than comparative organizational studies. Team-size ranges and coding percentages are illustrative, and the agent-era claim is a forecast grounded in one expert's workflow rather than measured management outcomes. Holmwood's categorical separation of engineering from management usefully resists treating management as a reward for technical output, while Nakazawa may understate the time and expertise required for coaching, performance management, hiring, coordination, and organizational care. Direct technical contribution can keep a manager current, but it can also create micromanagement, bottlenecks, confused ownership, or neglect of people work. Real designs vary by level, regulation, product risk, remote structure, platform complexity, staff-plus coverage, and organizational maturity.

## What Changed
- Added agent leverage as a reason to preserve current domain and technical judgment in some manager roles.
- Reconciled technical participation with the multiplier model by judging whether it enables group performance.
- Made direct coding only one possible form of technical leadership rather than a universal requirement.
