## Definition
[[OnlineDisinhibitionEffect]] is the loosening of face-to-face social restraints in online communication, producing either benign disclosure and experimentation or toxic hostility and misconduct.

## Current Synthesis
In the source's account of [[JohnSuler]]'s 2004 framework, online disinhibition is not synonymous with anonymity. The retained mechanism list includes dissociative anonymity, invisibility, asynchronicity, solipsistic introjection, dissociative imagination, and minimized authority, then points to personality variables, competing ideas of a “true self,” cross-media self-constellations, and altered self-boundaries. These mechanisms can overlap but need not produce the same outcome.

The benign/toxic distinction is central. Reduced restraint can let people disclose emotions, fears, wishes, or identities that are difficult to express face to face; it can also lower inhibition against harassment, racism, misogyny, griefing, and other harmful conduct. The concept therefore explains a change in constraints and self-presentation rather than a deterministic moral transformation.

## Key Claims
- Online communication can relax restraints that ordinarily operate in face-to-face settings.
- Disinhibition can be benign, enabling candid disclosure, or toxic, enabling harmful behavior.
- Anonymity is one mechanism among several and should not be treated as the whole explanation.
- Visibility, timing, imagined social presence, authority cues, personality, and perceived boundaries can alter online conduct.
- The framework describes possible mechanisms but the supplied source does not compare their prevalence or causal strength.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The wiki currently has only Know Your Meme's summary and a retained screenshot of Suler's framework, not the complete 2004 paper or later empirical literature. The listed mechanisms are plausible distinctions, but this source does not measure them, show which dominate in particular settings, or establish that identity disclosure improves conduct. Its selected toxic examples may obscure benign anonymous participation, including support-seeking, whistleblowing, political speech, identity exploration, and disclosure under unequal power.
