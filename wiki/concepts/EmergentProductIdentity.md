## Definition
[[EmergentProductIdentity]] is the meaning and primary use a product acquires through repeated user behavior, creator practice, community norms, and competitive change, which may diverge from the identity intended by founders or management.

## Current Synthesis
Vine and LiveJournal show that a product's technically possible uses do not determine which identity becomes culturally or economically central. Vine's founders and owner favored artistic short video, but users made comedy, personality, and sports more prominent while competition removed its earlier general-purpose sharing role. LiveJournal's users turned an experimental journal script into a private, pseudonymous, highly configurable community service whose norms later constrained owners seeking a more commercial or public product. The strategic task is therefore not merely to observe emergent behavior: a company must decide whether to support it, test its economics, preserve its distinctive value, change systems around it, or deliberately reject it. Ignoring the gap can leave strong culture without coherent investment, monetization, governance, or trust; following it blindly can also preserve a beloved identity without a sustainable business.

## Key Claims
- Users and creators can establish a product's practical identity independently of management's preferred positioning.
- Competitive entry can eliminate an early use case and make a previously secondary behavior strategically central.
- Editorial selection, promotion, creator funding, and product investment communicate which identity the platform supports.
- Cultural traction does not automatically produce a viable platform business or justify every creator subsidy.
- Acquisition autonomy works only when the parent and acquired product still align on strategy, resources, and accountability.
- Abruptly closing a user-shaped platform converts strategic ambiguity into a trust and stewardship problem.
- The longer an emergent identity is reinforced by promises, volunteer authority, privacy norms, and personalized support, the more product change becomes a governance problem rather than a feature decision.

## Evidence
- User-shaped identity: [[vine-insiders-say-twitter-never-liked-what-vine-became]] says Vine shifted from early artistic experimentation toward scripted comedy, performance, personality, and sports.
- Competitive change: [[vine-insiders-say-twitter-never-liked-what-vine-became]] says Instagram's introduction of video displaced Vine's early role as general social video sharing.
- Support signals: [[vine-insiders-say-twitter-never-liked-what-vine-became]] reports reduced creator promotion, a cancelled original series, and a rejected top-creator production proposal.
- Business-model gap: [[vine-insiders-say-twitter-never-liked-what-vine-became]] says popular creators earned sponsorship income while Twitter did not successfully monetize Vine.
- Acquisition alignment: [[vine-insiders-say-twitter-never-liked-what-vine-became]] describes Vine's independence under Twitter as neither faster-growing nor effectively monetized.
- Stewardship: [[vine-insiders-say-twitter-never-liked-what-vine-became]] connects the abrupt shutdown announcement to user-content and public-relations criticism.
- Community-shaped identity: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] says LiveJournal's users and volunteers shaped features, support expectations, policy, privacy practice, and resistance to later commercialization.
- Adaptation tension: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] reports that LiveJournal anticipated later social features but hesitated to adopt a cohesive public-feed model because of community backlash and an unclear product story.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Neither case proves that management should always follow the most visible emergent use. Popular behavior can be expensive, unsafe, strategically narrow, difficult to monetize, or incompatible with a needed transition; Vine's reported creator proposal itself may have been uneconomic. Conversely, copying a mass-market competitor can destroy the privacy, intimacy, or subcultural value that made a platform distinctive. Both sources are retrospective journalistic accounts relying heavily on insiders, and neither compares counterfactual strategies or establishes which intervention could have made its platform durable.

## What Changed
- Added LiveJournal as a case where community-created identity became embedded in privacy norms, volunteer authority, support expectations, and resistance to commercialization.
- Distinguished the obligation to understand an emergent identity from either automatically following it or replacing it with a mass-market model.
