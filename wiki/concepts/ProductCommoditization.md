## Definition
[[ProductCommoditization]] is the pressure that makes once-distinct products easier to copy, cheaper to build, and harder to defend through features alone.

## Current Synthesis
The Groove source argues that most startup products either already are commodities or will become commodities as building software gets cheaper and easier. Startups face copying from below by newer entrants and from above by larger companies with more cash. The practical response is not despair about product value; it is to build the business assets that are harder to copy, especially brand and culture.

## Key Claims
- Falling build cost makes feature-level product advantages less durable over time.
- Validated products invite copying from startups below and larger companies above.
- Sophisticated technology or defensible IP can slow commoditization but rarely eliminate it forever.
- A healthy business is harder to build than a product, even when the product can be copied.
- Brand and culture are presented as more defensible than the product artifact alone.

## Evidence
- Build-cost pressure: [[7-no-nonsense-pieces-of-startup-advice-i-wish-i-got-when-i-started]] argues that cloud computing and many smaller technical advances make products easier and cheaper to build each year.
- Copying directions: [[7-no-nonsense-pieces-of-startup-advice-i-wish-i-got-when-i-started]] says successful products risk copying by startups and cash-rich incumbents.
- Defensible business layer: [[7-no-nonsense-pieces-of-startup-advice-i-wish-i-got-when-i-started]] says founders should defend brand and culture because building a healthy business remains hard.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source acknowledges exceptions for technically sophisticated products and defensible intellectual property, though it treats them as slower to commoditize rather than permanently safe. The claim is strategic advice, not a measured rate of commoditization across categories.
