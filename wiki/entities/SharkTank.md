## Overview
[[SharkTank]] is the television investment program represented in the source as both a failed financing channel and a high-reach customer-exposure event for the company that became [[Ring]].

## Current Profile
[[JamieSiminoff]] says a personal introduction to a producer helped DoorBot reach the program from a pool of roughly 40,000 applicants. He prepared for about a month and asked the judges for $700,000 in exchange for 10% of the eight-person company, but left without an investment. He nevertheless describes the appearance as essential to Ring's survival because it reportedly produced at least $5 million in sales during a cash crisis, financing engineering growth and another product.

The case separates the show's two possible functions: an on-air investor decision and mass-market distribution. It does not establish that rejection generally benefits companies, that the sales were incremental or durable, or that later company performance was caused by the broadcast.

## Key Characteristics
- Selective television venue combining startup pitches, investor judgment, and audience exposure.
- Rejected DoorBot's requested $700,000-for-10% transaction in the reported 2013 episode.
- Functioned as customer acquisition for DoorBot despite producing no on-air investment.
- Became a source of operating cash indirectly through reported sales rather than equity financing.

## Evidence
- Selection: [[jamie-siminoff-ring-dfjvc-medium]] says about 40,000 people applied in the same year and that a producer invited DoorBot after an introduction.
- Financing outcome: [[jamie-siminoff-ring-dfjvc-medium]] says the judges passed on the requested investment.
- Audience outcome: [[jamie-siminoff-ring-dfjvc-medium]] attributes at least $5 million in sales to the appearance.
- Operating consequence: [[jamie-siminoff-ring-dfjvc-medium]] says the resulting cash allowed the company to add engineers and develop its next product without an immediate round.

## Qualifications
All application, sales, attribution, and operating-impact claims come from one participant's retrospective account. The source provides no episode analytics, order records, baseline sales, refunds, margins, repeat purchase, customer cohorts, or comparison with similar companies. The judges' refusal and audience demand measure different judgments, so the episode alone does not show that either investors or customers were generally correct.
