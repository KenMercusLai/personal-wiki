## Overview
[[LiveJournal]] is an early blogging and social-network platform that combined personal journals, comments, communities, friend groups, privacy controls, profiles, and mobile publishing before those patterns became standard social-media features.

## Current Profile
The source presents LiveJournal as both a technical pioneer and a governance failure. It grew organically from [[BradFitzpatrick]]'s personal posting script into a service with more than ten million accounts, strong volunteer participation, and communities whose pseudonymous, relatively private structure enabled meaningful affinity and support networks. That same history created a difficult transition: personalized support, consultation promises, fine-grained controls, inconsistent policy, infrastructure strain, founder burnout, and distrust between power users and owners constrained commercialization and adaptation. [[SixApart]] acquired it in 2005, SUP Media in 2007, and the service later became primarily Russian-run while former staff built [[Dreamwidth]].

## Key Characteristics
- Pioneered a journal-centered social graph with comments, friends pages, communities, group privacy, profiles, moods, music, and mobile or audio posting.
- Grew through user behavior, volunteer labor, and rapid founder-led experimentation rather than an initially formal product strategy.
- Supported intimate, pseudonymous, and interest-based communities that differed from later real-identity public networks.
- Reached more than ten million accounts but struggled with infrastructure, management capacity, monetization, and policy consistency.
- Accumulated strong user expectations that made advertising, interface change, and moderation decisions unusually contentious.
- Passed from founder-owned Danga to Six Apart and then SUP Media before becoming primarily oriented toward Russian users.

## Evidence
- Product and growth: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] traces the service from a CGI script through organic dorm growth, one million users, and eventually more than ten million accounts.
- Community value: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] reports users finding socially essential support in affinity communities unavailable locally.
- Technical legacy: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] credits Fitzpatrick with creating memcached for LiveJournal in 2003 and describes an infrastructure team operating continually over capacity.
- Governance and business tension: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] connects volunteer policy-setting, personalized support, old promises, “Nipplegate,” advertising pressure, and distrust to resistance against change.
- Ownership and decline: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] records the 2005 Six Apart sale, 2007 SUP Media sale, 2009 U.S. layoffs, later Russian server move, and user migration to Dreamwidth.

## Qualifications
The evidence is a 2019 retrospective built largely from former employees and volunteers. It establishes that technical innovation, intense community attachment, governance conflict, commercialization pressure, and competitive change coexisted, but it cannot isolate their causal weights or show that copying later public-feed platforms would have preserved what users valued.

## What Changed
- Created a profile of LiveJournal as a technically advanced, community-intensive platform whose inherited governance and identity constrained later adaptation.

## Relationships
- [[BradFitzpatrick]] - founded LiveJournal and built its early product and infrastructure.
- [[SixApart]] - acquired LiveJournal in 2005 and attempted to make the service commercially sustainable.
- [[Dreamwidth]] - forked LiveJournal's code and community model after former contributors left.
- [[CommunityGovernanceDebt]] - LiveJournal's promises, precedents, and distrust narrowed later decision space.
- [[EmergentProductIdentity]] - user behavior and community norms defined the product beyond management's plans.
- [[PlatformAbuseResponse]] - policy disputes exposed the limits of rigid enforcement without contextual standards.
- [[Facebook]] - public-feed and real-identity competitor that changed social-network expectations.
- [[Twitter]] - later public network that popularized short status updates and features LiveJournal had previously offered.
