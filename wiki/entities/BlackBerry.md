## Overview
[[BlackBerry]] appears in the wiki as a pre-iPhone smartphone incumbent whose hardware and software platform collapsed after [[Apple]] and [[Google]] moved the market into the modern app-centered [[MobileEcosystem]], while still reporting strong growth for years after the strategic break.

## Current Profile
Together, the sources present BlackBerry as the clearest case of "seeing without seeing" and of financial results lagging strategic decline. Its leaders recognized that the [[IPhone]] represented a deeper software-platform leap, but BlackBerry stayed with its old platform for years before acquiring [[QNX]] in 2010. One source says unit sales peaked at 14.6 million in Q4 2010 and fell to 1.7 million three years later; the newer source's inspected chart shows annual revenue continuing upward from $3.0B in 2007 to a $19.9B peak in 2011 before collapsing to $2.16B in 2016. Strong enterprise contracts and reported growth therefore obscured the shift toward consumer touch devices and app ecosystems until the viable response window had narrowed. The earlier inspected device image underscores the legacy design center: a physical keyboard handset with BlackBerry services and a traditional app grid rather than a modern full-touch app ecosystem.

## Key Characteristics
- Legacy smartphone maker built around messaging, physical keyboards, and enterprise reputation.
- Recognized the iPhone as a serious software-platform threat but delayed replacing its own operating-system base.
- Acquired [[QNX]] too late to catch up with iOS and Android application ecosystems.
- Continued growing reported revenue for four years after the iPhone launch, making financial success a lagging signal of platform decline.
- Exited internal hardware development in 2016 and moved toward outsourced hardware plus software, security, and applications.
- Suffered a steep unit-sales collapse from a 2010 peak to low single-digit millions by 2013.

## Evidence
- Platform recognition and delay: [[blackberry-meditation-at-the-grave-jean-louis-gassee-medium]] says BlackBerry recognized the iPhone's software depth in 2007 but waited until April 2010 to buy [[QNX]]; [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] argues that enterprise success and the existing product portfolio blocked an adequate consumer-market response.
- Lagging financial signal: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] and its inspected chart show annual revenue rising from $3.037B in 2007 to $19.907B in 2011 before falling to $2.160B in 2016.
- Unit and financial collapse: [[blackberry-meditation-at-the-grave-jean-louis-gassee-medium]] says units fell from 14.6M in Q4 2010 to 1.7M three years later and reports a swing from profit on $1.2B revenue in early 2015 to a $1B loss on $734M revenue in the comparable 2016 period.
- Strategic exit: [[blackberry-meditation-at-the-grave-jean-louis-gassee-medium]] cites John Chen saying BlackBerry would end internal hardware development and outsource hardware to partners; it says the later Android move failed to revive demand.
- Lifecycle interpretation: [[why-you-should-kill-your-cash-cow-the-startup-medium]] uses BlackBerry as the central [[InnovatorsDilemma]] case in which the incumbent optimized a mature [[TechnologySCurve]] while a challenger accelerated on a new one.

## Qualifications
Both sources are interpretive essays and use BlackBerry primarily as a cautionary case. Calling the company strategically "dead" at the 2007 iPhone launch is hindsight-heavy and should not erase the real revenue growth, installed base, enterprise value, or uncertainty that followed. The sources do not cover BlackBerry's later software, cybersecurity, licensing, automotive, patent, or enterprise-service history, and their diagnoses emphasize platform timing, software architecture, and incumbent incentives more than carrier relationships, pricing, industrial design, regulation, or detailed internal decision evidence.

## What Changed
- Distinguished the 2007 platform break from the later peaks in units and revenue.
- Added reported financial growth as a lagging indicator that delayed response to the consumer and app-ecosystem transition.
- Qualified the retrospective claim that BlackBerry was already "dead" when the iPhone launched.

- [[IPhone]] - competitive shock that exposed BlackBerry's software deficit.
- [[MobileEcosystem]] - ecosystem context in which BlackBerry lost scale and developer relevance.
