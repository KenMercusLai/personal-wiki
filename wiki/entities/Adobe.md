## Overview
[[Adobe]] is presented as the productivity-software company moving products such as [[Photoshop]] from packaged software purchases to the subscription-only [[CreativeCloud]] model, and as a large successful company whose product leadership had to drive self-disruption across a major existing revenue stream.

## Current Profile
Adobe's subscription shift is framed as aggressive but rational. By ending packaged Creative Suite sales and moving customers to Creative Cloud, Adobe can smooth revenue, reduce piracy, lower entry cost for new users, earn over time from heavy professional users, and align maintenance incentives with churn reduction. Adobe is therefore a leading example of why productivity software may fit subscriptions better than one-time licenses.

Adobe's old Creative Suite model produced roughly $2B in annual license revenue and represented about half of company revenue, so the move to Creative Cloud was not just packaging; it required product leadership across finance, engineering, sales, legal, marketing, customers, and executives. [[LeaHickman]]'s role is framed as making a new product vision compelling enough that the company could disrupt itself before the market did.

## Key Characteristics
- Moved major creative software away from perpetual packaged purchases toward Creative Cloud subscriptions.
- Used the subscription model to smooth launch-driven revenue spikes.
- Expected subscriptions to reduce piracy and increase revenue continuity.
- Could better monetize professional users whose economic value from Adobe tools grows over long careers.
- Had to manage a high-risk transition because Creative Suite represented a large existing revenue base.
- Used product leadership, prototypes, and repeated communication to align many functions around Creative Cloud.

## Evidence
- Business-model shift: [[adobes-subscription-model-why-platform-owners-should-care-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] says Adobe announced its products would be available solely through Creative Cloud.
- Revenue smoothing: [[adobes-subscription-model-why-platform-owners-should-care-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] names smoother revenue streams as one reason for the move.
- Piracy reduction: [[adobes-subscription-model-why-platform-owners-should-care-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] says subscriptions would significantly reduce piracy, while noting possible unintended consequences.
- Professional-user monetization: [[adobes-subscription-model-why-platform-owners-should-care-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] uses the graphic-designer Photoshop example to show how Adobe can earn recurring revenue as user value compounds.
- Maintenance incentive: [[adobes-subscription-model-why-platform-owners-should-care-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] says Adobe is incentivized to maintain the app to reduce churn.
- Existing-revenue risk: [[behind-every-great-product-silicon-valley-product-group]] says Creative Suite produced about $2B of annual license revenue and roughly half of Adobe's overall revenue.
- Product-leadership campaign: [[behind-every-great-product-silicon-valley-product-group]] says Hickman used prototypes and continuous communication to align executives and product teams behind Creative Cloud.

## Qualifications
The Stratechery source is a 2013 interpretation of Adobe's subscription announcement. It includes an update correcting the initial assumption that Creative Cloud would allow unlimited device use; the article notes app use remained limited to two PCs and tablet apps still required separate payment. The SVPG source is a retrospective product-leadership story and does not independently measure all factors behind Adobe's transition.

## What Changed
- Added the Creative Cloud transition as a large-company product-leadership and self-disruption case.
- Created Adobe as the central company case for productivity software's move from packaged software to subscriptions.
