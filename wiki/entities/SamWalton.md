## Overview
[[SamWalton]] is presented as a retail operator whose 15 years of pre-Walmart experience expose the limits of overnight-success and idea-first founder narratives.

## Current Profile
Walton began operating a Ben Franklin franchise at 27, combined its playbook with traffic, discounting, and direct-sourcing experiments, and grew his first store into the leading franchise in a six-state region. A missing lease-renewal option then let a competitor-landlord force the store to close. The source presents his response—responsibility, relocation, rebuilding, continued experiments, and course correction—as the capability-building period that later accounts of [[Walmart]] tend to hide.

By 1960 Walton had 15 stores and was described as the largest independent variety-store operator in the United States. He nevertheless faced broad rejection when financing the first Walmart, borrowing most of the required capital against his family's home and other stores. In this account, his low-price retail model and accumulated operating judgment mattered more than polish or contemporary prestige.

## Key Characteristics
- Learned retail through franchising while running local pricing, traffic, and sourcing experiments.
- Accepted responsibility for a consequential lease error and rebuilt after losing a successful store.
- Practiced [[AdaptivePersistence]] through daily improvement and rapid direction changes when wrong.
- Accumulated 15 years of operating experience before opening the first Walmart.
- Prioritized low customer prices over store polish and outside approval.
- Accepted unusually high personal and business financing risk after investor rejection.

## Evidence
- First-store performance: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] says Walton grew sales 3.5 times to $250,000 per year and became the top Ben Franklin franchisee in his region.
- Lease failure and recovery: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] says a missing renewal option cost him the store and forced his family to relocate before he rebuilt.
- Long apprenticeship: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] says Walton operated retail stores for 15 years before the first Walmart.
- Correction behavior: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] quotes a coworker describing Walton as unusually unafraid of being wrong and willing to change direction.
- Financing: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] says investors largely declined and Walton borrowed 95% of the first Walmart's capital.
- Low-price positioning: [[why-you-should-ignore-every-founders-story-about-how-they-started-their-company-trevor-mckendrick]] describes the first store's basic fixtures and consistently lower prices; its retained grand-opening advertisement promises customer savings.

## Qualifications
This profile relies on McKendrick's selected retelling, which in turn quotes Walton and an unnamed coworker. It does not independently audit the figures, Walton's full business history, the contributions of family and employees, or Walmart's later labor, supplier, community, and competitive effects. The case also has survivorship bias and should not make Walton's financing risk a general recommendation.
