## Overview
[[AOL]] is presented as a proprietary online service that simplified early consumer internet access, built lock-in through member-only mail and chat, and later exposed users to the open web through a browser that helped make its own access bundle less necessary.

## Current Profile
Siegler uses AOL as the historical model for a controlled platform's adoption and displacement cycle. A familiar interface, structured content, communication tools, and perceived safety helped newcomers get online despite access fees and member-only boundaries. Flat-rate pricing then accelerated usage, and the company became powerful enough to buy Time Warner. Yet AOL's browser opened a path to a rapidly expanding web, while broadband providers let users reach that web directly. In this account, email remained a switching cost, but it could not preserve a dial-up and walled-garden bundle whose central function had been unbundled.

## Key Characteristics
- Lowered the practical learning barrier for early mainstream internet users.
- Combined paid access, structured content, mail, and member-only chat in a controlled environment.
- Used an understandable interface and perceived safety as adoption advantages.
- Built communication-based switching costs through AOL email and chat.
- Added a browser that let users access value outside the proprietary service.
- Lost relevance as broadband and the expanding open web separated connectivity from AOL's bundle.

## Evidence
- Adoption interface: [[facebook-the-app-store-and-the-sound-of-inevitability-techcrunch]] credits AOL's understandable interface and relatively safe environment with attracting nontechnical users.
- Controlled communication: [[facebook-the-app-store-and-the-sound-of-inevitability-techcrunch]] describes chat and mail primarily among AOL members as both utility and lock-in.
- Scale and power: [[facebook-the-app-store-and-the-sound-of-inevitability-techcrunch]] says flat-rate pricing drove usage into the tens of millions before AOL bought Time Warner for $164 billion.
- Open window: [[facebook-the-app-store-and-the-sound-of-inevitability-techcrunch]] argues that AOL's browser exposed members to a larger and more diverse web.
- Unbundling: [[facebook-the-app-store-and-the-sound-of-inevitability-techcrunch]] connects always-on telephone and cable access with falling need for AOL's dial-up bundle.

## Qualifications
This profile is derived from one 2010 strategic essay, not a comprehensive corporate history. It compresses AOL's growth and decline into an open-versus-closed platform analogy and does not isolate the roles of pricing, broadband competition, the Time Warner merger, management, advertising, or other market changes. The source's $164 billion transaction figure and causal sequence are reported without primary documentation.
