## Overview
[[BerkshireHathaway]] is represented as a capital-allocation company that moves cash from high-return businesses with limited reinvestment needs into larger productive assets, and as the reported $10 billion investor in Alphabet's 2026 equity package.

## Current Profile
[[the-google-capital-company]] uses Berkshire to connect two business types. See's Candies reportedly required little incremental capital while sending decades of profit to Berkshire; BNSF Railway consumed billions in annual capital expenditure but produced more absolute annual income than See's had produced cumulatively. Berkshire's function was to move surplus cash from the first kind of business into the second rather than judge both only by percentage returns on invested capital.

The source then reverses the analogy. With a reported $373 billion of cash at the end of the preceding quarter and $25 billion of 2025 free cash flow, Berkshire itself becomes the cash generator seeking opportunities large enough to absorb capital. Its reported $10 billion Alphabet investment is interpreted as an endorsement of Google's AI-compute demand thesis and a way to place Berkshire's cash behind a company capable of funding infrastructure at exceptional scale. The position is small relative to both companies, so Thompson also treats it as a signal; the article does not establish Berkshire's actual rationale or investment terms.

## Key Characteristics
- Allocates cash across businesses with very different capital intensity and reinvestment capacity.
- Uses high-return cash generators to fund acquisitions and productive assets rather than requiring each subsidiary to reinvest locally.
- Owns capital-intensive businesses whose absolute profits can exceed those of higher-return but smaller operations.
- Had exceptionally large cash holdings and free cash flow in the source's 2026 snapshot.
- Reportedly invested $10 billion in Alphabet as part of its equity financing package.

## Evidence
- Cash-generator model: [[the-google-capital-company]] reports that See's required only $32 million of additional operating capital after acquisition while sending most cumulative profits to Berkshire for redeployment.
- Productive-asset model: [[the-google-capital-company]] contrasts See's with BNSF's reported $3.8 billion annual capital consumption and $5.5 billion net income.
- Deployment capacity: [[the-google-capital-company]] reports Berkshire holding $373 billion of cash at the end of the preceding quarter and producing $25 billion of free cash flow in 2025.
- Alphabet transaction: [[the-google-capital-company]] reports a $10 billion Berkshire investment inside Alphabet's wider equity package and interprets it as both capital deployment and signaling.

## Qualifications
This profile rests on one strategic essay, quoted shareholder letters, and article-supplied financial figures. It does not cover Berkshire's full portfolio, insurance float, governance, valuation process, tax position, transaction terms, or audited current accounts. The analogy compresses meaningful differences between candy, railways, cloud infrastructure, and AI accelerators.

The article asks whether CEO Greg Abel is replaying Buffett's capital-allocation strategy, but supplies no direct statement of Berkshire's rationale and says Buffett no longer makes investment decisions. The $10 billion position is described as small relative to both companies, so it cannot by itself establish Berkshire's conviction about AI demand, Alphabet's valuation, or future investment scale.
