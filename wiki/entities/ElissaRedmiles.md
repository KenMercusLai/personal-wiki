## Overview
[[ElissaRedmiles]] is presented as a security researcher whose work examines how socioeconomic status, skill, and advice access affect privacy and security behavior.

## Current Profile
The source uses Redmiles to explain why privacy and security advice is not equally usable for everyone. Her research suggests that protections such as two-factor authentication can impose higher practical costs on low-SES and low-skill users, making supposedly rational security behavior harder for the least-resourced people. She also finds that wealthier, higher-skill, and workplace-trained users tend to rely on more authoritative advice sources, while lower-income and less-educated users rely more on friends and family.

## Key Characteristics
- Studies the usability and socioeconomic cost of security protections.
- Shows that low-SES and low-skill users may face higher costs when adopting tools such as two-factor authentication.
- Treats security behavior as constrained by time, skill, training, and advice networks.
- Finds inequality in access to authoritative security advice.
- Supports the article's claim that individual privacy self-defense can reproduce class inequality.

## Evidence
- Security-cost finding: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] cites Redmiles's work on rational decisions around two-factor authentication for a fictional bank account.
- Low-resource burden: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] quotes Redmiles saying security and privacy can be too time-intensive for the least-resourced users.
- Advice-source inequality: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] reports that higher-income, higher-skill, and workplace-trained users rely more on IT professionals and high-quality websites.
- Informal advice reliance: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] reports that lower-income and less-educated internet users rely more on friends and family.
