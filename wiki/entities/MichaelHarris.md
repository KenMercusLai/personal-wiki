## Overview
[[MichaelHarris]] is an author represented in the wiki by a first-person essay about digital media, attention, and the erosion of his capacity for sustained book reading.

## Current Profile
Harris writes as both cultural critic and case subject. He contrasts an earlier experience of books as immersive refuge with a later habit of scanning for facts, links, novelty, and usefulness, then follows that change into his own writing choices. His contribution is strongest as reflective diagnosis: media habits can change after childhood, reading quality is distinct from reading quantity, and creators may reproduce the qualities of what they consume. The source does not establish how typical his experience is or demonstrate a singular neurological cause.

## Key Characteristics
- Uses first-person experience to examine changes in reading, attention, and creative practice.
- Distinguishes sustained literary absorption from instrumental extraction of useful or shareable fragments.
- Treats reading habits as plastic and trainable rather than permanently fixed by childhood.
- Connects input quality with the form and ambition of later creative output.
- Proposes patient, slow, non-instrumental book reading as a practice for recovering attention.

## Evidence
- First-person diagnosis: [[i-have-forgotten-how-to-read-the-globe-and-mail]] recounts Harris's inability to complete a chapter despite his identity and work as an author.
- Media comparison: [[i-have-forgotten-how-to-read-the-globe-and-mail]] contrasts immersion in printed books with screen reading organized around links, facts, comments, and novelty.
- Creator feedback loop: [[i-have-forgotten-how-to-read-the-globe-and-mail]] describes his own prose becoming shorter, more provocative, and easier to consume as his reading became more instrumental.
- Proposed recovery: [[i-have-forgotten-how-to-read-the-globe-and-mail]] recommends changing the reading diet and practicing patient book reading without demanding immediate utility.

## Qualifications
The wiki has one essay by Harris, published in 2018. It combines introspection, anecdotes, historical interpretation, and brief references to researchers and public figures; it does not present study methods, direct measurements, representative sampling, or a controlled comparison of print and screen reading. His experience should not be generalized into a clinical diagnosis or a universal account of digital-media effects.
