## Overview
[[BenThompson]] is the [[Stratechery]] author represented in the wiki through analyses of software business models, platform incentives, mature-company strategy, consumer-market theory limits, [[AggregationTheory]], operational unbundling, capability stacks, agent-mediated computing, and the economic incentives surrounding frontier AI policy.

## Current Profile
The Adobe source presents Thompson as analyzing [[Adobe]]'s shift to [[CreativeCloud]] through the economics of packaged software, SaaS, productivity apps, and mobile platforms. His argument moves from user-level willingness to pay to producer incentives and then to platform-owner strategy: subscriptions can help app makers survive, but they can also help platform owners retain users who depend on indispensable apps. The Apple middle-age source shows the same incentive lens applied to a hardware company: Thompson argues that [[AppleMusic]] and Services should be understood as ways to differentiate and sell Apple devices, with [[HomePod]] as a case where service adoption bridges into hardware profit. The Apple social-network source adds a measurement lens: if Apple wants investors to value engagement and installed-base monetization, Thompson argues it should disclose active customers and revenue per user rather than only active devices and Services revenue.

Thompson applies a goal-strategy-tactics and infrastructure-economics lens to Amazon. He argues that ecommerce is a tactic inside a broader services model: Amazon uses itself—or an acquired operation such as [[WholeFoods]]—as the [[FirstAndBestCustomer]] for expensive infrastructure, decomposes the system into primitives, and sells it to outside customers. The essay is strongest as a model of Amazon's behavior; its specific grocery delivery and restaurant-supply claims are forecasts.

The data-factory essay applies the same incentive and system-boundary reasoning to Facebook and Google. Thompson separates demand aggregation from advertising sales, then adds the processing layer that combines user, content, advertiser, partner, and third-party inputs into recommendations, distribution, targeting, and inferred profiles. His regulatory proposal follows from that model: disclose the processed output rather than stopping at user-submitted raw data. The proposal remains a 2018 strategic argument and assumes more effective individual agency than the wiki's privacy-inequality evidence supports.

The Facebook monopoly essay applies surplus analysis to a free, multi-sided aggregator. Thompson argues that near-zero user-serving cost can hide market power if analysis stops at consumer price: publishers may receive less revenue, differentiated ad scarcity may raise prices, and Facebook can copy Snap's innovations into a stronger network. He distinguishes the incentive to create a new monopoly from an established monopolist's freedom to appropriate innovation, but the essay does not supply a legal market definition or measured counterfactual.

The faceless-publisher essay applies the same system-boundary and scale reasoning to media operations. Thompson argues that internet distribution atomized creators and audience-facing brands while pushing advertising sales, technology, subscriptions, support, and administration toward shared scale. The Ringer and Vox Media partnership supplies the case: creator ownership and editorial independence remain visible while reusable backend functions consolidate.

The transportation essay applies capability decomposition to a market in transition. Thompson separates drivers, cars, mapping, routing, and riders, then traces how their economics change from UberX to pooling, commuter sharing, and autonomous fleets. His 2016 competitive judgment resists reducing the market to self-driving technology: Google's lead in maps and autonomy had to be integrated with routing, capital deployment, approval, and customer demand, while Uber's marketplace advantage would weaken when drivers disappeared. The company ranking remains a forecast, but the five-component model extends his recurring practice of locating power at system boundaries rather than in a headline product alone.

The IBM essay adds a historical-analogy and organizational-readiness lens. Thompson separates the durable logic of Gerstner's customer-led integration model from its 1990s conditions, then asks whether Red Hat and OpenShift solve a real 2018 enterprise problem or mainly IBM's need for a position after missing public cloud. The argument ties strategy to capital investment, customer alternatives, competitive value-chain position, and culture rather than treating an acquisition announcement as execution.

The Tesla essay adds a theory-scope lens. Thompson agrees that Tesla is sustaining rather than classic disruptive innovation, then rejects the inference that low-end disruption is the only viable market-entry strategy. He uses Apple and Tesla to argue that consumer choice includes integration, identity, emotion, design, and [[BrandEquity]] that modular good-enough comparisons may miss. The argument is deliberately bounded by refundable reservations, external financing, and unresolved Model 3 execution.

The 2026 agent essay combines several of these recurring lenses. Thompson uses a first-person Muse recipe experiment to argue that agents equipped with computers can abstract task-specific apps, generate personal UI, and continue work asynchronously. He then extends Aggregation Theory from discovery to inspiration and execution, interpreting Muse and [[MicrosoftCopilot]] as bids to control the primary consumer or employee interface while downstream apps become suppliers. The essay is a forecast grounded in one useful but incomplete workflow and product announcements; it does not establish mass adoption or durable agent concentration.

The frontier-overhangs essay applies Thompson's incentive and modularity lenses directly to frontier AI governance. He groups capability adoption, product capture, pricing, capital returns, and cyber defense as gaps created by rapid model improvement, then argues that slower progress could help labs absorb four of them while worsening the defensive-safety gap. His strongest analytical move is separating concrete malicious use from speculative alignment risk; his most contestable move is inferring that Anthropic's pacing proposal is shaped by its position behind OpenAI. The article preserves one material correction: reported Anthropic profitability did include training costs.

“Write Things Down” joins Thompson's first-person adoption method with a philosophical boundary around AI. He connects [[DavidAllen]]'s trusted-capture model to assistants, Markdown-backed agent continuity, task boards, and deterministic bot harnesses, then distinguishes those operational gains from continuous model learning, persistent identity, volition, and moral agency. His incident analysis keeps responsibility with the people who specify goals and expose capabilities, while the final productivity coda insists that even excellent systems do not replace action.

The Vision Pro essay applies Thompson's first-person adoption method to medium design. He contrasts television's multi-camera solution to a flat screen with immersive video's ability to make one camera position feel like a physical seat. His proposed production rule—protect continuous presence and let the viewer choose a seat—extends his recurring system-boundary analysis: the device's differentiated value can be destroyed when the content layer imports conventions optimized for the previous medium. The argument is vivid but remains one enthusiast's experience rather than a viewer study or production-cost analysis.

## Key Characteristics
- Frames technology announcements and regulation through business-model incentives and system boundaries.
- Uses economic-surplus reasoning to connect software monetization with user value and platform strategy.
- Treats app-store subscriptions as a platform-retention tool while distinguishing services revenue from a company's underlying business model.
- Interprets Apple through vertical integration, installed-base leverage, and market maturity rather than through a simple services-pivot story.
- Separates goals, strategies, tactics, capability layers, and theory scope while testing playbooks against changed customers, interfaces, competitors, culture, and value-chain position.
- Uses first-person technology adoption as a concrete input while distinguishing demonstrated workflow utility, spatial presence, external continuity, and human agency from broader capability claims.
- Tests safety-policy arguments against commercialization incentives, competitive timing, and concrete offense-defense dynamics.

## Qualifications
This page covers Thompson only through fourteen Stratechery articles. It does not summarize his broader publishing history or full current views. The Amazon grocery-services, faceless-publisher, transportation-service, IBM hybrid-cloud, Model 3 customer-grace, agent-aggregation, frontier-overhang, and fixed-camera immersive-media theses are partly prospective and should not be read as verified later outcomes. The Muse, personal-assistant, and Vision Pro cases are first-person experiences and do not establish general reliability, affordability, comfort, preference, or transfer. The frontier essay provides no audited lab economics, capital limit, broad performance-surplus evidence, or proof of policy motive, while “Write Things Down” offers personal AGI, agency, and moral distinctions rather than validated technical tests. The data-factory proposal is not evidence that profile disclosure changed privacy outcomes or competition, and the monopoly, IBM, frontier, agent-incident, and immersive-production essays are strategy arguments rather than causal, legal, financial, security, safety, or audience studies.
