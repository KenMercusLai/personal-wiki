## Overview
[[MichaelSchulson]] is the author of a 2015 Aeon essay arguing that engineered digital compulsion should be treated as a product-design and regulatory problem rather than only as individual weakness.

## Current Profile
In the source, Schulson synthesizes behavioral psychology, gambling-machine research, Silicon Valley habit design, attention-funded economics, and ethical-design advocacy. His distinctive move is to preserve personal responsibility while relocating part of the causal and moral burden to firms that design repeated triggers and possess much more behavioral data than their users. He then sketches regulation intended to increase user agency rather than prohibit digital services.

## Key Characteristics
- Separates the internet as a useful network from particular products engineered for compulsive interaction.
- Connects variable rewards and habit cycles to attention-funded revenue rather than treating them as neutral interface techniques.
- Uses gambling research to frame compulsion as a designed human-machine interaction.
- Critiques user-only self-control advice and unequal access to voluntary digital-wellbeing tools.
- Proposes dashboards, stopping points, warnings, usage feedback, and user-set cutoffs as regulatory options.

## Evidence
- Causal framing: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] rejects both technological determinism and exclusive blame of users.
- Incentive analysis: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] links prolonged sessions and page transitions to advertising revenue.
- Policy stance: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] argues that regulation can expand practical choice by restoring user control.

## Qualifications
The profile is based on one 2015 popular essay. Its diagnosis and proposals are argumentative and interview-based, not a clinical study, causal product experiment, legal design, or policy evaluation, and should not be generalized to Schulson's complete work.
