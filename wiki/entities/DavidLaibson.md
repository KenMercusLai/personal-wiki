## Overview
[[DavidLaibson]] is a Harvard behavioral economist represented here through his explanation of retirement compounding, investing mistakes, and workplace-plan defaults.

## Current Profile
Laibson contrasts get-rich-quick stories and stock selection with a long sequence of early contributions to diversified, low-fee stock-and-bond funds. Because knowledge of this strategy does not ensure action, he emphasizes employer auto-enrollment, automatic contribution escalation, suitable default funds, and preservation of accumulated balances during job changes. The account joins compounding with institutional design: good outcomes depend on both time in the market and conditions that help people avoid postponement or premature withdrawal.

## Key Characteristics
- Applies behavioral economics to saving and investment decisions that people know are beneficial but often postpone.
- Rejects persistent stock-picking advantage for ordinary investors and most professional funds.
- Favors recurring early contributions, diversification, passive implementation, and low fees.
- Treats enrollment, escalation, fund selection, and rollover continuity as important behavioral infrastructure.

## Evidence
- Stock-selection critique: [[in-praise-of-incrementalism-ep-264-freakonomics-freakonomics]] says information about recognizable good and bad companies is generally already reflected in market prices and that fund outperformance has little persistence.
- Compounding sequence: [[in-praise-of-incrementalism-ep-264-freakonomics-freakonomics]] describes annual contributions beginning early, employer matches, diversified stock-and-bond funds, and avoidance of premature decumulation.
- Default design: [[in-praise-of-incrementalism-ep-264-freakonomics-freakonomics]] identifies auto-enrollment, auto-escalation, suitable default allocation, low fees, passive management, and rollover continuity as supports for sustained saving.

## Qualifications
This profile is limited to one edited 2016 popular interview and is not a comprehensive account of Laibson's research or current views. The retirement examples are general, U.S.-employer-centered, and not individualized financial advice; the episode does not supply underlying studies, effect sizes, tax context, or distributional analysis.
