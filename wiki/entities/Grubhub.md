## Overview
[[Grubhub]] is an online food-delivery company represented through both [[CaseyWinters]]'s retrospective examples of finding [[FeatureProductFit]] for its early mobile app and [[HClaireBrown]]'s investigation of restaurant-linked domains, microsites, phone numbers, and commissions.

## Current Profile
Grubhub's mobile development combined behavioral diagnosis, user research, incentives, richer decision information, and high-touch support. The team reportedly disabled inaccurate current-location behavior, enriched restaurant search results, offered $10 off a first mobile order after observing higher mobile lifetime value, and monitored social media so customer service could intervene quickly.

Brown's merchant-side investigation shows that the same discovery and attribution capabilities can redistribute control and margin. Grubhub reportedly registered more than 23,000 restaurant-linked domains, built generic microsites, used forwarding numbers to classify commission-bearing calls, and linked marketing spend to marketplace rank. These mechanisms could help restaurants gain orders, but they could also compete with independent sites and turn existing brand demand into platform-attributed demand. Grubhub denied cybersquatting and said its contracts authorized the discontinued domain service and allowed transfers on request.

## Key Characteristics
- Used conversion data to identify a location-accuracy problem in the mobile app.
- Added decision information such as cuisine, delivery time, minimum order, rating, and rating count to search results.
- Used a first-order discount to move web users into mobile ordering.
- Treated persistent lifetime value after the incentive as more meaningful than redemption alone.
- Used social-media monitoring and customer service as an adoption and recovery mechanism.
- Registered restaurant-linked domains and created microsites intended to generate orders and increase online presence.
- Used platform-controlled phone numbers and marketing attribution to determine commission-bearing demand.

## Evidence
- Behavioral diagnosis: [[feature-product-fit-casey-accidental]] says current-location users converted worse than address-entry users and attributes the gap to accuracy.
- Search decision support: [[feature-product-fit-casey-accidental]] says richer restaurant-result information improved conversion and retention despite making the page look denser.
- Incentive economics: [[feature-product-fit-casey-accidental]] reports that mobile users had twice the lifetime value of web users and that the difference persisted after a $10 first-order incentive.
- Support intervention: [[feature-product-fit-casey-accidental]] describes monitoring social media and having customer service respond quickly to mobile-app problems.
- Branded discovery control: [[grubhub-is-buying-up-thousands-of-restaurant-web-addresses-that-means-mom-and-pop-cant-own-their-slice-of-the-internet-new-food-economy]] reports more than 23,000 Grubhub domain registrations and examples of generic pages competing with restaurants' independent sites.
- Commission attribution: [[grubhub-is-buying-up-thousands-of-restaurant-web-addresses-that-means-mom-and-pop-cant-own-their-slice-of-the-internet-new-food-economy]] reports platform-controlled forwarding numbers, an executive estimate of about $30 million in annual phone commissions, and restaurant disputes over false order classification.
- Contractual defense and merchant variation: [[grubhub-is-buying-up-thousands-of-restaurant-web-addresses-that-means-mom-and-pop-cant-own-their-slice-of-the-internet-new-food-economy]] includes Grubhub's claim of contractual authority and transfer on request, plus one non-partner owner's view that added web presence might help.

## Qualifications
The product claims come from one former operator's retrospective without dates for each experiment, sample sizes, absolute conversion or retention figures, test designs, cost accounting, or independent verification. “First food delivery app” is the author's claim and is not established comparatively. The domain investigation is historical and based on a publication's dataset, selected restaurant accounts, examples, testimony, and company statements rather than a representative merchant study or legal finding. Permission is disputed; current domain status, search position, fees, and policies are not established, and merchant economics vary with delivery needs and direct-order capability.
