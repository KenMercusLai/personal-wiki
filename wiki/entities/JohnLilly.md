## Overview
[[JohnLilly]] is represented as a former Apple ATG employee, startup operator, CS183C instructor, and [[GreylockPartners]] investor whose reflections connect design, leadership ethics, stage transitions, organizational communication, founder evaluation, and the difference between operating and investing attention.

## Current Profile
The scaling synthesis uses Lilly to explain the move from OS1 to OS2 and from informal to explicit communication. He distinguishes product-building teams from the support functions that defend and enable them, says competition matters more once an opportunity becomes visible, and recommends a small set of repeated CEO messages as companies grow. His New York Times interview supplies the personal mechanism behind that rule: varying a message because he was bored with repetition led employees to form inconsistent views of his intent.

As an investor, Lilly separates context-free assessment of a founder's reasoning and behavior from contextual assessment of whether he can work productively with that person. He uses expanding questions to observe thought quality, listening, candor, aggression, and defensiveness, but says the investor should explain the questioning frame and admit that a probe may be wrong. He also contrasts the operator's concentrated focus with the venture capitalist's persistent attention to opportunities declined or missed.

His remembrance of [[SteveJobs]] adds an earlier Apple perspective. Lilly says Jobs made great human-centered design desirable and economically credible, inspired intense commitment during Apple's 1997 crisis, and acted rapidly to tighten confidentiality. He simultaneously describes Jobs's treatment of people as sometimes dehumanizing and personally destructive. The resulting leadership judgment is deliberately non-binary: outcomes and motivational force deserve recognition, but they neither erase harm nor make Jobs a personality to copy.

## Key Characteristics
- Differentiates product-building work from support and defense functions as companies scale.
- Emphasizes repeated simple messages for organizational alignment and conspicuous changes when priorities truly shift.
- Evaluates founders through both thought process and reciprocal working fit.
- Frames difficult questions explicitly so candid disagreement can remain productive.
- Contrasts operator concentration with investors' fear of missed opportunities.
- Treats human-centered design as both a cultural and economic source of value.
- Holds leadership achievement and motivational force apart from the human cost of demeaning behavior.

## Qualifications
The profile remains bounded to a scaling synthesis, a condensed interview, and one personal remembrance rather than Lilly's full Apple, Mozilla, startup, teaching, or investment record. The interview supplies self-reported practice without employee, founder, rejected-deal, or investment-outcome evidence. Challenging questions may reveal reasoning and interaction style, but interviewer behavior and power asymmetry can also produce the defensiveness being observed. The Jobs essay is retrospective: its 1997 details are recalled years later, the stock-price figure is explicitly uncertain, and claims about harmful treatment rely partly on friends' experiences rather than incidents Lilly says he observed directly.

## What Changed
- Added Lilly's Apple ATG background and first-person memory of Jobs's 1997 return.
- Added his judgment that design can create cultural, economic, and creative-enablement value.
- Added his explicit refusal to let extraordinary leadership outcomes erase interpersonal harm.
- Added self-directed contribution, rather than imitation of admired leaders, as his legacy principle.
