## Overview
[[GeoffreyHinton]] is presented as a neural-network researcher whose long commitment to connectionist methods links early Boltzmann-machine and backpropagation work with deep belief networks and the AlexNet breakthrough.

## Current Profile
The sources use Hinton's career as a narrative spine of deep learning's revival. They describe his persistence from 1970s doctoral work through periods when neural networks were academically marginal, credit him with important work on Boltzmann machines, multilayer error correction, and layer-wise pretraining, and follow his lab through AlexNet and the acquisition of DNNResearch by Google. The two 2016 magazine accounts add his experimental-psychology motivation, collaboration with Yann LeCun, influence on Microsoft's speech-recognition work, and leadership of the University of Toronto team associated with ImageNet's reported jump from 72% winning accuracy in 2010 to 85% in 2012. They treat that result as an opinion and reputation shift. This profile records his central role while avoiding the implication that any one researcher solely invented methods with broader lineages.

## Key Characteristics
- Pursued neural-network research during periods of strong skepticism toward connectionism.
- Preferred experience-driven neural learning to logic-first accounts of intelligence, drawing on an experimental-psychology background.
- Contributed to Boltzmann-machine research and the practical revival of multilayer neural-network training.
- Used deep belief networks and layer-wise pretraining to help make deep architectures trainable in the 2000s.
- Led the group associated with AlexNet's 2012 ImageNet result and later DNNResearch.
- Connected academic work to commercial speech and vision adoption through Microsoft, Google, and the post-ImageNet hiring race.

## Qualifications
This profile is derived from three secondary popular histories, not a complete biography or primary publication record. Their language can assign Hinton singular credit where backpropagation, convolutional networks, and other methods have longer, multi-author histories. The magazine features simplify benchmark metrics and the division of labor in the AlexNet team; the Economist excerpt calls Hinton the team leader but does not name the students or metric, while Fortune supplies names but still compresses the result. Claims about disciplinary consensus, acquisition bidding, causal links from a research visit to product improvement, and Hinton's forecasts should be checked against primary records before use as definitive history.
