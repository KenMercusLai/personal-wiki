## Overview
[[RodneyBrooks]] is represented in the wiki as a roboticist and essayist analyzing why self-driving-car adoption depends on social coordination, public legitimacy, and real-world perception rather than technical driving capability alone.

## Current Profile
In the source, Brooks studies Level 4 and Level 5 autonomy through ordinary street interactions in Cambridge, Massachusetts. He observes how pedestrians and drivers negotiate acknowledgement, right of way, patience, and local convention, then argues that vehicles unable to participate in those exchanges may be treated as either threatening outsiders or exploitable, traffic-blocking pushovers. He extends that social analysis to owners who can send empty vehicles to consume road and parking capacity, and to a public that may tolerate far fewer machine-caused deaths than human-caused deaths.

Brooks' practical position is skeptical about deployment timing but not about the eventual destination. He expects constrained operational domains, separated infrastructure, automated garages, and special-case services to precede broad Level 5 autonomy. In the article and comments, he also rejects stylized trolley problems when they assume perceptual distinctions that vehicles cannot reliably make, preferring attention to obstacle avoidance, edge cases, and measurable safety improvement.

## Key Characteristics
- Analyzes autonomous systems as participants in human social environments, not only as technical control systems.
- Uses close observation of pedestrian-driver interaction to surface tacit norms and signalling requirements.
- Distinguishes eventual technical inevitability from near-term deployment speed and public acceptance.
- Focuses on externalities created when individual convenience can consume shared road, parking, or institutional capacity.
- Prioritizes practical perception and aggregate safety improvement over hypothetical moral classification problems.

## Evidence
- Social interaction analysis: [[unexpected-consequences-of-self-driving-cars-rodney-brooks]] describes acknowledgement, tentative crossings, patience, turn-giving, and locally variable norms in Cambridge streets.
- Adoption forecast: [[unexpected-consequences-of-self-driving-cars-rodney-brooks]] predicts restricted Level 4 domains and a long delay before broad Level 5 autonomy.
- Commons analysis: [[unexpected-consequences-of-self-driving-cars-rodney-brooks]] gives circling, illegal waiting, advance parking, and school pickup as ways owners could externalize costs.
- Safety stance: [[unexpected-consequences-of-self-driving-cars-rodney-brooks]] argues that machine-caused deaths will face an unusually low social tolerance even if total deaths fall.
- Ethics dispute: [[unexpected-consequences-of-self-driving-cars-rodney-brooks]] records Brooks' extended exchange with commenters over whether trolley cases are operationally meaningful or useful proxies for everyday risk allocation.

## Qualifications
The profile is based on one 2017 essay and its comment thread, not Brooks' full robotics career or later autonomous-vehicle work. Many claims are forecasts grounded in observation and analogy rather than measured deployment evidence. The comment thread materially qualifies the essay by proposing external intention signals, shared fleets, and policy restrictions, and by disputing the claim that moral thought experiments are irrelevant.
