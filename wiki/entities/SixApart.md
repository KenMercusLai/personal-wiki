## Overview
[[SixApart]] was a blogging-software company associated with Movable Type and TypePad that acquired Danga and [[LiveJournal]] in 2005 before selling LiveJournal to SUP Media in 2007.

## Current Profile
The LiveJournal source presents Six Apart as an acquirer whose capital and professionalization initially raised staff optimism but whose business model and organizational experience did not fit an unusually vocal consumer community. It hired longtime volunteers and introduced project management and advertising pressure, yet inherited promises against ads, expectations of consultation and personalized responses, and policy conflicts such as “Nipplegate.” Distrust between decision-makers and power users remained unresolved before the platform was sold.

Dash's retrospective adds an earlier product-design role. The company's blogging tools helped popularize title-based post URLs, initially using underscores because they visually resembled spaces. The team later enabled hyphens after publishers feared that Google's stated preference would harm ranking. This makes Six Apart both a builder of early social-publishing conventions and a case of a smaller software company adapting its product to a dominant distribution intermediary.

## Key Characteristics
- Blogging-software company associated with Movable Type and TypePad.
- Helped establish readable, title-based URLs and initially represented spaces with underscores.
- Changed URL behavior under publisher pressure to conform to Google's stated preference for hyphens.
- Acquired LiveJournal and funded the hiring of experienced community volunteers.
- Brought commercial and project-management pressure to a platform shaped by informal founder and volunteer governance.
- Struggled to reconcile advertising needs with inherited promises and power-user expectations.
- Sold LiveJournal to SUP Media in 2007 after a two-year ownership period.

## Evidence
- Acquisition and initial optimism: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] says staff expected Six Apart to provide capital and a durable roadmap, and that volunteers became paid employees.
- Business-model conflict: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] describes management pushing for ads after users had been promised the site would not include them.
- Governance mismatch: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] says Six Apart was accustomed to selling to businesses rather than handling the policy intensity of a direct consumer community.
- Divestment: [[the-linux-of-social-media-how-livejournal-pioneered-then-lost-blogging-ars-technica]] records the 2007 sale to Russian company SUP Media.
- Publishing-tool design: [[underscores-optimization-arms-races-humane-tech-medium]] says Dash's team used underscores in title-based URLs because they looked like spaces.
- Search-platform pressure: [[underscores-optimization-arms-races-humane-tech-medium]] says the team eventually enabled hyphens because customers did not want to risk lower Google ranking.

## Qualifications
The LiveJournal account is told mainly through insiders and does not provide Six Apart executives' full rationale, financial data, alternative plans, or a controlled comparison with other acquisitions. Dash's URL account is likewise retrospective and does not measure the actual ranking effect of underscores or isolate Google's recommendation from competing product conventions. Together they support organizational-fit and platform-pressure mechanisms more strongly than simple claims of managerial incompetence or technical necessity.
