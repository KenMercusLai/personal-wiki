## Overview
[[IOS]] is Apple's mobile operating-system ecosystem, treated in the wiki as a strategic platform, a concrete interaction-design environment, and the ecosystem behind the [[AppStore]]'s 2015 revenue lead over [[GooglePlay]].

## Current Profile
iOS appears as both a strategic platform and a concrete design environment. Strategically, it is one pole of the ARM/iOS/Android mobile ecosystem that shifted investment and use away from the Wintel PC model; it gives [[Apple]] control over a large, economically valuable user base and over what other services, including [[Google]] services, can reach or do on Apple devices. The App Annie/VentureBeat source makes that economic concentration explicit: Apple's App Store generated 75% more revenue than Google Play in 2015 despite Google Play's much larger download volume. As an interface environment, iOS carries inherited conventions such as the top navigation bar, whose recognizability and implementation advantages can become physically strained as iPhones grow larger. Edge-swipe back, Reachability, sheets in Maps and Apple Music, and bottom-oriented app controls show iOS design adapting toward [[ThumbReachErgonomics]] and [[ReachNavigation]] rather than treating the original navbar pattern as permanent.

## Key Characteristics
- Anchors Apple's side of the mobile platform victory.
- Represents a high-value user and app-store-revenue ecosystem in the source's framing.
- Gives Apple control over third-party service reach.
- Is part of the broader shift from PC-centered computing to mobile-centered computing.
- Anchors an app-store ecosystem that produced higher 2015 store revenue than Google Play despite fewer downloads.
- Carries inherited top-navbar conventions that were useful on smaller iPhones but became ergonomically strained on larger devices.
- Includes system and app patterns, such as swipe-back, Reachability, and sheets, that can reduce dependence on top-corner controls.

## Evidence
- Platform victory: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] argues Apple and Google both won mobile in different ways.
- Ecosystem economics: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] associates Apple's ecosystem with high mobile browsing and app-store revenue share.
- Service control: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] says Google's reach on iOS depends on what Apple allows.
- PC displacement: [[16-mobile-theses-benedict-evans]] frames ARM, iOS, and Android as the ecosystem likely to swallow more PC use cases.
- App-store revenue: [[app-annie-2015-google-play-saw-100-percent-more-downloads-than-ios-app-store-venturebeat]] reports Apple's App Store at 75% more revenue than Google Play in 2015.
- Revenue geography: [[app-annie-2015-google-play-saw-100-percent-more-downloads-than-ios-app-store-venturebeat]] says China, the U.S., and Japan primarily drove App Store revenue growth.
- Navbar convention: [[all-thumbs-why-reach-navigation-should-replace-the-navbar-in-ios-design]] says the iOS navbar became recognizable because it was customizable, scalable, accessible, and easy to implement.
- Ergonomic strain: [[all-thumbs-why-reach-navigation-should-replace-the-navbar-in-ios-design]] argues that larger iPhones made top navbar buttons awkward for one-handed thumb use.
- System accommodations: [[all-thumbs-why-reach-navigation-should-replace-the-navbar-in-ios-design]] uses edge-swipe back and Reachability as iOS examples that reduce top-button dependence.
- App-pattern shift: [[all-thumbs-why-reach-navigation-should-replace-the-navbar-in-ios-design]] uses iOS 10-era Maps and Apple Music sheets as evidence of moving away from persistent navbars.

## Qualifications
The platform evidence is a 2015 strategy snapshot and does not describe later iOS features, privacy rules, App Store policy, or developer economics. The App Annie/VentureBeat source is also a 2015 snapshot and does not provide absolute revenue or later app-store economics. The reach-navigation evidence is a 2017 design-practice essay and does not provide controlled usability testing or later iOS adoption evidence; its claims should be read as a historically situated argument about large-phone ergonomics.
