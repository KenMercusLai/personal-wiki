## Overview
[[ClaytonChristensen]] is represented through [[BenThompson]]'s use and critique of his strategy theories: low-end disruption in consumer markets and the transition from integrated to modular product architectures after performance becomes good enough.

## Current Profile
The source attributes to Christensen a theory developed substantially from business purchasing examples, in which an entrant begins with a cheaper or otherwise inferior product for an overserved or neglected segment and improves until it becomes good enough for mainstream buyers. Thompson accepts the theory's analytic distinction—he agrees that Tesla is a sustaining rather than disruptive innovation—but disputes its use as a complete prescription for market entry.

In Thompson's consumer-market qualification, buyers value attributes that may resist complete documentation or modular replication, including design, integration, emotion, identity, and brand. The page records Thompson's interpretation of Christensen's framework rather than a comprehensive account of Christensen's scholarship or later views.

Christensen's integration/modularity framework supplies a second role in the current profile. Its inspected figure shows interdependent proprietary architectures leading while functionality and reliability fall short of customer needs; after performance exceeds needs, modular architectures can compete on speed, responsiveness, convenience, and customization. Thompson applies that transition to AI models and harnesses, arguing that independent multi-model harnesses and customer sensitivity to data policies may signal a performance surplus.

## Key Characteristics
- Associated with disruption theory and the distinction between disruptive and sustaining innovation.
- Represented through a secondary critic rather than Christensen's own full exposition.
- Provides the framework Thompson uses to classify Tesla as non-disruptive.
- Becomes the subject of a scope critique when business-purchasing assumptions are extended to consumer choice.
- Supplies an integration-to-modularity model in which the basis of competition changes after performance becomes good enough.

## Evidence
- Theory attribution: [[its-a-tesla-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] summarizes low-end disruption through rational buyers, measurable attributes, and modular products becoming good enough.
- Tesla classification: [[its-a-tesla-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] agrees with researchers using Christensen's framework that Tesla was a sustaining innovation rather than a classic disruptor.
- Consumer-market critique: [[its-a-tesla-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] argues that iPhone and Tesla demand depended on attributes the simplified low-end model did not capture.
- Platform qualification: [[its-a-tesla-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] notes that smartphones disrupted PCs while arguing that this alone did not explain why particular handset incumbents failed.
- Architecture transition: [[frontier-overhangs]] reproduces an inspected Christensen figure contrasting integrated performance leadership with modular competition after a performance surplus.
- AI application: [[frontier-overhangs]] applies the framework to separable models and harnesses, customer data-policy choices, and the claim that pure capability no longer guarantees a moat.

## Qualifications
The sources are Thompson's interpretation and selected quotation, not a systematic review of Christensen's scholarship. They compress large theories into Apple, Tesla, and frontier-model cases. The AI evidence does not establish a broad performance surplus: one data-policy reversal, a multi-model harness, and a personal-agent example may not generalize across workloads or customers. Agreement that Tesla was not technically disruptive should not be confused with agreement that disruption theory predicts every successful entrant.
