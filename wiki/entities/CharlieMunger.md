## Overview
[[CharlieMunger]] appears in the wiki through the Farnam Street essay on the [[CircleOfCompetence]], where he supplies the step that takes Buffett's investing rule and turns it into a general prescription for how to spend a limited life.

## Current Profile
The source quotes Munger answering the question of where a person should devote limited time to achieve the most success: figure out your own aptitudes, because playing games where other people hold the aptitude is "as close to certain as any prediction that you can make" a way to lose. His instruction is to find where you have an edge and to play inside your own circle of competence. His illustration is deliberately unglamorous - anyone who wants to be the best tennis player in the world will likely discover the goal is hopeless, while becoming the best plumbing contractor in a town like Bemidji is attainable for roughly two-thirds of readers given will, intelligence, and enough discipline, because that knowledge accumulates gradually through work. In his telling the circle is therefore partly inherited and partly developed, and mastery inside it is an attainable objective rather than a lottery win. The essay uses him as the bridge that carries the boundary discipline out of markets and into career and life choices.

## Key Characteristics
- Extends the circle of competence from investing into general life and career strategy.
- Makes aptitude the first question: figure out what you are actually good at before choosing the contest.
- Treats playing where other people hold the aptitude as a near-certain path to losing.
- Directs people toward the domain where they have an edge rather than the domain they find most attractive.
- Prefers attainable, locally dominant objectives to winner-take-all contests, contrasting world-class tennis with becoming the best plumbing contractor in a small town.
- Describes the circle as partly innate and partly developed slowly through work.
- Ties attainment inside the circle to will, intelligence, and discipline rather than to talent alone.

## Qualifications
The wiki knows Munger only through this one 2013 Farnam Street essay, which quotes his prescription without dating the remarks, naming their setting, or situating them among his other work. The "two-thirds of you" figure and the tennis-versus-plumbing comparison are rhetorical illustrations rather than evidence, and the source does not discuss how someone should discover their aptitudes, how reliable self-assessed aptitude is, or what happens to a person whose edge lies in a domain with no local demand. It also frames the question purely as maximizing success and leaves out failures of the same discipline - for instance, staying inside a circle that has become obsolete, or mistaking early success for competence.
