## Overview
[[TristanHarris]] is presented as a Google design philosopher and ethical-design advocate arguing that technology should compete on its net contribution to people's lives rather than raw attention.

## Current Profile
The 2015 source connects Harris's interest in manipulation to an early background in sleight-of-hand, study of persuasive technology with [[BJFogg]], interface work at Apple, and cofounding Apture before its acquisition by Google. His Time Well Spent proposal asks products to help users carry out stated intentions—such as a bounded social session or interruption-free work period—and then stop. He recognizes user responsibility but argues that it operates against concentrated teams, data, and commercial incentives optimized to overcome willpower.

## Key Characteristics
- Frames platform-user interaction as an asymmetric contest over attention and self-control.
- Advocates measuring net life benefit rather than maximizing raw engagement.
- Proposes intention-aware, time-bounded product behavior and delayed non-urgent delivery.
- Uses an organic-food analogy for certification and alternative digital products.
- Acknowledges, through the source's critique, that voluntary wellbeing tools may remain niche and inaccessible.

## Evidence
- Power asymmetry: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] quotes Harris on highly skilled teams working to overcome user willpower.
- Product vision: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] describes Time Well Spent designs that ask what users intend and help sessions end accordingly.
- Incentive shift: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] reports his call to compete on net positive contribution rather than attention alone.

## Qualifications
The page reflects Schulson's 2015 reporting and therefore captures an early, source-bounded profile rather than Harris's later organizations, positions, or influence. The source does not evaluate Time Well Spent experimentally, and it questions whether optional tools and certification can overcome access gaps or investor incentives.
