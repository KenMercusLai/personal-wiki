## Overview
[[Minecraft]] is a game used in the source as an extreme example of public release, rapid iteration, and finding the core fun with paying users.

## Current Profile
The source describes Minecraft's first public build as a crude block-placement experience released after six days of coding by one developer. More than one hundred releases reportedly followed in the first year, with players communicating directly with the developer and paying for early access. In the article's product-development argument, this made real play rather than internal completeness the main feedback mechanism.

## Key Characteristics
- Began with a very narrow block-digging and placement loop.
- Reached public users after a reported six days of development.
- Used frequent releases and direct player communication to discover what was fun.
- Asked early users to pay, giving them an incentive to participate in improvement.

## Evidence
- First release: [[crisps-blog-making-sense-of-mvp-minimum-viable-product-and-why-i-prefer-earliest-testable-usable-lovable]] describes a six-day, single-developer public build with crude block manipulation.
- Iteration cadence: [[crisps-blog-making-sense-of-mvp-minimum-viable-product-and-why-i-prefer-earliest-testable-usable-lovable]] reports more than one hundred releases in the first year.
- User loop: [[crisps-blog-making-sense-of-mvp-minimum-viable-product-and-why-i-prefer-earliest-testable-usable-lovable]] says paid early users played the game and communicated feedback directly.

## Qualifications
This is a compressed retrospective from a practitioner essay, not a verified development history or causal study. Minecraft's later success does not show that rapid public release is sufficient for other games, and the source does not analyze discoverability, community selection, platform context, or the many early-access games that fail.
