## Overview
[[BillBuxton]] is represented in this wiki as the author of the Long Nose of Innovation argument, a technology-history and product-development frame that emphasizes decades of refinement before apparent breakthroughs.

## Current Profile
The source presents Buxton as a critic of invention-centered innovation mythology. His main contribution here is the [[LongNoseInnovation]] metaphor: many technologies that seem newly disruptive have already spent years moving through research labs, prototypes, commercial experiments, design improvements, and adoption bottlenecks.

Buxton's stance is practical rather than merely historical. He argues that companies, universities, and governments should reward the work of finding, adapting, and refining neglected ideas because that work can materially shorten the time from known concept to broad impact.

## Key Characteristics
- Author of the Long Nose of Innovation frame.
- Treats innovation as refinement and adoption work, not only invention.
- Uses technology-history examples, especially the computer mouse, to challenge sudden-breakthrough narratives.
- Argues that future-impact technologies are often already visible before they become mainstream.
- Critiques institutional impatience around quick technology-transfer returns.

## Evidence
- Authorship: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] identifies Buxton as the author of the January 2, 2008 BusinessWeek article.
- Conceptual frame: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] introduces the Long Nose as a mirror image of the Long Tail.
- Historical method: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] uses the mouse's multi-decade path from research prototype to mainstream PC interface as the main example.
- Refinement emphasis: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] says innovation is not alchemy and highlights augmentation, prospecting, mining, refining, and goldsmithing.
- Institutional critique: [[bill-buxton-the-long-nose-of-innovation]] criticizes universities, governments, and businesses for glorifying invention while undervaluing refinement.
