## Overview
[[LindaHirshman]] is a legal scholar, former labor lawyer, and author represented here through her history and strategic interpretation of the U.S. gay-rights movement.

## Current Profile
Hirshman rejects the idea that nationwide same-sex marriage arrived suddenly. She traces a sequence from Frank Kameny's resistance and early organizing through challenges to psychiatric classification, state sodomy-law reform, *Lawrence v. Texas*, state marriage victories, and changing public opinion. Her movement advice emphasizes focused interests, moral positioning, durable in-person relationships, and a next demand bold enough to matter but grounded in conditions already created.

## Key Characteristics
- Interprets legal and social victories through long institutional sequences rather than dramatic-event narratives.
- Treats research quality and classification change as part of civil-rights strategy.
- Uses the NAACP's ordered litigation as a model of movement sequencing while noting that gay-rights organizing lacked equivalent centralized control.
- Recommends issue focus, moral credibility, and recurring face-to-face organization for emerging movements.

## Evidence
- Historical sequence: [[in-praise-of-incrementalism-ep-264-freakonomics-freakonomics]] traces organizing, psychiatric declassification, state reform, Supreme Court reversals, marriage cases, and public-opinion change.
- Research challenge: [[in-praise-of-incrementalism-ep-264-freakonomics-freakonomics]] presents activists' criticism of psychiatric studies drawn from unrepresentative patient populations.
- Movement guidance: [[in-praise-of-incrementalism-ep-264-freakonomics-freakonomics]] records Hirshman's advice to protect scarce movement capacity, claim the moral high ground, maintain weekly meetings, and choose feasible next steps.

## Qualifications
This profile derives from one edited 2016 interview and does not represent Hirshman's full legal career or scholarship. The episode's compressed movement history privileges litigation and selected organizations, and her advice is interpretive rather than comparative evidence that the same sequence or organizational form will work for other movements.
