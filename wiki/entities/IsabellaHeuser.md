## Overview
[[IsabellaHeuser]] is represented as director of the Charité Clinic for Psychiatry and Psychotherapy and as the clinician who explains why habitual social-media use may become painful during depression.

## Current Profile
Heuser distinguishes preserved anticipation from the blunted positive response associated with depression. In her account, daily social-media use teaches the brain to expect escapism or connection, but anhedonia can prevent the reward from arriving; remembering what joy felt like makes that absence painful. She also connects reduced resistance to depression's lack of energy, impaired attention to an already low concentration threshold, and curated social comparison to depression's broader tendency to produce self-defeating interpretations.

## Key Characteristics
- Psychiatrist presented through an explanatory interview about depression and social-media use.
- Separates learned reward anticipation from the inability to experience the expected pleasure or connection.
- Links depleted willpower to the low energy characteristic of depression.
- Treats curated comparison as one input into depression's wider self-critical cognition.

## Evidence
- Clinical role and patient pattern: [[facebooks-mental-health-problem-anxy-magazine-medium]] identifies Heuser as the Charité clinic director and reports that most patients she had seen stayed away from social media.
- Reward explanation: [[facebooks-mental-health-problem-anxy-magazine-medium]] records her account of anticipation persisting while the positive response fails.
- Energy and attention: [[facebooks-mental-health-problem-anxy-magazine-medium]] records her explanation that low energy weakens will and depression can push attention below a tolerable threshold.
- Comparison qualification: [[facebooks-mental-health-problem-anxy-magazine-medium]] records her view that social media indirectly supplies material for depression's self-critical interpretations.

## Qualifications
The page is grounded in one magazine interview rather than Heuser's research publications, a formal clinical assessment, or a tested causal model. The article paraphrases much of her reasoning, and the dopamine language should not be treated as a measured neurobiological result for the author or as a universal explanation of social-media use during depression.
