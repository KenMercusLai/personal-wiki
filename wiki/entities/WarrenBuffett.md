## Overview
[[WarrenBuffett]] appears in the wiki as the value-investing comparison point for long-term bets on enduring business advantages, as a quoted admirer and healthcare collaborator in the Forbes profile of [[JeffBezos]], as a rare professional investor example of resisting technology-bubble pressure, as Housel's example of a role model whose patience is more repeatable than his early market environment, as the source of the [[CircleOfCompetence]] boundary rule, and as the originator of the "ovarian lottery" thought experiment used to explain unequal starting conditions.

## Current Profile
The timeless-strategy essay uses Buffett's decades-long ownership of GEICO as a case where many surface details changed while the underlying advantage persisted. GEICO moved from cafeteria sales to broader direct insurance on phones, and analytics advanced from primitive tools to AI, but the cost and convenience advantage of selling directly rather than through brokers remained the compounding driver. The Forbes profile adds Buffett as an outside validator of Bezos's business achievement and as a partner in the Bezos-Buffett-Dimon nonprofit healthcare initiative, where he emphasizes uncertain exploration and encourages others to copy any discoveries. Wulc's Zhang Xiaoyu source adds Buffett as the counterexample in the 1999-2000 technology bubble: he could refuse Internet-stock pressure despite underperforming and being criticized before the crash vindicated that restraint. Housel's repeatability essay turns Buffett into a learning-boundary example: patience is broadly learnable, but the market conditions of the 1950s are not.

The Farnam Street essay adds Buffett's own statement of where that restraint comes from. In the 1996 shareholder letter he tells investors that they do not need to be experts on every company, or even many, only able to evaluate the companies inside their circle of competence, and he treats knowledge of the circle's boundary as more important than its size. The same source supplies his Mrs. B case: he says he could not have paid a business manager in Berkshire stock because she did not understand stocks, while she understood cash, furniture, and real estate well enough to buy end tables and odd-lot carpets on the spot. His stated rule for her was to leave the stock market alone and work inside what she knew. The essay also records the closing advice attached to the model - operate inside the perimeter, widen it slowly, never fool yourself about where it stands today, and never be afraid to say "I don't know".

Clear's essay adds Buffett's societal thought experiment. Before birth, a person may design society's rules but does not know which position they will occupy; Buffett calls that draw the ovarian lottery and treats it as more consequential than schooling or effort. The article pairs this with his acknowledgement that being born in the United States gave him and Charlie Munger extraordinary advantages, using Buffett to support the claim that extreme achievement cannot be separated from circumstances the achiever did not choose.

## Key Characteristics
- Represents the "betting against change" side in the source's venture-versus-value comparison.
- Uses long holding periods to benefit from enduring advantages, with GEICO illustrating persistent cost and convenience amid changing channels.
- Praises Bezos for redefining retail and cloud computing simultaneously.
- Joins Bezos and Jamie Dimon in a nonprofit healthcare initiative aimed at lower-cost employee care.
- Illustrates the rare ability to withstand bubble-era pressure while separating repeatable temperament from non-repeatable market conditions.
- Defines investing judgment as staying inside a circle of competence whose boundaries matter more than its size.
- Uses the ovarian lottery to connect accidental birth conditions with moral evaluation of social rules and personal success.

## Qualifications
The page remains source-scoped. It does not analyze Buffett's full record, Berkshire Hathaway, valuation methods, later GEICO performance, detailed dot-com-era holdings, or later outcomes of the healthcare initiative. The repeatability source gives a high-level learning distinction rather than a detailed comparison between Buffett's early opportunities and current markets.

The circle-of-competence material arrives as a 2013 Farnam Street essay that quotes Buffett second-hand and gives no measurement of how competence or its boundary should be assessed; the wiki's investing sources describe the same rule as a guardrail rather than as demonstrated performance. The ovarian-lottery account combines versions from a shareholder meeting and a student speech, lightly edited by Clear, so it is evidence of Buffett's stated moral frame rather than a causal estimate of how much luck explains any outcome.
