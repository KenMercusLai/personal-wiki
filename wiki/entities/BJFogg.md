## Overview
[[BJFogg]] is the behavior-design researcher and author presented in the source as the creator of the [[FoggBehaviorModel]] and founder of the [[BehaviorDesignLab]] at Stanford University.

## Current Profile
Within this wiki, Fogg functions as the source authority for a practical behavior-change design framework. His captured page describes him as directing research and innovation at Stanford's Behavior Design Lab, teaching professionals to use behavior-design models and methods, and writing or editing books on persuasive technology, mobile persuasion, and tiny habits. Schulson's 2015 essay places that work in an earlier captology tradition of using computers to change behavior: it reports that Fogg taught future technology leaders and included an ethics coda in a 1998 paper, but characterizes the appeal to defensible standards as weaker than enforceable governance.

## Key Characteristics
- Created and presents the [[FoggBehaviorModel]].
- Founded the [[BehaviorDesignLab]] at Stanford University.
- Teaches professionals how to apply behavior-design models and methods.
- Frames his work around helping people improve their lives.
- Authored or edited behavior-change and persuasive-technology books, including [[TinyHabits]].
- Recognized an ethical obligation for designers of computer-mediated persuasion, while the source questions whether voluntary standards are sufficient.

## Evidence
- Model authorship: [[behavior-model]] is written in Fogg's voice and presents the Fogg Behavior Model as his behavior model.
- Lab role: [[behavior-model]] says Fogg founded Stanford's Behavior Design Lab and directs research and innovation there.
- Teaching role: [[behavior-model]] describes professional training in behavior design.
- Purpose: [[behavior-model]] says his research and teaching aim to help millions of people improve their lives.
- Publications: [[behavior-model]] names Persuasive Technology, Mobile Persuasion, and [[TinyHabits]] in connection with Fogg's work.
- Captology and ethics: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] describes Fogg's early computer-persuasion work, teaching influence, and call for defensible ethical standards.

## Qualifications
The behavior-model source is self-authored or self-presentational, so it is reliable for the model's own framing but narrow for independent evaluation of Fogg's influence or empirical claims. Schulson's essay adds historical and ethical context through a brief secondary account, not the full 1998 paper or a systematic record of Fogg's students and influence. Neither source defines who determines beneficial behavior or shows that voluntary ethical standards prevent harmful application.

## What Changed
- Added Fogg's early captology and explicit ethics framing to the model-centered profile.
- Qualified voluntary ethical standards through the unresolved gap between stated benefit and enforceable user protection.
