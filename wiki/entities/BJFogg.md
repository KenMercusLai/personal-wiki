## Overview
[[BJFogg]] is the behavior-design researcher and author presented in the source as the creator of the [[FoggBehaviorModel]] and founder of the [[BehaviorDesignLab]] at Stanford University.

## Current Profile
Within this wiki, Fogg functions as the source authority for a practical behavior-change design framework. His captured page describes him as directing research and innovation at Stanford's Behavior Design Lab, teaching professionals to use behavior-design models and methods, and writing or editing books on persuasive technology, mobile persuasion, and tiny habits. Eyal’s product-adoption essay adds a concrete ability mechanism from Fogg’s simplicity factors: non-routine behavior is harder because learning and training require effort. Schulson's 2015 essay places the work in an earlier captology tradition and reports an ethics coda in a 1998 paper, but characterizes defensible voluntary standards as weaker than enforceable governance.

## Key Characteristics
- Created and presents the [[FoggBehaviorModel]].
- Founded the [[BehaviorDesignLab]] at Stanford University.
- Teaches professionals how to apply behavior-design models and methods.
- Frames his work around helping people improve their lives.
- Authored or edited behavior-change and persuasive-technology books, including [[TinyHabits]].
- Recognized an ethical obligation for designers of computer-mediated persuasion, while the source questions whether voluntary standards are sufficient.
- Treats non-routine behavior as less simple because it imposes learning effort.

## Evidence
- Model authorship: [[behavior-model]] is written in Fogg's voice and presents the Fogg Behavior Model as his behavior model.
- Lab role: [[behavior-model]] says Fogg founded Stanford's Behavior Design Lab and directs research and innovation there.
- Teaching role: [[behavior-model]] describes professional training in behavior design.
- Purpose: [[behavior-model]] says his research and teaching aim to help millions of people improve their lives.
- Publications: [[behavior-model]] names Persuasive Technology, Mobile Persuasion, and [[TinyHabits]] in connection with Fogg's work.
- Captology and ethics: [[if-the-internet-is-addictive-why-dont-we-regulate-it-aeon-essays]] describes Fogg's early computer-persuasion work, teaching influence, and call for defensible ethical standards.
- Routine and simplicity: [[people-dont-want-something-truly-new-they-want-the-familiar-done-differently]] quotes Fogg on people staying with routines, sometimes despite extra time or money, because non-routine action feels harder.

## Qualifications
The behavior-model source is self-authored or self-presentational, so it is reliable for the model's own framing but narrow for independent evaluation of Fogg's influence or empirical claims. The routine discussion reaches the wiki through Eyal’s excerpt rather than a complete evaluation of Fogg’s six simplicity factors. Schulson's essay adds historical and ethical context through a brief secondary account, not the full 1998 paper or a systematic record of Fogg's students and influence. None defines who determines beneficial behavior or shows that voluntary ethical standards prevent harmful application.
