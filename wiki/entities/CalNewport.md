## Overview
[[CalNewport]] is the author of the Study Hacks article "Facebook's Desperate Smoke Screen," represented here through his critique of social-media engagement incentives and attention capture.

## Current Profile
In the source, Newport distinguishes Facebook's political-content problems from what he considers the more fundamental problem of compulsive use. He argues that an advertising business tied to time spent can debate and mitigate democratic harms while remaining structurally reluctant to reduce engagement itself. In replies to commenters, he acknowledges that Facebook Groups can provide valuable support and affinity communities but separates that deliberate, bounded use from the much larger volume of usage the business seeks.

## Key Characteristics
- Frames attention as a scarce input diverted from meaningful personal and professional activity.
- Analyzes platform behavior through the incentives of an engagement-funded advertising model.
- Treats Facebook's democracy initiative as a strategically convenient response rather than sufficient evidence of reform.
- Acknowledges useful social-platform functions while arguing they do not require continuous feed engagement.

## Evidence
- Attention critique: [[facebooks-desperate-smoke-screen-study-hacks-cal-newport]] says compulsive social-media use packages time and attention for resale to advertisers.
- Incentive analysis: [[facebooks-desperate-smoke-screen-study-hacks-cal-newport]] argues that reducing addictive features would threaten usage-linked revenue more directly than changing political content.
- Qualified product view: [[facebooks-desperate-smoke-screen-study-hacks-cal-newport]] records Newport acknowledging valuable Facebook Groups while advocating much more bounded use.

## Qualifications
This profile is based on one 2018 polemical blog post and its comment replies, not a broad account of Newport's books, academic work, later positions, or empirical evidence. The source does not prove Facebook's intent, quantify the revenue effect of reduced engagement, or use clinical criteria for addiction.
