## Overview
[[MichaelSeibel]] appears in the wiki as the CEO of [[YCombinator]] Core and a founder-operator: he sold Twitch to [[Amazon]] for $1 billion in 2014 and was previously a founder of SocialCam, acquired for $60 million. The source is an edited AMA that presents his operating judgments about product-market fit, application screening, technical founding teams, persistence, and accelerators.

## Current Profile
The source frames Seibel as an accelerator insider speaking from the selection side of the table rather than as a disinterested analyst. He describes YC as a university, an investor, and a software company combined whose goal is to last hundreds of years while putting founders first, and he explains how that stance shapes which companies he wants to back. His advice is consistently stage-aware and evidence-driven: demand that outruns capacity is the real test of fit, applications are read for disqualifying negatives, technical founders are non-negotiable, and persistence is loyalty to the customer and the problem rather than to the current solution.

## Key Characteristics
- Is introduced as CEO of YC Core with a founder track record of the Twitch sale to Amazon and the SocialCam acquisition.
- Leads an institution he describes as a university, an investor, and a software company combined, oriented toward founders-first longevity.
- Screens applications by looking for negatives, treating the absence of objections as the strong positive signal.
- Names unclear communication, the absence of a technical founder, and slow accomplishment as the three turn-offs.
- Distinguishes persistence from stubbornness: keep the customer and the problem, iterate hard on the solution.
- Rejects contract developers outright for technology companies while accepting missing business experience.
- Treats accelerators as optional and uneven, warning that weak programs overpromise and can set founders back.

## Qualifications
The page rests on a single edited AMA, so all characteristics are Seibel's stated positions rather than observed outcomes. His accelerator and application judgments come from the leader of the best-known accelerator, which is a self-interested vantage point on both his own program and rival ones. The source offers no data on how often the stated turn-offs predict failure, and its side opinions on software patents, ICOs, MBAs, and women-in-tech conferences are personal positions rather than evidence-backed findings. The interview also predates later YC and startup-market changes.
