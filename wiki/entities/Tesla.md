## Overview
[[Tesla]] is represented as a founder commitment, a premium electric-vehicle entrant whose product and brand created unusual Model 3 demand, a camera-led autonomous-driving strategy built around integrated fleet data, and a tightly coupled manufacturing system that required capacity margin.

## Current Profile
The motivational source pairs Tesla with [[SpaceX]] to show how serious desire can pull a founder into a difficult, uncertain field. Thompson's 2016 strategy essay gives that ambition a market-entry structure. Tesla moved from the expensive Roadster through Model S and Model X toward the lower-priced Model 3, accumulating [[BrandEquity]] around performance, sustainability, and Silicon Valley identity instead of entering through a low-cost, inferior neighborhood vehicle. Thompson classifies this as sustaining rather than classic disruptive innovation, but argues that [[ConsumerMarketDisruptionLimits]] make that label an incomplete prediction of commercial viability.

The Model 3 reservation response is strong but bounded demand evidence. Thompson reports 276,000 refundable $1,000 deposits after three days, including 115,000 placed before the car was shown. The retained luxury-sales table also reports 25,202 U.S. Model S sales in 2015, up 51.01% while every listed large-luxury rival declined. Neither reservations nor a selected premium segment establish profitable mass-market scale, and the proposed master plan was funded through stock issuance and debt as well as vehicle gross profit.

Evans' autonomy essay adds a specific historical technology strategy: Tesla planned to build the significant technology itself, retain its fleet data, and use eight cameras, forward radar, and ultrasonic sensors to gather mapping and driver-behavior observations. Because only the radar looked forward and practical lidar was absent, Evans describes this as a contrarian bet that computer vision would become capable enough to interpret the collected imagery before cheap lidar made the shortcut irrelevant.

The 2018 Mindset fragment adds a production-system view. Musk reports three Model 3 weeks above 2,000 vehicles, plans short factory shutdowns for upgrades, calls for around-the-clock Fremont production and rapid hiring, and requires internal teams and suppliers to demonstrate capacity above the desired near-term output. The stated rationale is [[ProductionCapacityBuffer]]: when thousands of components and logistics steps are coupled, nominal capacity without margin leaves whole-system output exposed to the weakest part. These are historical internal claims and targets, not independently verified outcomes.

## Key Characteristics
- Represents electric-vehicle ambition expressed through founder commitment and external capital.
- Entered at the premium end, built product and brand meaning, and then announced a lower-priced mass-market vehicle.
- Generated unusually strong reservation demand before delivery, without those deposits proving sales, profit, or fulfillment capacity.
- Uses vertical integration to keep vehicle data inside its own autonomy learning loop in the 2017 essay.
- Pursues a camera-led autonomy approach that trades early fleet reach against a harder computer-vision problem.
- Treated internal manufacturing, external suppliers, staffing, factory upgrades, and logistics as one coupled Model 3 production system in the 2018 memo.
- Required subsystem burst capacity above the near-term whole-system output target to create margin for variation.

- Reservation demand: [[its-a-tesla-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] reports 276,000 refundable deposits, including 115,000 placed before the Model 3 reveal.
- Luxury-segment momentum: [[its-a-tesla-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] retains a company-report table showing 25,202 U.S. Model S sales in 2015, up 51.01%, while the listed rivals declined.
- Funding boundary: [[its-a-tesla-stratechery-by-ben-thompson]] says annual losses meant equity and debt, not high-end model profits alone, funded research and development.

- Technical tradeoff: [[winner-takes-all-effects-in-autonomous-cars-benedict-evans]] argues that avoiding then-impractical lidar let Tesla collect at customer-fleet scale but required computer vision to solve a harder 3D-perception problem.

## Qualifications
The sources do not provide a complete Tesla history, current business assessment, safety record, labor assessment, regulatory status, or current performance. Thompson's Model 3 demand and customer-grace judgments are 2016 forecasts built from refundable deposits and a selected company-reported luxury segment; they do not establish fulfillment, margin, mass-market share, or that brand caused demand. Evans' sensor and capability claims are a 2017 snapshot and explicitly speculative: a large raw dataset has limited value if the system cannot interpret it, while cheaper lidar or slower progress elsewhere in the stack could erase the proposed timing advantage. The 2018 production source is an incomplete blog reproduction of an executive email; it reports internal numbers, plans, and aspirations without independently showing that the 6,000-per-week rate or tenfold precision goal was achieved at acceptable quality, cost, or worker impact.

## What Changed

- Added Model 3 reservations and 2015 large-luxury sales as strong but bounded demand evidence.
- Qualified the master-plan funding story with equity issuance, debt, annual losses, and unresolved execution risk.
