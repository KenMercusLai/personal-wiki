## Overview
[[DanahBoyd]] is represented as a researcher and author interpreting how hacker culture, social platforms, media incentives, and political organization converged in efforts to manipulate public attention.

## Current Profile
Boyd's 2017 essay follows techniques rather than treating “hackers” as a single political group. It connects early boundary-pushing and 4chan attention games with marketing, activism, Anonymous, Gamergate, propaganda, and institutional distrust. Her central analytic move is to separate reusable network tactics from their motives: coordinated amplification, algorithm gaming, coded meaning, doxing, swatting, and gaslighting can serve humor, profit, ideology, activism, harassment, or power.

The profile is limited to one historical essay. It establishes Boyd's argument and selected cases, not a complete account of her scholarship or empirical proof that every later campaign descended from the same communities.

## Key Characteristics
- Treats hacking as intimate system knowledge used to stretch capabilities, not only unauthorized technical access.
- Analyzes media manipulation as a coupled product of platform mechanics, social coordination, and old-media incentives.
- Distinguishes shared tactics from divergent commercial, civic, ideological, and abusive motives.
- Emphasizes the transition from playful attention games toward organized harassment, propaganda, and power acquisition.

## Evidence
- Hacker framing: [[hacking-the-attention-economy-data-society-points]] uses historical hacker definitions and youth subcultures to broaden hacking beyond security breaches.
- Network mechanism: [[hacking-the-attention-economy-data-society-points]] connects memes, coordinated campaigns, algorithms, trending systems, and journalistic amplification.
- Political transition: [[hacking-the-attention-economy-data-society-points]] traces a selected path through Anonymous, Gamergate, doxing, swatting, gaslighting, and institutional doubt.
- Normative boundary: [[hacking-the-attention-economy-data-society-points]] argues that legality and familiar marketing language do not settle whether manipulation practices are socially acceptable.

## Qualifications
The wiki currently represents Boyd through one January 2017 interpretive essay rather than her broader body of research. Its examples are selected and largely qualitative; they do not measure prevalence, effect size, failed campaigns, platform differences, or causal descent among meme culture, activism, harassment, propaganda, and electoral politics.
