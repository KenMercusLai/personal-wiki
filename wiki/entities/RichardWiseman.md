## Overview
[[RichardWiseman]] is a psychologist represented in the wiki through a popular account of his research into behavioral differences between people who describe themselves as lucky or unlucky.

## Current Profile
Wiseman proposes that luck is partly shaped by behavior rather than only by uncontrollable events. His account links opportunity detection to relaxed and broad attention, and describes intuition, positive expectations, and resilient interpretation of setbacks as additional practices. The strongest intervention claim is that a month of “luck school” exercises improved reported happiness, satisfaction, and luck for 80 percent of participants, but the article does not provide the study design or measurements needed to evaluate that estimate.

## Key Characteristics
- Studies self-described lucky and unlucky people through interviews, diaries, questionnaires, tests, and experiments.
- Uses simple attention demonstrations to make psychological mechanisms legible to a general audience.
- Treats opportunity detection, intuition, expectation, and resilience as trainable contributors to perceived luck.
- Presents behavior change as probabilistic influence rather than a complete account of chance.

## Evidence
- Research program: [[be-lucky-its-an-easy-skill-to-learn]] says Wiseman recruited 400 volunteers over a decade and used interviews, diaries, questionnaires, intelligence tests, and experiments.
- Attention mechanism: [[be-lucky-its-an-easy-skill-to-learn]] reports that many self-described unlucky participants missed conspicuous instructions and a prize offer while counting newspaper photographs.
- Intervention claim: [[be-lucky-its-an-easy-skill-to-learn]] reports that a month of exercises based on four luck principles improved participant-reported outcomes.

## Qualifications
This profile is based on one 2003 newspaper article rather than the underlying studies or Wiseman's broader scholarly record. The article omits essential intervention details, and its participant labels, examples, and outcomes rely heavily on self-description. Its behavioral account should not be read as denying structural inequality, harmful chance, or constraints that mindset and attention cannot overcome.
