## Overview
[[MaxWeber]] appears as the sociologist whose distinction between procedural and substantive rationality helps explain why meetings can become organizational rituals.

## Current Profile
Barnett invokes Weber to distinguish work that follows rational-looking procedures from work that substantively achieves its purpose. In the source, Weber is not discussed biographically; he functions as the theoretical anchor for seeing recurring meetings as legitimacy signals that may hide the absence of a real question, decision, or outcome.

## Key Characteristics
- Provides the procedural-versus-substantive rationality distinction used in the meeting critique.
- Serves as a sociological lens on organizational ritual.
- Helps separate rational-looking process from useful work.

## Evidence
- Theoretical attribution: [[bill-barnett-delete-all-meetings]] identifies meetings as an example of what Weber called procedural rationality.
- Substantive contrast: [[bill-barnett-delete-all-meetings]] argues that meetings can symbolize rational work regardless of whether they help get work done.
- Agenda inversion: [[bill-barnett-delete-all-meetings]] says people often set meetings and only afterward decide what should be on the agenda.

## Qualifications
The source gives a compressed use of Weber's theory rather than a primary reading of Weber or a detailed account of his sociology.
