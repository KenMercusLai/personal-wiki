## Overview
[[FarnamStreet]] is a publication that translates investing, judgment, and decision-making ideas into reusable mental models, represented here by essays on the [[CircleOfCompetence]] and [[ReversibleDecisionMaking]].

## Current Profile
The circle-of-competence essay is a curated synthesis rather than original research. It assembles Buffett, Munger, and Tom Watson Sr. into a boundary discipline: define what you understand, operate inside it, expand it slowly, and admit ignorance. The reversible-decisions essay uses a similar editorial method, combining Bezos's shareholder-letter distinction with startup velocity, learning loops, sunk costs, a Patton maxim, and a second-hand Gladwell medical example. Together they show Farnam Street's recurring form: extract a portable heuristic from attributed examples, widen its application, and add practical guidance.

That portability is useful but can hide classification problems. Knowing whether a domain is inside one's competence and whether a decision is truly reversible both require judgment the essays do not operationalize. The publication's advice is therefore best treated as a framing aid whose source claims and consequences still need independent scrutiny.

## Key Characteristics
- Publishes essays that translate investing and decision-making concepts for a general audience.
- Builds the essay mainly by quoting primary and secondary sources rather than reporting new research.
- Curates a "latticework of mental models" collection that the circle of competence belongs to.
- Treats an investing rule as a general thinking tool applicable to business and personal life.
- Keeps the practical instruction narrow even while widening the concept's scope: define the perimeter, stay inside it, expand slowly, admit when you do not know.
- Attributes the concept to Buffett while using Munger to extend it beyond markets.
- Uses Bezos's one-way-door/two-way-door distinction to argue for faster learning on reversible choices.

## Qualifications
This page is source-scoped to two essays and does not establish the quality of the publication's wider catalog. Both pieces curate attributed material rather than report original comparative research. The circle-of-competence essay reproduces Buffett and Munger second-hand; the reversible-decisions essay's medical example is also second-hand and should not be treated as clinical guidance. Its 70% threshold and broad claims about startup advantage are heuristics rather than measured universal rules.
