## Overview
[[Triplebyte]] is represented here as an engineering recruiting platform that combined candidate assessment and job placement with employer-published analysis of career economics and rejection feedback.

## Current Profile
The 2016 relocation article uses Triplebyte's placements to challenge lower BLS salary figures for San Francisco: it reports an average offer of about $139,000 and slightly higher offers in the city than on the peninsula. The company presents access to candidates and employers as practical evidence about location decisions, while its placement business creates an incentive to emphasize the Bay Area's advantages.

The 2018 feedback article adds an operating view of Triplebyte's standardized assessment. Interviewers reportedly recorded detailed observations across multiple dimensions, while [[KelseyPiper]] compiled and reviewed those observations, identified decision-relevant weaknesses, and maintained free learning recommendations. Triplebyte limited individualized feedback to candidates who completed a two-hour interview and iterated on wording after angry responses. This extends the profile from recruiting intermediary to hiring-process operator, but both accounts remain interested, self-reported descriptions rather than independent outcome evidence.

## Key Characteristics
- Recruiting intermediary between software engineers and Bay Area technology employers.
- Source of internal offer data used to supplement public salary datasets.
- Publisher of career and location analysis aimed at prospective engineering candidates.
- Operator of a standardized technical interview and individualized feedback process for later-stage candidates.
- Used central editorial review and repeated wording changes to improve reported candidate reactions.
- Commercial beneficiary when candidates pursue jobs in the market it serves.

## Evidence
- Recruiting role: [[does-it-make-sense-for-programmers-to-move-to-the-bay-area-triplebyte-blog]] says Triplebyte helped engineers around the country and world get jobs at top Bay Area companies.
- Internal data: [[does-it-make-sense-for-programmers-to-move-to-the-bay-area-triplebyte-blog]] reports an average candidate salary near $139,000 and a small San Francisco advantage over peninsula placements.
- Publishing role: [[does-it-make-sense-for-programmers-to-move-to-the-bay-area-triplebyte-blog]] combines those observations with public salary, housing, and compensation sources.
- Incentive: [[does-it-make-sense-for-programmers-to-move-to-the-bay-area-triplebyte-blog]] ends by inviting readers interested in moving to the Bay Area to use Triplebyte's hiring process.
- Feedback process: [[i-know-why-rejection-emails-suck-i-write-them-triplebyte-blog]] describes detailed interviewer notes, multi-axis ratings, central review, tailored advice, and periodically maintained free resources.
- Coverage boundary: [[i-know-why-rejection-emails-suck-i-write-them-triplebyte-blog]] says individualized feedback followed a two-hour interview but was not available to every online-quiz participant.
- Iteration: [[i-know-why-rejection-emails-suck-i-write-them-triplebyte-blog]] reports better reactions after messages bounded claims to demonstrated performance, emphasized decisive sections, and made mixed judgments more specific.

## Qualifications
This is a source-scoped 2016-2018 profile, not a current company history. The relocation article does not disclose candidate selection, sample size, role mix, experience distribution, offer dates, or employer composition. The feedback article provides no email corpus, response denominator, cost accounting, candidate survey, demographic analysis, or legal outcomes; its legal discussion relies on the author's conversation with an unnamed lawyer. Triplebyte's commercial incentive does not invalidate either account, but it raises the evidentiary burden for claims about placement markets and candidate benefit.

## What Changed
- Expanded Triplebyte from recruiting intermediary and publisher to operator of a standardized technical assessment and rejection-feedback process.
- Added the resource boundary, editorial controls, wording iterations, and missing outcome evidence around individualized feedback.
