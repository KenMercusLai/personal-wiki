## Overview
[[KirstenWeir]] is the writer of the 2013 article “Feel like a fraud?”, represented here through her synthesis of research, clinical interpretation, and graduate-student accounts of the impostor phenomenon.

## Current Profile
Weir frames impostor feelings as common but often hidden intellectual self-doubt among high achievers. Her article distinguishes the experience from a formal DSM diagnosis, expands it beyond its original women-only framing, connects it to achievement pressure and outsider status, and organizes practical responses around social support, realistic self-evaluation, reduced perfectionism, and professional help.

## Key Characteristics
- Translates psychological research and clinical commentary for a general audience.
- Uses first-person student accounts to show how doubts about skill can become doubts about belonging.
- Presents self-help practices while preserving a boundary between a named phenomenon and a formal diagnosis.

## Evidence
- Explanatory synthesis: [[feel-like-a-fraud]] combines the construct's history with risk factors, maintenance mechanisms, and possible responses.
- Experiential reporting: [[feel-like-a-fraud]] uses graduate-student accounts across race, gender, sexuality, age, and career stage.
- Clinical boundary: [[feel-like-a-fraud]] states that the phenomenon is not a DSM diagnosis and includes therapy as one option for persistent distress.

## Qualifications
The wiki contains one 2013 magazine article by Weir, not a representative sample of her work. The article summarizes research and practitioner views but is neither a systematic review nor a treatment study, and some terminology and evidence may have developed since publication.
