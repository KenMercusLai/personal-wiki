## Overview
[[JoshuaTaylor]] is represented as the author of a 2016 essay arguing that designers should develop business fluency alongside design craft.

## Current Profile
Taylor’s source-scoped position is that design influence grows when designers understand company success, operating constraints, and executive priorities well enough to apply design to core business problems. He preserves craft, research, analytics, prototyping, and testing as necessary while challenging designers to spend more learning time with sales, operations, finance, planning, and management.

## Key Characteristics
- Advocates business study as a complement to design craft.
- Frames cross-functional inquiry as an accessible alternative to formal business education.
- Connects design influence with the ability to discuss company goals and executive problems.
- Rejects business orientation as a justification for poorly designed experiences.

## Evidence
- Business-first context: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] says designers need to understand how a business succeeds before deciding where craft matters most.
- Practical learning: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] recommends conversations with sales and fulfillment plus study of projections, economics, fundraising, business models, teams, and competition.
- Influence thesis: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] argues that informed contributions to business conversations give design recommendations more weight.
- Craft boundary: [[designers-shouldnt-code-they-should-study-business]] explicitly retains the need for continued craft growth and well-designed experiences.

## Qualifications
This profile is derived from one short essay and its source attribution. It does not establish Taylor’s broader career history, whether the named companies followed his proposed model, or whether business study independently improves designer influence or company outcomes.
