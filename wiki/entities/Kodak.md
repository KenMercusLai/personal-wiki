## Overview
[[Kodak]] is represented as the photography incumbent whose film-market dominance and early digital-camera invention did not prevent bankruptcy after digital photography and smartphones transformed image capture.

## Current Profile
Norton's essay uses Kodak as a compact [[InnovatorsDilemma]] case. It reports dominant U.S. film and camera shares, high film margins, and Steven Sasson's construction of an early digital camera inside Kodak in 1975. Management's fear that digital would harm film revenue is presented as a rational defense of the existing profit engine that became strategically destructive as digital cameras and then smartphones expanded photography at a radically different scale.

The profile preserves that argument as the source's interpretation rather than a complete causal history. The essay does not compare Kodak's later digital investments, patents, competitors, cost structure, execution, financing, or the separate smartphone transition.

## Key Characteristics
- Held a historically dominant position in U.S. film and camera sales according to the source.
- Combined valuable imaging knowledge with unusually profitable dependence on film economics.
- Developed an early digital-camera prototype internally but did not convert that invention into durable leadership.
- Serves as a case where rational product defense, hierarchy, and downside sensitivity can obstruct strategic renewal.

## Evidence
- Market position: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] reports 90% of U.S. film sales, 85% of U.S. camera sales, and film margins above 80% at Kodak's peak.
- Internal invention: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] says Kodak engineer Steven Sasson built an early digital camera in a Kodak lab in 1975.
- Defensive incentive: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] quotes Sasson saying approval was blocked by fear of effects on the film market.
- Outcome: [[ken-norton-10x-not-10-percent]] connects digital cameras and smartphones to revenue decline, Kodak's 2012 bankruptcy filing, and 50,000 lost jobs.

## Qualifications
The evidence comes from one keynote-derived essay and compresses a century-scale company history into a cautionary narrative. The quoted figures and management response are not independently documented within the source, and possessing an early prototype did not by itself guarantee a viable digital business. Later investment, execution, competition, patents, capital structure, and smartphone substitution remain outside the account.
