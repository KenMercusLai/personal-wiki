## Overview
[[ARKit]] appears in the source as Apple's augmented-reality software platform for turning existing iPhone and iPad cameras into early AR surfaces.

## Current Profile
The article presents ARKit less as an end-user product than as a strategic seeding mechanism. By putting AR capabilities on hundreds of millions of iPhones and iPads, Apple can expose developers and users to AR behaviors before a dedicated glasses form factor is technically ready. ARKit 2 is described as making smartphone and tablet cameras into smarter eyes, while the source argues that the long-term fit is stronger for [[SmartGlasses]] than for handheld AR viewfinders.

## Key Characteristics
- Gives Apple an installed-base path for augmented-reality experimentation.
- Lets developers explore AR before smart glasses are ready.
- Uses existing iPhone and iPad cameras as interim "smart eyes."
- Is framed as strategically incomplete on handheld devices because users still have to hold the screen and camera.
- Supports Apple's preparation for future eye-worn computing.

## Evidence
- Installed base: [[above-avalon-apple-is-figuring-out-whats-next]] says ARKit lets Apple use hundreds of millions of iPhones and iPads to seed AR potential.
- Developer surface: [[above-avalon-apple-is-figuring-out-whats-next]] says Apple can inspire roughly 20 million developers with AR possibilities.
- Handheld limitation: [[above-avalon-apple-is-figuring-out-whats-next]] argues iPhone and iPad AR demos are less ideal because users must hold the device up.
- Glasses fit: [[above-avalon-apple-is-figuring-out-whats-next]] connects ARKit to future smart glasses as the form factor where its capabilities make more sense.

## Qualifications
The source is a 2018 strategy interpretation and does not evaluate ARKit adoption, app quality, technical limitations, or later Apple AR hardware outcomes.

## What Changed
