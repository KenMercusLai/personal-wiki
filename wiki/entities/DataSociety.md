## Overview
[[DataSociety]] is presented as a research institute whose work on data, privacy, and inequality informs the article's account of low-income privacy vulnerability.

## Current Profile
The source cites Data & Society as the institutional home for research on class-differentiated data harms. It appears through work on big data and privacy vulnerability, the privacy and security survey discussed by [[MaryMadden]], and the broader framing that marginalized communities experience digital privacy differently from affluent consumers. In this article, Data & Society functions as an empirical bridge between legal examples of welfare surveillance and survey evidence about scams, harassment, financial-data theft, and law-enforcement targeting.

## Key Characteristics
- Provides research context for privacy, security, and digital inequality.
- Supplies survey evidence about low-income Americans' heightened concern over data collection and online harms.
- Connects digital privacy vulnerability with class, race, immigration status, and offline insecurity.
- Supports the article's shift from individual privacy behavior toward structural accountability.

## Evidence
- Research institute context: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] identifies Madden as a Data & Society researcher.
- Survey evidence: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] describes a nationally representative survey commissioned in 2015 with an oversample of adults below $40,000 annual household income.
- Privacy vulnerability framing: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] links to Data & Society work on class differentials in big data and privacy vulnerability.
- Marginalized concerns: [[another-tax-on-the-poor-surrendering-privacy-for-survival]] reports especially high concern among foreign-born Hispanic respondents about financial-information loss and law-enforcement targeting.
