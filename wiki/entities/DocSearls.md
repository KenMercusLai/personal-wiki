## Overview
[[DocSearls]] is represented in the wiki as a writer and critic of tracking-based advertising who distinguishes direct-response adtech from sponsorship-oriented brand advertising.

## Current Profile
In the available source, Searls interprets the 2017 YouTube advertiser boycott as evidence that automated audience chasing and contextual media sponsorship are different businesses. He argues for direct advertiser-publisher relationships, customer opt-in, tracking protection, and user-controlled interest signaling, but the corpus currently contains only this one opinion essay and no later assessment of his predictions.

## Key Characteristics
- Distinguishes [[Adtech]] from [[BrandAdvertising]] by objective, data practice, placement logic, and relationship to media context.
- Treats privacy tools and ad blocking as legitimate user market responses to tracking.
- Advocates deliberate customer opt-in and individual control over interest signals.
- Connects advertising infrastructure to publisher incentives, brand safety, fraud, malware, and low-quality content production.

## Evidence
- Advertising distinction: [[doc-searls-brands-need-to-fire-adtech]] contrasts automated pursuit of targetable people with direct sponsorship of selected media.
- User agency: [[doc-searls-brands-need-to-fire-adtech]] argues for opt-in direct response and open protocols controlled by individuals.
- Market critique: [[doc-searls-brands-need-to-fire-adtech]] frames ad blocking and tracking protection as responses to surveillance costs.
- Publisher and brand effects: [[doc-searls-brands-need-to-fire-adtech]] links third-party ad systems to lost placement control and weaker media-brand association.

## Qualifications
The profile is based on one polemical 2017 article. Its effectiveness claim is unmeasured, its GDPR forecast is not evaluated against later outcomes, and its binary framing does not cover contextual programmatic buying, guaranteed inventory, or hybrid campaign objectives.
